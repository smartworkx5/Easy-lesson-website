The water cycle explained simply is the endless journey of water as it moves between the Earth and the sky. The same water that falls as rain today may once have been part of an ocean, a cloud, a river, or even a glass of drinking water long ago. Nothing is added and nothing is lost. Water simply changes form and place, driven by energy from the sun and pulled by gravity. Once you can name the four main stages and explain how they connect, you understand the cycle.

## What the Water Cycle Is

The water cycle is the continuous movement of water through the environment. Water evaporates from oceans, lakes, and land, rises into the air as invisible water vapour, cools and condenses into clouds, falls back to the ground as precipitation, and collects in rivers, lakes, and underground stores before the journey begins again. The sun provides the energy that lifts water into the sky, and gravity pulls it back down. Together, these two forces keep the cycle turning.

It is called a cycle because it has no true starting point. You can begin your explanation at any stage, as long as you show how each stage leads to the next. In school answers, the most common starting point is evaporation from the surface of the sea, because the oceans hold most of the Earth's water and supply most of the water vapour in the air.

## Evaporation: Water Rises Into the Air

Evaporation is the process by which liquid water changes into water vapour, an invisible gas. Heat from the sun warms the surface of oceans, lakes, and rivers, and some of the water molecules gain enough energy to escape into the air. Evaporation also happens from wet soil and puddles. A related process, transpiration, is the release of water vapour from the leaves of plants. Together, evaporation and transpiration are often called evapotranspiration.

You can see evaporation in everyday life. A puddle shrinks on a warm day, wet clothes dry on a line, and a wet towel dries without being heated directly. In each case, liquid water is slowly becoming water vapour and mixing with the air. On a hot, windy day, evaporation happens faster, because heat gives water molecules more energy and wind carries the vapour away.

## Condensation: Vapour Becomes Clouds

Condensation is the opposite of evaporation. As water vapour rises, the air gets colder, and the vapour cools and changes back into tiny liquid water droplets. These droplets gather around tiny particles in the air, such as dust, and form clouds. A cloud is therefore not a solid object but a visible mass of tiny water droplets or ice crystals floating in the air.

You can see condensation on a cold drink. Drops of water appear on the outside of the glass because water vapour in the warm air cools against the cold surface and turns back into liquid. The same idea explains dew on grass in the morning and fog on a cool day. In the sky, condensation is what builds clouds, and clouds are the holding stage of the water cycle.

## Precipitation: Water Falls Back Down

Precipitation is any form of water that falls from clouds to the ground. Rain is the most familiar form, but precipitation also includes snow, hail, and sleet. It happens when the droplets in a cloud grow heavy enough that the air can no longer hold them up, and gravity pulls them down. Cold temperatures can turn the droplets into snow or ice before they reach the ground.

Where the water lands decides what happens next. Some of it falls directly into oceans, lakes, and rivers. Some falls on land, where it may soak into the soil, run across the surface, or be taken up by plants. In cold regions, snow can pile up and stay frozen for months before melting. Whatever the form, precipitation is the stage that returns water from the sky to the Earth.

## Collection: Water Gathers and the Cycle Repeats

Collection is the stage in which water gathers in rivers, lakes, oceans, and underground stores. Water that lands on the ground may flow downhill as runoff and join a stream or river. Some soaks into the soil and becomes groundwater, which may later feed springs or wells. Water stored in lakes and oceans is ready to evaporate again when the sun warms it, and the cycle continues.

This stage explains why rivers keep flowing. A river is not a single body of water travelling once from a mountain to the sea. It is constantly refilled by rain, melting snow, and groundwater. The water cycle is also why water supplies depend on rainfall. If an area receives little precipitation for a long time, rivers and wells can run low, because the collection stage is not being refilled.

## Check Your Understanding

1. What are the four main stages of the water cycle? Evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection.
2. What is the difference between evaporation and condensation? Evaporation turns liquid water into invisible water vapour; condensation turns water vapour back into liquid droplets.
3. What is precipitation? Any water that falls from clouds to the ground, including rain, snow, hail, and sleet.
4. What two forces drive the water cycle? Energy from the sun lifts water into the air, and gravity pulls it back down.

## Conclusion

The water cycle is the endless loop in which water evaporates from the Earth's surface, condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation, and collects in rivers, lakes, and oceans before evaporating again. The sun supplies the energy and gravity supplies the pull. If you can describe each stage and explain how it leads to the next, you can answer almost any basic question on this topic. ## FAQ

### What is the water cycle in simple words?

The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between the Earth and the sky. Water evaporates, condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation, and collects in rivers and oceans before evaporating again.

### What causes evaporation in the water cycle?

Heat from the sun causes evaporation. It gives water molecules enough energy to escape from the surface of oceans, lakes, and land into the air as invisible water vapour.

### What is the difference between condensation and precipitation?

Condensation is water vapour cooling and turning into tiny liquid droplets that form clouds. Precipitation is water falling from clouds to the ground as rain, snow, hail, or sleet.

### Why is the water cycle important?

The water cycle distributes fresh water around the planet, refills rivers, lakes, and groundwater, and drives weather patterns. Without it, water would stay locked in one place and most land areas would dry out.
