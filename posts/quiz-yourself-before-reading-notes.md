Quiz yourself before reading your notes. It sounds backwards. Why would you test yourself on material you have not reviewed yet? But this strange habit, sometimes called pretesting, is one of the most effective study techniques there is. Attempting questions before you study primes your brain to notice the answers when you do read, and it turns passive rereading into active hunting.

Most students read their notes first and test themselves later, if they test at all. That order feels logical, but it wastes an opportunity. When you read without questions in mind, your brain treats every sentence as equally important, and most of it slides past. When you attempt questions first, your brain goes into the reading with a purpose: find the answers to the questions you could not answer. The same notes suddenly teach you far more.

## What Pretesting Actually Is

Pretesting means attempting questions about a topic before you study it. You do not need to know the answers. In fact, getting them wrong is part of the point. The attempt itself prepares your mind to learn.

A pretest can be very short. Before you open your notes on a topic, write down three to five questions about it from memory, or use the questions at the end of the chapter. Attempt them with the book closed. Write your best guesses, even if you are unsure. Then open your notes and read to find the answers.

This is different from a normal self-test. A normal self-test checks what you already studied. A pretest prepares you to study. Its purpose is not to measure your knowledge. Its purpose is to wake up your curiosity and focus your attention on the gaps.

Pretesting works even when you score badly. A pretest where you answer nothing correctly still helps, because the failed attempts create questions in your mind. When you then read the answers, your brain recognises them as the missing pieces it was looking for, and the learning sticks far better than if you had read the same sentences cold.

## Why Guessing First Helps You Learn

Guessing first helps for three reasons. The first is attention. When you have tried to answer a question and failed, the correct answer becomes interesting. Your brain pays attention to it when it appears in your notes, instead of letting it drift past with everything else. The failed guess turns the answer into something worth noticing.

The second reason is organisation. A pretest gives you a mental map of the topic before you read. You know what the questions are, so as you read, you can sort new information into the slots the questions created. Reading without questions is like putting groceries away in a kitchen with no shelves. Reading after a pretest is like putting them into labelled cupboards.

The third reason is error correction. When you guess wrong and then discover the right answer, the contrast between your guess and the truth is memorable. You remember not only the correct answer, but also the mistake you made, which makes you less likely to repeat it. This is why a wrong guess followed by feedback teaches more than simply reading the correct answer in the first place.

## How to Do a Five-Minute Pre-Quiz

A pre-quiz does not need to be long or formal. Here is a five-minute version you can use before any study session.

First, pick your topic and set a timer for five minutes. Write down three to five questions about the topic. You can take them from the chapter's end-of-chapter questions, from headings turned into questions, or from your own curiosity: "What is this about? Why does it happen? How does it work?"

Second, answer each question from memory, in writing. Do not open your notes. Write your best guess, even if it is only a fragment. If you truly have no idea, write "I don't know yet" and move on. The point is the attempt, not the score.

Third, open your notes and read with your questions in front of you. As you find each answer, tick off the question and note the answer in a sentence. Pay special attention to the questions you got wrong: those are the ones your brain is most ready to learn.

Fourth, close your notes and answer the questions again from memory. This second attempt is your real self-test. If you can answer them now, the pretest-plus-reading cycle has worked. ## What to Do with Wrong Guesses

Wrong guesses are the fuel of pretesting, so treat them well. When you discover that a guess was wrong, do not just sigh and move on. Write down the correct answer next to your guess, in your own words. The side-by-side comparison of "what I thought" and "what is true" is one of the most memorable study experiences you can create.

Look for patterns in your wrong guesses. Do you keep guessing the same wrong term? Do you confuse two similar ideas? Do you guess answers that are too vague? Each pattern points to a specific weakness, and naming it is the first step to fixing it.

Keep a small "guess log" for a week: the question, your guess, and the correct answer. At the end of the week, retest yourself on the questions you guessed wrong. If you can answer them correctly now, the pretest cycle is working. If you miss the same ones again, those ideas need a slower, more careful study session with smaller chunks.

Never punish yourself for wrong guesses. A pretest is not a test of your worth. It is a tool for focusing your attention. The students who benefit most from pretesting are not the ones who guess well; they are the ones who guess boldly, check honestly, and learn from every miss.

## Pretesting Before Class vs Before Revision

Pretesting helps in two different situations: before a class and before a revision session. The technique is the same, but the questions are different.

Before a class, pretest with curiosity questions. Look at the topic heading and ask: "What do I already know about this? What do I expect to learn? What confuses me already?" Write your guesses down. When the class covers the topic, you will listen with purpose, because you already have questions waiting for answers. Students who pretest before class often find that lessons feel easier to follow, because they are confirming and correcting ideas instead of meeting everything for the first time.

Before a revision session, pretest with chapter questions or old quiz questions. Attempt them cold, then revise only the topics you missed. This keeps revision focused on real gaps instead of rereading everything equally. It also gives you a before-and-after picture: the pretest shows where you started, and the post-test shows how far the session took you.

In both cases, keep the pretest short. Five minutes is enough. A pretest that drags on becomes a test, and the point is to prepare for learning, not to replace it.

## Common Worries About Pretesting

Many students worry that pretesting will damage their confidence. "If I fail before I even start, I will feel discouraged." In practice, the opposite usually happens. A pretest reframes failure as information. You expect to miss questions, so missing them carries no shame. And when you then read and answer them correctly, the improvement feels like a win you earned.

Another worry is that guessing might plant wrong answers in your memory. This is a reasonable concern, but feedback solves it. As long as you check your guesses against a reliable source soon after, within the same session, the correct answer replaces the guess. The danger only appears if you guess and never check. Always check, always in the same session.

A third worry is time. "I barely have time to study once. How can I add a pretest?" The answer is that pretesting saves time. Five minutes of pretesting makes the following twenty minutes of reading far more effective, because you read with focus. You also waste less time rereading material you already know, because the pretest tells you what to skip.

## Conclusion

Quiz yourself before reading your notes, and your reading will work harder for you. Five minutes of guessing first gives your brain questions to answer, focuses your attention, and makes correct answers memorable. Expect to miss questions. Check every guess in the same session, learn from the misses, and retest at the end. Try it once today, and you will feel the difference in your very next study session.

## FAQ

### Do I need to know anything before I pretest?

No. Pretesting works even when you know nothing about the topic. The attempt itself creates the questions your brain will try to answer while reading. A pretest with zero correct answers still prepares you to learn.

### How is pretesting different from a normal self-test?

A normal self-test measures what you learned after studying. A pretest prepares you to study, by giving you questions before you read. Both are useful. Pretest before studying, then self-test again after studying to check what you learned.

### Won't guessing plant wrong answers in my memory?

Not if you check your guesses promptly. Always check against a reliable source in the same session. The feedback corrects the guess, and the contrast between your guess and the correct answer actually strengthens the memory of the right answer.

### How many questions should a pretest have?

Three to five questions is enough. A pretest should take about five minutes. Its job is to focus your attention, not to test everything. Keep it short, and spend most of your session on reading and retesting.

### Can I pretest before a class I have not prepared for?

Yes. Look at the lesson topic, write down what you already know and what you expect to learn, and guess at two or three questions. You will walk into class with questions waiting for answers, which makes the lesson easier to follow.
