States of matter explained simply come down to one idea: the same material can behave very differently depending on how its particles are arranged and how much energy they have. A solid keeps its shape, a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container, and a gas spreads out to fill whatever space is available. Ice, liquid water, and water vapour are all water, yet they behave in completely different ways. The particle model explains why.

## The Three Common States of Matter

The three states you meet first in science are solids, liquids, and gases. A solid has a fixed shape and a fixed volume. Think of a brick, a coin, or an ice cube. A liquid has a fixed volume but no fixed shape. It flows and takes the shape of the bottom of its container. Think of water in a jug or oil in a pan. A gas has neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume. It spreads out to fill the whole container. Think of the air in a room or steam rising from a kettle.

There are other states of matter, such as plasma, but solids, liquids, and gases are the ones you need first. Most of the materials around you can exist in all three states if the temperature changes enough. Water is the clearest example, because you see it as ice, liquid water, and steam in everyday life.

## The Particle Model: What Is Really Happening

The particle model says that all matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving. The state of a material depends on how tightly those particles are held together and how much energy they have. In a solid, the particles are packed closely together and held in fixed positions. They can vibrate slightly, but they cannot move past each other. That is why a solid keeps its shape.

In a liquid, the particles are still close together, but they are not locked in place. They can slide past each other, which is why a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. In a gas, the particles are far apart and move freely and quickly in all directions. They spread out, bump into each other and into the walls of the container, and fill whatever space is available. Heating a material gives its particles more energy, so they move faster and the forces holding them together weaken.

## Changing State: Melting, Freezing, and Boiling

When a solid is heated, its particles gain energy and vibrate more strongly until they can break free from their fixed positions. The solid becomes a liquid. This change is called melting. The reverse change, when a liquid cools and its particles lose energy and lock into fixed positions, is called freezing. Melting and freezing happen at the same temperature for a given material, called its melting point.

When a liquid is heated, its particles gain even more energy until they can escape into the air as a gas. This change is called boiling or vaporisation. The reverse change, when a gas cools and its particles lose energy and gather into a liquid, is called condensation. You see condensation when water droplets form on a cold drink. These changes of state are physical changes, not chemical ones, because the material itself stays the same. Ice, water, and steam are all still water.

## Evaporation, Sublimation, and Other Changes

Evaporation is like boiling, but it happens only at the surface of a liquid and at any temperature, not just at the boiling point. A puddle dries because the fastest-moving particles at the surface escape into the air. Evaporation is slower than boiling, but it follows the same idea: particles with enough energy leave the liquid and become gas.

Sublimation is a rarer change in which a solid turns directly into a gas without becoming a liquid first. Dry ice, which is solid carbon dioxide, does this, producing a misty gas. Deposition is the reverse: a gas turns directly into a solid, which is how frost can form on a cold surface. You do not need to memorise many examples, but knowing that these direct changes exist will help you answer questions that go beyond melting and boiling.

## Why the Particle Model Is So Useful

The particle model is useful because one simple idea explains many observations. It explains why solids keep their shape, why liquids flow, why gases fill a container, and why materials expand when heated. It also explains why a gas can be compressed, or squeezed into a smaller space, while a solid or liquid cannot. In a gas, there is a lot of empty space between particles, so there is room to push them closer together. In a solid or liquid, the particles are already close, so there is little room to compress them.

When you answer questions on this topic, always connect the observation back to the particles. Instead of only saying that a gas fills its container, say that its particles move freely and spread out in all directions. Instead of only saying that a solid keeps its shape, say that its particles are locked in fixed positions. That habit of linking behaviour to particles is what earns full marks.

## Check Your Understanding

1. How do particles behave in a solid? They are packed closely together in fixed positions and can only vibrate slightly.
2. What is the difference between boiling and evaporation? Boiling happens throughout a liquid at its boiling point; evaporation happens only at the surface and at any temperature.
3. What is condensation? The change from gas to liquid when a gas cools and its particles lose energy.
4. Why can a gas be compressed but a solid cannot? Gas particles have large spaces between them, so they can be pushed closer; solid particles are already packed tightly.

## Conclusion

Solids, liquids, and gases differ because of how their particles are arranged and how much energy they have. Solids hold their shape, liquids flow, and gases spread out. Heating gives particles energy and drives changes of state, while cooling takes energy away and reverses them. If you can describe the particle arrangement in each state and explain melting, freezing, boiling, and condensation, you understand the core of this topic. ## FAQ

### What are the three states of matter?

The three common states of matter are solids, liquids, and gases. A solid has a fixed shape and volume, a liquid has a fixed volume but flows, and a gas spreads out to fill its container.

### What is the particle model?

The particle model says that all matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving. The state of a material depends on how closely its particles are packed and how much energy they have.

### What is the difference between melting and freezing?

Melting is a solid changing into a liquid when heated. Freezing is a liquid changing into a solid when cooled. Both happen at the same temperature for a given material.

### Is a change of state a physical or chemical change?

A change of state is a physical change. The material stays the same substance, only its form changes. Ice, water, and steam are all still water.
