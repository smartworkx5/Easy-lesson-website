Every class is a mixed-ability class. In any group of students, some will grasp a new idea quickly, some will need more time, and some will need the idea explained in a different way. Teaching to the middle leaves the fastest students bored and the slowest students lost. Differentiation is the craft of teaching the same lesson to all of them at once — same destination, different routes.

Differentiation is often misunderstood as writing three separate lessons. That is neither practical nor necessary. This guide shows a simpler approach: one lesson, three entry points, varied support, and checks that show whether everyone is learning. It is designed for real classrooms, where a teacher has one lesson, one class, and limited planning time.

## What Differentiation Really Means

Differentiation does not mean different goals for different students. It means different levels of support on the way to the same goal. Every student in the class is learning the same core idea. What differs is how much scaffolding they get, how they practise, and how they show what they know. The destination stays the same. The route adapts.

This distinction matters, because it keeps expectations high for everyone. A supported task is not an easier topic. It is the same topic with more steps shown, more hints, and more worked examples. A stretch task is not a different topic. It is the same topic with the supports removed and a harder question added. When the goal is shared, every student can join the same class discussion, because everyone has been thinking about the same idea.

Differentiation also does not mean labelling students permanently. A student who needs support on one topic may need stretch on another. Support and stretch should change with the topic, the day, and the task. Flexible grouping — students choosing or being assigned a level for one lesson — respects that reality far better than fixed groups that follow students all year.

## Start With One Lesson, Three Entry Points

The simplest way to differentiate is to design one lesson with three entry points: a supported version, a core version, and a stretch version of the same activity. All three versions teach the same idea. The supported version includes worked steps, hints, and simpler numbers or shorter texts. The core version is the standard task. The stretch version removes the supports and adds a question that asks students to explain, apply, or extend the idea.

Students can choose their entry point, or you can assign it based on what you know. Choice works well when students know themselves honestly, and it builds independence. Assignment works well when you have a clear picture of who needs what. Either way, keep movement between levels easy. A student who finishes the supported version should be able to try the core version. A student who finds the core version easy should be able to move to stretch. Levels are starting points, not cages.

Planning three versions sounds like triple the work, but it is not. You write one good activity, then you add supports to make the supported version and remove supports to make the stretch version. The core work is done once. For ideas on drafting versions quickly, see [How Teachers Can Use AI Tools to Save Planning Time](/blog/how-teachers-can-use-ai-tools.html).

## Differentiate the Support, Not Just the Task

The most powerful form of differentiation is not a different worksheet. It is different support for the same worksheet. Every student can attempt the same task if the supports around it vary: a worked example for one group, a hint card for another, and no support for a third. The task stays shared, which means the class discussion stays shared too.

Useful supports include worked examples that show the first step, word banks for key vocabulary, sentence starters for written answers, and checklists that remind students of the steps. These supports are scaffolds: they hold students up while they learn, and they come down as students grow confident. A student who used a worked example last week may not need it this week, and that is progress.

Support can also come from people. Pairing a student who is confident with a student who is stuck, for one task, helps both. The confident student deepens their understanding by explaining. The stuck student gets help in peer language. Keep these pairings short and purposeful, and rotate them, so no student is always the helper or always the helped.

## Use Choice Without Chaos

Choice is a gentle form of differentiation. When students choose how to practise — a set of problems, a short writing task, or a drawing that shows the idea — they pick the format that fits them, and they take more ownership of the work. Choice works when it is bounded: two or three clear options, all teaching the same idea, all taking about the same time.

Unbounded choice creates chaos. Ten options, unclear expectations, and different finishing times leave the teacher managing activities instead of teaching. Keep choices few, make the instructions identical in structure, and set the same finishing time for all options. Students choose the route, but the destination and the deadline stay the same.

Choice also works for showing understanding. Some students explain best in writing, others by talking, others by drawing a labelled diagram. Letting students show what they know in more than one format gives you a truer picture of their learning, and it gives students who struggle with one format a fair chance to show what they know.

## Group Students With a Purpose

Grouping is a differentiation tool, but only when the groups have a purpose. Random groups are fine for variety. Ability groups are useful when the task genuinely needs different support levels. Mixed groups are useful when students can learn from each other. The right grouping depends on the task, and it should change from lesson to lesson.

Keep group tasks structured. A group activity where one student does the work and the rest watch is not differentiation; it is an audience. Give each student a role or a part: one student explains the first step, another checks it, another writes the answer. Roles make participation visible, and they let you see who understood and who did not.

Short, focused group work beats long, loose group work. Ten minutes with a clear task and a clear product — one solved problem, one labelled diagram, one paragraph — produces more learning than thirty minutes of unstructured discussion. Set the time, name the product, and circulate while groups work.

## Check That Everyone Is Learning

Differentiation without checking is guessing. You need to know whether the supported group, the core group, and the stretch group all reached the goal. The fastest way is a shared exit check: one question, same for everyone, answered by every student at the end of the lesson. The exit check tells you whether the routes all arrived at the same destination.

If the exit check shows a gap, your next lesson has a clear job. The students who did not reach the goal get a reteaching step. The students who did get a stretch step. Without the check, you would discover the gap weeks later, when it is much harder to fix. For questioning methods that check understanding during the lesson, see [Classroom Questioning Techniques That Get Everyone Thinking](/blog/classroom-questioning-techniques.html).

Over time, exit checks also show you which supports work. If the supported group keeps reaching the goal, the supports are well pitched. If they keep missing it, the supports need adjusting — perhaps more worked steps, or a smaller first task. Differentiation improves with this kind of evidence, not with guesswork.

## Conclusion

Differentiating for a mixed-ability class does not require three lessons. It requires one lesson with three entry points, varied support around a shared task, bounded choice, purposeful grouping, and a shared check at the end. When every student is heading to the same goal with the right amount of support, the fast learners stay challenged, the slower learners stay included, and the whole class learns together.

## FAQ

### Does differentiation mean writing three separate lessons?

No. It means one lesson with different levels of support on the way to the same goal. Write one good activity, add supports for a supported version, and remove supports and add a harder question for a stretch version.

### Should students choose their own level?

Choice works well when students know themselves honestly, and it builds independence. Assignment works well when you have a clear picture of who needs what. Either way, keep movement between levels easy so no student is trapped in one level.

### How do I differentiate without labelling students?

Keep groups flexible and task-based. Support and stretch should change with the topic, not follow students all year. A student who needs support on one topic may need stretch on another, and flexible grouping respects that.

### How do I know if differentiation is working?

Use a shared exit check: one question, same for everyone, answered by every student. If all groups reach the goal, the differentiation is working. If one group keeps missing it, adjust the supports for that group.
