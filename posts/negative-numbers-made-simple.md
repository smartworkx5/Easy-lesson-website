Negative numbers explained with a number line become far less confusing. Negative numbers often seem to break the rules of ordinary arithmetic: how can you subtract more than you have, and why do two negatives make a positive? The answer is that negative numbers follow the same rules as positive ones, once you picture them correctly. This guide uses a number line — the single most useful model for negatives — and builds every rule from it, with worked examples for all four operations.

## Negative Numbers Explained: The Number Line

Draw a horizontal line. Mark 0 in the middle. Positive numbers go to the right: 1, 2, 3, and so on. Negative numbers go to the left: −1, −2, −3, and so on. The minus sign tells you which side of zero you are on, and the number tells you how far from zero you are. The number −4 is four steps to the left of zero.

Two ideas fall out of this picture immediately. First, numbers get bigger as you move right and smaller as you move left. So −2 is bigger than −5, because −2 sits to the right of −5. That surprises beginners, but the number line makes it obvious: −5 is further left, so it is smaller.

Second, the distance from zero has a name: the absolute value. The absolute value of −4 is 4, and the absolute value of 4 is also 4. Both are four steps from zero. Absolute value ignores the sign and keeps only the distance.

## Adding Negative Numbers: Walk the Line

Addition on a number line means walking. Adding a positive number means walking right; adding a negative number means walking left. Start at your first number, then walk in the direction of the second number's sign, for as many steps as its size.

Example: −3 + 5. Start at −3. Adding +5 means walking 5 steps right: −3 → −2 → −1 → 0 → 1 → 2. You land on 2. So −3 + 5 = 2. The walk shows why the answer is positive even though you started negative: the +5 walk was long enough to cross zero.

Example: −4 + (−3). Start at −4. Adding −3 means walking 3 steps left: −4 → −5 → −6 → −7. So −4 + (−3) = −7. Adding a negative always moves you left, further from zero on the negative side.

A shortcut for adding two negatives: add their sizes and keep the negative sign. For −4 + (−3), 4 + 3 = 7, so the answer is −7. The shortcut matches the walk exactly.

## Subtracting Negative Numbers: The Double-Negative Flip

Subtraction is the opposite of addition, so subtracting a positive number means walking left, and subtracting a negative number means walking right. The rule "subtracting a negative is the same as adding a positive" is just this picture in words.

Example: 5 − (−2). Start at 5. Subtracting −2 means walking 2 steps right (the opposite of the leftward walk that adding −2 would give). You land on 7. So 5 − (−2) = 7. The double negative flips to addition: 5 + 2 = 7.

Example: −6 − 3. Start at −6. Subtracting +3 means walking 3 steps left: −6 → −7 → −8 → −9. So −6 − 3 = −9. This matches the everyday idea of a debt growing: if you owe 6 and spend 3 more on credit, you owe 9.

The most common error is treating 5 − (−2) as 5 − 2 = 3. The two minus signs side by side always combine into a plus. When you see "− (−", replace it with "+ (" and carry on.

## Worked Example: Multiplying and Dividing Negatives

Multiplication of negatives follows a sign rule that the number line can also justify: same signs give a positive answer, different signs give a negative answer. Examples: (−2) × (−3) = 6 (same signs, positive), (−4) × 3 = −12 (different signs, negative), 4 × (−3) = −12 (different signs, negative), and 2 × 3 = 6 (same signs, positive).

Why do two negatives make a positive? One way to see it: multiplying by −1 flips the number line, turning right into left and positive into negative. Multiplying by −1 twice flips it back to where it started. So (−2) × (−3) is the same as flipping twice, which returns you to the positive side: 6.

Division follows the identical sign rule. Examples: (−6) ÷ 2 = −3 (different signs, negative), (−15) ÷ (−3) = 5 (same signs, positive), and 12 ÷ (−4) = −3 (different signs, negative). Work out the size with ordinary division, then set the sign with the rule.

## Worked Example: A Mixed Calculation

Compute −8 + 3 − (−4). Work left to right, one step at a time. First, −8 + 3: start at −8 and walk 3 right to land on −5. Next, −5 − (−4): subtracting a negative flips to addition, so −5 + 4 = −1. The final answer is −1.

Check with a second route: rewrite the subtraction as addition first: −8 + 3 + 4. Now add in a friendlier order: 3 + 4 = 7, and −8 + 7 = −1. Same answer. Reordering is allowed for addition, and it often makes the arithmetic easier.

A longer example: (−3) × 2 + 10 ÷ (−5). Multiplication and division come before addition. (−3) × 2 = −6, and 10 ÷ (−5) = −2. Then −6 + (−2) = −8. The answer is −8. Handling the signs first, then the sizes, keeps each step small and checkable.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid with Negative Numbers

The most common mistake is assuming −5 is bigger than −2 because 5 is bigger than 2. On the number line, −5 is further left, so it is smaller. When comparing negatives, the one closer to zero is bigger. Always picture the line.

Another mistake is the double-negative slip: writing 5 − (−2) = 3. Two minus signs together become a plus. Whenever you see a minus followed immediately by a bracketed negative, flip it to addition before doing anything else.

A third mistake is the sign rule for multiplication: (−2) × (−3) is +6, not −6. Same signs give positive; different signs give negative. If you are unsure, count the negative signs: an even number of negatives in a multiplication gives a positive answer, and an odd number gives a negative one.

## FAQ

### What is a negative number?

A negative number is a number less than zero, written with a minus sign, like −4. On a number line, negative numbers sit to the left of zero. They describe things like temperatures below zero, depths below sea level, or amounts owed.

### Is −2 bigger than −5?

Yes. On the number line, −2 sits to the right of −5, and numbers get bigger as you move right. The negative number closer to zero is always the bigger one, even though its size (absolute value) is smaller.

### Why does subtracting a negative give a plus?

Subtraction is the opposite of addition. Adding a negative moves you left on the number line, so subtracting a negative moves you right — the same as adding a positive. That is why 5 − (−2) = 5 + 2 = 7.

### Why do two negatives make a positive when multiplied?

Multiplying by −1 flips the number line. Multiplying by −1 twice flips it back to its original orientation, returning the result to the positive side. That is why (−2) × (−3) = 6. The general rule is: same signs give a positive answer, different signs give a negative one.

### What is the absolute value of a number?

The absolute value is the number's distance from zero, ignoring its sign. The absolute value of −4 is 4, and the absolute value of 4 is also 4. It tells you how far from zero a number sits, not which side it is on.
