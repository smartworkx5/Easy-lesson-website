This angles explained geometry guide starts with a simple idea: an angle is a measure of a turn between two lines that meet at a point. Angles are measured in degrees, and a full turn is 360 degrees. This guide introduces the four main angle types, shows how to measure an angle with a protractor without the classic reading error, and explains the angle-pair rules — complementary, supplementary, vertical, and triangle angles — that solve most school geometry problems.

## Angles Explained Geometry: What an Angle Is

Two lines meeting at a point form an angle. The meeting point is called the vertex, and the two lines are called the arms. The angle measures how far one arm has turned away from the other. A tiny turn gives a small angle; a wide turn gives a large angle. If one arm turns all the way around back to where it started, that is a full turn of 360 degrees.

The degree symbol ° marks the unit. A right angle is 90°, a straight line is 180°, and a full turn is 360°. These three anchors are worth memorising, because nearly every angle problem is built from them. When you see 90°, think "corner of a square". When you see 180°, think "straight line". When you see 360°, think "all the way around".

Angles are named in several ways. A single angle at a vertex can be called by its vertex letter, like angle A. An angle between three points is named with the vertex in the middle, like angle ABC, where B is the vertex. Either way, the name always points you to the vertex.

## The Four Main Types of Angle

An acute angle is smaller than 90° — a sharp, narrow turn. A right angle is exactly 90° — the corner of a square, usually marked with a small square in diagrams. An obtuse angle is between 90° and 180° — a wide, open turn. A straight angle is exactly 180° — the two arms form a straight line.

There is also a reflex angle, which is between 180° and 360° — the "outside" of the turn. If a problem shows a small angle of 60° at a vertex, the reflex angle at the same vertex is 360° − 60° = 300°. Most school problems focus on the first four types, but knowing that reflex angles exist prevents confusion when a diagram shows the larger turn.

Classifying an angle before measuring it is a smart habit. If an angle looks acute but your protractor reading says 140°, you have read the wrong scale — an acute angle can never be 140°. Estimating first catches that classic error.

## Worked Example: Measuring with a Protractor

Place the protractor's centre exactly on the vertex, with its baseline (the 0° line) along one arm of the angle. Then read where the other arm crosses the protractor's scale. The tricky part is that most protractors have two scales — one running 0° to 180° left to right, and one running 0° to 180° right to left. You must use the scale that starts at 0° on the arm you aligned with the baseline.

Example: an angle's first arm lies along the baseline pointing right, and the second arm crosses the scale at 50°. Since the baseline arm is at 0° on the inner scale, read the inner scale: the angle is 50°. Because 50° is less than 90°, the angle is acute, which matches the diagram — a good sense-check.

If the first arm points left along the baseline, use the other scale, the one that reads 0° on the left. A common beginner error is reading the wrong scale and reporting 130° instead of 50°. The two readings always add to 180°, so if your answer looks wrong for the diagram, try 180° minus your reading.

## Complementary and Supplementary Angles

Two angles are complementary if they add to 90°. If one angle is 35°, its complement is 90° − 35° = 55°. Complementary angles often appear in right triangles and in corners, where two angles together make a right angle.

Two angles are supplementary if they add to 180°. If one angle is 110°, its supplement is 180° − 110° = 70°. Supplementary angles appear wherever a straight line is split: the two angles on a straight line always add to 180°. This is one of the most used facts in geometry.

Worked example: a straight line is split into two angles, one of 65°. The other is 180° − 65° = 115°. Check: 65° + 115° = 180°. Correct. Problems like this are usually one subtraction — the hard part is recognising that the angles sit on a straight line.

A memory trick: "C" for complementary and "C" for corner (90°), and "S" for supplementary and "S" for straight (180°). The letter match reminds you which total each pair adds to.

## Vertical Angles and Angles in a Triangle

When two straight lines cross, they form two pairs of vertical angles — angles opposite each other. Vertical angles are always equal. If one angle at a crossing is 120°, the angle opposite it is also 120°, and the two adjacent angles are each 180° − 120° = 60°. All four angles around the crossing add to 360°.

The angles inside any triangle always add to 180°. This is a powerful problem-solving tool. If a triangle has angles 50° and 70°, the third angle is 180° − 50° − 70° = 60°. Check: 50 + 70 + 60 = 180. Correct. No matter how the triangle is shaped, the three interior angles always total 180°.

Worked example combining both ideas: two lines cross, and one angle is 45°. The vertical angle is also 45°. The adjacent angles are 180° − 45° = 135° each. If a triangle is then drawn using the 45° angle and another angle of 80°, the triangle's third angle is 180° − 45° − 80° = 55°. Each rule is simple; the skill is spotting which rule applies at each step.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid with Angles

The most common mistake is reading the wrong protractor scale and reporting the supplement of the true angle (130° instead of 50°). Always estimate the angle type first: acute, right, or obtuse. Your reading must match your estimate.

Another mistake is mixing up complementary (90°) and supplementary (180°). Use the letter trick: C for corner (90°), S for straight (180°). A question about a corner wants 90°; a question about a straight line wants 180°.

A third mistake is forgetting that triangle angles add to 180°, not 360°. The 360° total applies to angles around a point, not inside a triangle. Angles around a point add to 360°; angles on a straight line add to 180°; angles in a triangle add to 180°. Keep the three totals straight and most angle problems reduce to one subtraction.

## Try It Yourself: Practice with Answers

Try these three problems, then check your answers below.

1. What is the complement of a 25° angle?
2. What is the supplement of a 140° angle?
3. A triangle has angles of 80° and 45°. What is the third angle?

Answers: (1) 90° − 25° = 65°. (2) 180° − 140° = 40°. (3) 180° − 80° − 45° = 55°. If all three are right, you have the core angle rules. If the triangle one felt tricky, write "180° −" first, then subtract each known angle in turn.

## Conclusion

An angle measures a turn in degrees: acute is under 90°, right is exactly 90°, obtuse is between 90° and 180°, and straight is 180°. Measure with a protractor by aligning the baseline and reading the correct scale, then estimate to check. Complementary angles add to 90°, supplementary angles add to 180°, vertical angles are equal, and triangle angles add to 180°. ## FAQ

### What is an angle?

An angle is a measure of the turn between two lines that meet at a point called the vertex. It is measured in degrees. A full turn is 360°, a straight line is 180°, and a right angle is 90°.

### What are the types of angles?

Acute angles are smaller than 90°, right angles are exactly 90°, obtuse angles are between 90° and 180°, straight angles are exactly 180°, and reflex angles are between 180° and 360°. Estimating the type before measuring helps you catch protractor reading errors.

### How do you measure an angle with a protractor?

Place the protractor's centre on the vertex and its baseline along one arm. Read where the other arm crosses the scale, using the scale that starts at 0° on the aligned arm. Then check that your reading matches the angle type you estimated.

### What is the difference between complementary and supplementary angles?

Complementary angles add to 90°, while supplementary angles add to 180°. A memory trick: C for complementary and C for corner (90°); S for supplementary and S for straight (180°).

### Why do the angles in a triangle add to 180°?

The three interior angles of any triangle always total 180°, which is a fundamental fact of flat geometry. It lets you find a missing angle by subtracting the two known angles from 180°. This works for every triangle shape: acute, right, or obtuse.
