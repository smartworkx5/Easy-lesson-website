Area and perimeter are two of the most confused ideas in maths, and the confusion is understandable: both describe a shape, and both use its side lengths. But they measure completely different things. Perimeter measures the distance around the outside; area measures the space inside. This guide uses the fence-versus-carpet analogy to keep them apart, then works through rectangles, squares, and triangles with fully checked examples.

## Area and Perimeter: Fence Versus Carpet

Imagine a garden. The perimeter is the fence around it — the length you would walk if you strolled all the way around the edge. The area is the carpet of grass inside it — the amount of ground the garden covers. Fence and carpet are different jobs, measured in different units, and no fence can ever be mistaken for a carpet once you picture it.

Units tell the same story. Perimeter is a length, measured in ordinary units: centimetres, metres, or kilometres. Area is a surface, measured in square units: square centimetres, square metres, or square kilometres. If your answer for an area comes out in plain centimetres, something went wrong — the units are your built-in error detector.

The two values can also move independently. A long, thin garden can have a huge perimeter and a tiny area, while a compact square garden can have a small perimeter and a large area. Never assume that a bigger perimeter means a bigger area.

## Perimeter of Rectangles and Squares

The perimeter of any shape is the sum of all its side lengths — just add up every side. For a rectangle with length 8 cm and width 5 cm, the sides are 8, 5, 8, and 5 (opposite sides are equal). Add them: 8 + 5 + 8 + 5 = 26 cm. The shortcut formula is P = 2 × (length + width) = 2 × (8 + 5) = 2 × 13 = 26 cm.

For a square, all four sides are equal, so the perimeter is 4 times one side. A square with side 6 cm has perimeter 4 × 6 = 24 cm. If you know a square's perimeter and need its side, divide by 4: a perimeter of 36 cm means a side of 9 cm.

A common slip is to add only two sides of a rectangle and call that the perimeter. A rectangle has four sides, and opposite sides match, so always count all four or use the doubling formula. Writing the four side lengths around the shape before adding is a habit that prevents this error.

## Area of Rectangles and Squares

The area of a rectangle is length times width. For the same 8 cm by 5 cm rectangle, the area is 8 × 5 = 40 square centimetres. Picture it as a grid: 8 columns and 5 rows of 1 cm squares gives 40 little squares in total. The multiplication is simply a fast way to count the grid.

For a square, the area is side times side. A square with side 6 cm has area 6 × 6 = 36 square centimetres. This is why the area of a square is written as side squared — the formula and the picture agree.

A helpful check: area answers should always be in square units. If you write 40 cm for the area of the rectangle above, the missing word "square" is a warning. Get into the habit of writing cm² or "square centimetres" every time, and your units will catch mistakes before they reach the answer line.

## Worked Example: A Triangle's Area

Triangles need a different area formula: area = 1/2 × base × height. The base is any side you choose; the height is the perpendicular distance from that base to the opposite corner. "Perpendicular" matters: the height must meet the base at a right angle, not slant along the triangle's edge.

Take a triangle with base 10 cm and height 4 cm. The area is 1/2 × 10 × 4. Half of 10 is 5, and 5 × 4 = 20, so the area is 20 square centimetres. Another route: 10 × 4 = 40, and half of 40 is 20. Both routes agree.

Why the half? Two identical triangles fit together to make a rectangle (or parallelogram) with area base × height, so one triangle is exactly half of that. If you ever forget the 1/2, picture the triangle doubled into a rectangle and the reason comes back.

The perimeter of a triangle is simpler: add all three sides. A triangle with sides 7 cm, 8 cm, and 9 cm has perimeter 7 + 8 + 9 = 24 cm. No formula needed — just add.

## Worked Example: Comparing Two Shapes

A rectangle measures 7 cm by 3 cm, and a square has side 5 cm. Which has the bigger perimeter, and which has the bigger area? Rectangle perimeter: 2 × (7 + 3) = 2 × 10 = 20 cm. Square perimeter: 4 × 5 = 20 cm. The perimeters are equal. Rectangle area: 7 × 3 = 21 square centimetres. Square area: 5 × 5 = 25 square centimetres. The square has the bigger area despite the equal perimeter.

This example shows why the two measures must never be confused: equal perimeters can hide different areas. It also shows a deep pattern — for a fixed perimeter, the shape closest to a square (or circle) encloses the most area. That pattern matters in real design, from fencing a field to packaging a product.

Another comparison: a triangle with base 12 cm and height 5 cm has area 1/2 × 12 × 5 = 30 square centimetres. A rectangle 6 cm by 5 cm also has area 30 square centimetres. Different shapes, same area — the numbers, not the shape, decide the answer.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid with Area and Perimeter

The classic mistake is swapping the formulas: using length + width for area, or length × width for perimeter. The fence-versus-carpet picture fixes this: a fence is walked (added), a carpet is covered (multiplied into a grid). If your method does not match the picture, it is the wrong method.

Another mistake is forgetting the 1/2 in the triangle area formula. Without it, your answer is double the truth. Always write the 1/2 first when you set up a triangle area problem, before any numbers go in.

A third mistake is using a slanted side as the height of a triangle. The height must be perpendicular to the base. If the triangle's height is not given or cannot be found, you cannot use the 1/2 × base × height formula directly — you need another route, such as splitting the triangle or using known side lengths.

Perimeter is the fence — add up all the sides, measured in ordinary units. Area is the carpet — cover the inside, measured in square units, with length × width for rectangles and 1/2 × base × height for triangles. Keep the picture in mind, write the units every time, and check that equal perimeters do not trick you into assuming equal areas. With the fence-versus-carpet habit, area and perimeter stay permanently untangled.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between area and perimeter?

Perimeter is the distance around the outside of a shape, measured in ordinary units like centimetres. Area is the amount of space inside the shape, measured in square units like square centimetres. Think fence for perimeter and carpet for area.

### How do you find the perimeter of a rectangle?

Add all four sides, or use the shortcut P = 2 × (length + width). For a rectangle 8 cm by 5 cm, the perimeter is 2 × (8 + 5) = 26 cm. Opposite sides of a rectangle are equal, so count each pair twice.

### How do you find the area of a triangle?

Use area = 1/2 × base × height, where the height is perpendicular to the base. For a base of 10 cm and a height of 4 cm, the area is 1/2 × 10 × 4 = 20 square centimetres. Never forget the 1/2.

### Can two shapes have the same perimeter but different areas?

Yes. A 7 cm by 3 cm rectangle and a 5 cm square both have perimeter 20 cm, but their areas are 21 and 25 square centimetres. Perimeter and area are independent measures, so one never determines the other.

### What units do you use for area?

Square units: square centimetres (cm²), square metres (m²), or square kilometres (km²). If your area answer is in plain centimetres, you have probably used a perimeter method by mistake. Writing the units every time catches this error early.
