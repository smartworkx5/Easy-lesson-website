Formative assessment is a plain idea with an unhelpful name. It means finding out what students understand while there is still time to do something about it. Not at the end of the term, when the results arrive too late to help. During the lesson, or right after it, when a quick adjustment can change everything. The best formative assessment is fast, simple, and used the next day.

This guide is a menu of ideas you can use tomorrow, with no special materials and no extra marking. Each idea answers the same question: what do my students understand right now, and what should I do about it?

## What Formative Assessment Actually Is

Formative assessment is any check that shapes what happens next. A question you ask during a lesson, a task students complete, a short written answer at the end of class — all of these are formative assessment if you use the results to decide what to teach next. The key word is "use." A check that produces information nobody acts on is not formative assessment. It is just a check.

It helps to separate formative assessment from grading. Formative checks are for learning, not for judging. Students should feel safe being wrong in them, because a wrong answer is useful information for both the student and the teacher. When formative checks carry grades, students hide their confusion instead of revealing it, and the teacher loses the very information the check was meant to provide.

Formative assessment also does not need to be formal. You do not need a rubric, a spreadsheet, or a special form. You need a quick way to see what every student understands, and a habit of acting on what you see. A single question, asked well and used well, is formative assessment.

## Quick Checks That Take Five Minutes

The fastest formative checks take five minutes or less and involve every student. One of the simplest is the one-question check: after teaching an idea, ask every student to answer one question that uses it. Collect the answers on small slips, on small whiteboards, or in notebooks you can scan quickly. In two minutes of reading, you know whether the class is ready to move on.

Another quick check is the confidence check. After a task, ask students to rate their own understanding: confident, unsure, or lost. A quick show of hands, or a mark in the corner of the page, gives you a rough map of the class. Confidence is not the same as understanding — a student can feel confident and be wrong — so pair it with a content question. The combination tells you who is confident and right, confident and wrong, and unsure but close.

A third quick check is the mistake hunt. Show the class a worked solution that contains one error, and ask students to find it. This check does two jobs at once: it reveals whether students understand the method well enough to spot a wrong step, and it teaches them that errors are normal and findable. Students who can find a mistake understand the idea more deeply than students who can only copy a correct solution.

## Exit Tickets That Tell You Something

An exit ticket is a short task students complete before leaving the lesson. The classic version has two or three questions: one recall question, one application question, and one reflection question such as "What was the hardest part of today's lesson?" The ticket takes three to five minutes, and it gives you a record of the whole class, not just the students who spoke.

The power of an exit ticket is in what you do with it. After class, sort the tickets quickly into three piles: got it, partly there, and not yet. You do not need to mark each one in detail. The piles tell you what tomorrow's lesson needs: a quick recap, a reteaching step, or a stretch task. A ticket you sort in five minutes and use the next day is worth far more than a detailed quiz you mark a week later.

Keep exit tickets short and consistent. The same format every lesson — same number of questions, same reflection prompt — means students know what to expect and you can sort them fast. Vary the content, not the format. And keep the reflection question. It often tells you things the content questions cannot, such as which step confused the class or which explanation landed best.

## Questions That Reveal Thinking

Some questions reveal more than others. A question with a single short answer tells you whether a student got it right. A question that asks for reasoning tells you how the student thinks. For formative assessment, reasoning questions are far more useful, because they show you the misunderstanding, not just the mistake.

Useful formats include: "Explain why this answer is correct," "What would happen if we changed this one thing?" and "Which of these two answers is right, and how do you know?" Each format forces students to show their thinking, which gives you something to respond to. A wrong answer with visible reasoning is more useful than a right answer with none, because it points to the exact point where the thinking went wrong.

Student questions are formative assessment too. The questions students ask reveal what confuses them. Make space for them. A regular "what questions do you still have?" slot at the end of a lesson, where students write one question each, gives you a list of the class's real confusions. Answering those questions the next day is one of the most efficient forms of reteaching there is. ## Use What You Learn the Next Day

Formative assessment only works if it changes the next lesson. The simplest way to do that is to start the next lesson with the results. "Yesterday, most of you got this step right, but this step tripped many people up. Let us look at it again." That opening takes two minutes, and it shows students that their answers mattered. It also fixes the misunderstanding before it hardens.

Plan three responses in advance, so acting on results is easy. If most students got it, move on to the stretch task. If about half got it, reteach the tricky step with a new example, then check again. If most students missed it, reteach the idea a different way from the start. Having these three responses ready means you never have to invent a plan while standing in front of the class.

Over time, keep a short note of what each check revealed. A line per lesson — "exit ticket: fractions step 3 still shaky" — is enough. These notes build into a picture of the class across a term: which topics need more time, which errors keep returning, and which explanations worked. That picture makes future planning far easier than memory alone.

## Conclusion

Formative assessment does not need to be complicated. Ask one good question, collect an exit ticket, sort the answers into three piles, and use what you learn the next day. When students see that their answers change what happens in class, they answer honestly, and every check makes the next lesson a little better. That is formative assessment doing its real job.

## FAQ

### How is formative assessment different from a test?

A test usually measures learning at the end of a unit and carries a grade. Formative assessment checks learning during the unit and shapes what happens next. Its purpose is to guide teaching, not to judge students.

### How often should I use formative assessment?

A small check every lesson is ideal: one question, an exit ticket, or a quick confidence check. Short daily checks give you a steady picture of the class and let you adjust before misunderstandings grow.

### Does formative assessment mean more marking?

No. The fastest formative checks need no detailed marking — just a quick sort into got it, partly there, and not yet. The value is in what the check tells you, not in a mark on each paper.

### What if formative checks show most of the class is lost?

Reteach the idea a different way from the start, with a new example and smaller steps, then check again. A class-wide gap means the teaching needs another approach, not that the students failed. The check did its job by revealing the gap early.
