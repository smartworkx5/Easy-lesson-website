Hard problems often feel brand new, but most of them are old problems wearing disguises. Pattern spotting is the skill of seeing through the disguise. When you notice what repeats, what changes, and what rule connects them, a strange problem turns into a familiar one you already know how to solve.

This skill shows up everywhere: number sequences in maths, repeating structures in grammar, cycles in science, and recurring themes in literature. Learn to spot patterns deliberately and every subject gets a little easier.

## What Is Pattern Spotting?

Pattern spotting means looking at a set of items and asking three questions. What stays the same? What changes? What rule connects the two? That is the whole technique. The items can be numbers, shapes, words, events, or experiment results.

Beginners look at each question as a separate event. Skilled students look at a question and immediately ask what it resembles. That shift, from reading the surface to reading the structure, is what makes some students seem fast. They are not thinking faster. They are recognising sooner. You can train this by slowing down on purpose for a few weeks and narrating what you see: this looks like the same shape as the last problem, only with different numbers.

## Number Patterns: What Repeats and What Changes

Start with sequences, because they are the cleanest training ground. Write the first few terms and look at the gaps between them. If the gaps are constant, you are looking at a steady step. If the gaps themselves grow by a constant amount, something is accelerating. If each term multiplies by the same factor, you have growth, which behaves very differently from adding.

Always check two things before you commit to a rule. First, test your rule on at least two more terms, because the first guess is often wrong. Second, ask what the rule predicts for the next item and see if the answer choices or the question context agree. A powerful habit is to keep a short list of the patterns you meet: steady steps, doubling, alternating signs, squares, and prime-based sequences. Each new pattern you name becomes a template your brain can reuse.

## Shape and Sequence Patterns

Visual patterns work the same way as number patterns, except the items are shapes, positions, or arrangements. Cover the answer choices and describe what is changing in words: the triangle rotates a quarter turn each time, one dot is added to the left side, the shaded region moves clockwise.

Describing the change in words is the secret step most students skip. When you force yourself to name the change, vague seeing becomes precise thinking, and the correct answer usually follows. A classic trap is to watch only one feature. Real patterns often change two things at once, like size and shading, while a third feature stays constant. Train yourself to track features separately: shape, size, colour, position, and count. Check each one in turn and the combined rule will emerge.

## Turning Hard Problems into Familiar Ones

The payoff of pattern spotting is recognition. A word problem about filling tanks looks hard until you notice it has the same structure as the work-rate problem you solved last week. An unfamiliar grammar exercise becomes easy when you see it is just the same sentence pattern with new vocabulary.

Build this deliberately with a recognition habit. After solving any problem, spend thirty seconds writing what made it solvable: this was a gap-finding sequence, this was a two-feature shape rotation, this was an either-or logic structure. Over time you build a personal library of patterns. When a new problem arrives, you flip through the library instead of starting from zero. ## FAQ

### How do I get better at spotting patterns quickly?

Practise slowly first. Narrate what you see in words before you guess the rule. Speed comes naturally after a few weeks of careful, deliberate practice. Trying to rush pattern spotting usually just trains you to guess.

### What if my guessed pattern rule is wrong?

Being wrong is useful information. A failed guess tells you which feature does not control the pattern, so you can try the next feature. Test every guess against at least two more items before trusting it.

### Do patterns help outside maths?

Yes. Grammar rules are sentence patterns, verb conjugations are ending patterns, science cycles are process patterns, and historical events often follow cause-and-effect patterns. The skill transfers because the habit, look for what repeats and what changes, is the same everywhere.

### Should I memorise common patterns?

Memorising a few common ones helps, but naming patterns you discover yourself helps more. Keep a personal pattern list and add to it as you learn. Your own examples stick better than any list in a book.

## Putting It Together

Pattern spotting is three questions asked every time: what stays the same, what changes, and what rule connects them. Practise on sequences, then shapes, then real problems, always describing the change in words before you commit to an answer. Keep your pattern library growing and watch hard problems turn familiar.
