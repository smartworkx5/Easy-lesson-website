A shadow sundial experiment lets you tell the time with nothing but sunlight and a shadow. As the day passes, the shadow of a stick moves steadily around a marked dial, and the pattern it traces reveals one of the biggest ideas in science: Earth is turning. Building and reading your own sundial makes that idea visible in a way that reading about it never quite does.

## Materials and Setup for the Shadow Sundial Experiment

You need a paper plate or large card, a pencil or short stick as the gnomon, modelling clay or tape to hold it upright, a marker pen, a ruler, and a watch or clock for comparison. Choose a flat, sunny spot that stays in sunlight most of the day, and do not move the dial once you start. Fix the gnomon upright in the centre, not leaning, since a leaning gnomon casts a misleading shadow.

## Marking Shadows Through the Day

Place the plate flat in sunlight and mark the end of the shadow with a short line and the clock time. Return every hour, or every half hour if you can, and mark each new shadow position with its time. Do not move the plate between markings, since even a small shift ruins the comparison. If clouds cover the sun, skip that hour and note the gap honestly. By day's end, your plate will carry a fan of time-marked lines.

## What to Observe as Shadows Move

Notice the shadow's direction, length, and speed of movement. In the morning shadows are long and point one way; around midday they are shortest; in the afternoon they lengthen again and point the opposite way. The shadow moves steadily, not in jumps, which is why regular markings give a smooth fan shape. A sundial needs direct sunlight, so it cannot tell the time at night or under thick cloud.

## Why the Shadow Sundial Experiment Works

The shadow moves because Earth rotates, turning your location steadily relative to the Sun. As Earth turns, the Sun appears to move across the sky, and the shadow of a fixed object sweeps around in the opposite sense. The gnomon does not move; the changing angle of sunlight does all the work, and your dial captures that changing angle as a sequence of positions. The shortest shadow falls around midday, when the Sun is highest, while morning and evening shadows are longer because low sunlight stretches them across the ground.

## Variables You Can Test Next

Change one thing at a time: a taller gnomon against a shorter one, half-hour markings instead of hourly ones for a finer dial, or the same dial in a different spot to see why the marks no longer match. You can also compare shadows of different objects at the same time, such as a pencil, a bottle, and a tall stick, noting how shadow lengths relate to heights. Keep time and location identical for fair comparison, and photograph each set of shadows with a ruler for scale.

## Staying Safe and Recording Your Results

Adult supervision is needed for long periods outdoors, and sun safety matters: wear a hat, use shade between checks, drink water, and never look directly at the Sun to judge its position; judge it only by the shadow. Record a photo and a short report: marked times, the pattern of movement, and your explanation in terms of Earth's rotation. Check your explanation against your textbook, remembering that AI tools can make mistakes.

## FAQ

### Why does the shadow move if the stick stays still?

The stick stays still, but Earth rotates, changing the angle at which sunlight reaches it. As that angle changes, the shadow sweeps around the dial. The moving shadow is evidence of Earth's rotation, seen from the ground where you stand.

### Why is the midday shadow the shortest?

Around midday the Sun is highest in the sky, so sunlight strikes the gnomon from above and the shadow falls close to its base. In the morning and evening the Sun is lower, so sunlight strikes at a slant and stretches the shadow out much longer.

### Will my sundial work on a cloudy day?

No, because a sundial needs direct sunlight to cast a clear shadow. Under thick cloud the shadow is too faint to read, and at night there is no sunlight at all. That is why people needed other timekeepers, such as clocks, for cloudy days and nights.

### Why must the sundial stay in exactly the same spot?

Shadow positions depend on the location and orientation of the dial as well as the time of day. Moving or turning the plate changes the shadow pattern, so the hour marks no longer match. Keeping the dial fixed is what makes the second-day test a fair check.

## Conclusion

A shadow sundial turns sunlight into a timekeeper and Earth's rotation into something you can watch. Mark shadows hourly, note their changing length and direction, and test your dial on a second sunny day. That patient observation connects a simple paper plate to the turning of the planet itself.
