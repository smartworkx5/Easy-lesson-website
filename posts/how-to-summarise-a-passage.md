Learning how to summarise a passage is one of the fastest ways to prove you understand it. A summary is a short version of a text that keeps only the main ideas, written in your own words. It is not a copy, and it is not a list of random facts. If someone reads only your summary, they should understand what the original text was saying.

Many students find summarising hard because they try to keep too much. They copy whole sentences, include every example, and end up with a summary nearly as long as the original. The three-step method in this guide fixes that: delete, keep, rewrite.

## What Makes a Good Summary

A good summary is short, accurate, and in your own words. Short means it keeps only the main ideas, usually in about a quarter of the original length or less. Accurate means it keeps the writer's meaning, without adding your opinions or changing the emphasis. Your own words means you have understood the text well enough to restate it, not just rearranged a few copied phrases.

A summary also keeps the structure of the original. If the text explains a cause and then an effect, your summary should keep that order. If it compares two ideas, keep the comparison. Structure is part of the meaning, and dropping it makes a summary confusing.

## How to Summarise a Passage: Delete, Keep, Rewrite

The method has three steps. First, delete: cross out examples, repetitions, minor details, and explanations that only illustrate a point already made. Second, keep: list the main ideas that remain, in the order the text presents them. Third, rewrite: turn that list into a short paragraph in your own words, linking the ideas with simple connecting words.

![Delete keep rewrite summary method](/assets/images/blog/how-to-summarise-a-passage-diagram-1.svg)

Work from your list, not from the original text, when you rewrite. Looking at the original while writing tempts you to copy its sentences. Looking only at your list forces you to use your own words, which is exactly the point.

## Step 1: Delete the Extra Detail

Read the passage fully first, then go back and mark what can go. Examples can go, once you have noted the point they illustrate. Repeated ideas can go; keep only the clearest version. Minor details, lists of names or numbers that do not change the meaning, and side remarks can all go. What stays is the skeleton: the main ideas and the links between them.

Be careful with two kinds of detail. Definitions of key terms should stay, because the summary needs them to make sense. Numbers that carry the argument should stay too. The test is simple: if removing a detail changes what the passage means, keep it. If removing it only makes the passage shorter, delete it.

## Step 2: Keep the Main Ideas

After deleting, list the main ideas in order. A paragraph usually contributes one main idea, so a four-paragraph passage gives you roughly four points. Use the skill from our guide on [how to find the main idea](/blog/how-to-find-the-main-idea.html): ask what each paragraph is mostly saying, and write that point in a few words.

Check your list when it is done. Does it cover the whole passage, from beginning to end? Does it keep the writer's emphasis, so the most important idea gets the most space? If one section of the original is missing from your list, you deleted too much. If one point repeats another, combine them.

## Step 3: Rewrite in Your Own Words

Now turn your list into a paragraph. Use your own sentence structures, not the writer's. Swap in simpler words where you can, and link the ideas with words like "first", "because", "however", and "as a result". Read your summary aloud: it should sound like you explaining the text to a friend.

Then check your summary against the original. Is anything important missing? Did you add any opinion or fact the original never stated? Is it clearly shorter? A quick check against the original catches most mistakes, and it trains your eye for the next summary.

## Mistakes That Make Summaries Fail

The most common mistake is copying sentences and changing a word or two. That is not summarising; it is paraphrasing badly, and it shows you have not digested the text. Another mistake is writing a summary that only covers the first half, because attention faded. Always check that your list reaches the end of the passage.

A third mistake is adding your own view. A summary reports what the text says, even if you disagree with it. Keep opinions for a separate response. Finally, do not make the summary a list of unconnected facts. Link the ideas, keep the order, and make it read as one short paragraph. ## FAQ

### How long should a summary be?

Usually about a quarter of the original length or less. A 400-word passage often becomes a summary of 80 to 100 words. The exact length matters less than coverage: every main idea must survive, and most detail must go.

### Can I use any words from the original?

Yes, for key terms and names that have no good substitute. Technical terms, names, and dates can stay. What should change is the sentence structure and most of the wording, so the summary is genuinely in your own words.

### What is the difference between a summary and a paraphrase?

A paraphrase restates a short passage at roughly the same length, keeping all the detail. A summary shortens a whole text, keeping only the main ideas. Summarising is the harder skill, because it forces you to judge what matters.

### How can I practise summarising?

Summarise one short passage a day. Read it, list the main ideas, rewrite them in your own words, then compare your summary with the original. Over time, try longer passages and tighter word limits, such as a fifty-word summary of a full page.

## Conclusion

Summarising is a three-step habit: delete the extra detail, keep the main ideas, and rewrite them briefly in your own words. Practise it daily, and you will read with sharper judgement and remember far more of what you read.
