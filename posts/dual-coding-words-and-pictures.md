The dual coding study technique is simple: combine words and pictures when you learn. Instead of relying on written notes alone, you add a diagram, a sketch, a timeline, or a labelled drawing that shows the same idea visually. Your brain then stores the idea in two forms, verbal and visual, and two routes to the same memory make recall easier and stronger.

This does not require artistic talent. A rough sketch with labels is often more useful than a beautiful drawing, because the learning happens when you decide what to draw and how to label it. A quick diagram you make yourself will help you more than a polished diagram you only glance at. Dual coding is about thinking visually, not drawing beautifully.

## The Dual Coding Study Technique in Action

The dual coding study technique works in three steps: read or listen to the idea, draw a simple visual for it, and label the visual in your own words. For example, if you are learning the parts of a plant, you might draw a simple plant, label the roots, stem, and leaves, and write one short phrase beside each label explaining its job. The drawing and the words together carry the full idea.

You can use dual coding with almost any topic. For a process, draw a flowchart with boxes and arrows. For a comparison, draw a two-column table with small sketches. For a timeline, draw a line with events marked along it. For a hierarchy, draw a tree with branches. The visual form should match the shape of the idea: steps become flowcharts, sequences become timelines, and relationships become maps.

The key rule is that the picture and the words must refer to the same idea. A decoration beside your notes is not dual coding. A diagram that shows the same content as your notes, in visual form, is dual coding. When the two match, each one strengthens the other.

## Why Pictures Help Words Stick

Pictures help words stick because they give your brain a second way to store and retrieve the same idea. Words are stored as language. Pictures are stored as images. When you learn with both, you build two paths to the memory. On a test, if the verbal path is weak, the visual path can still bring the idea back. You might remember the diagram you drew, and the labels on it, even when the textbook sentence has faded.

Pictures also force you to understand. You can copy a paragraph without understanding it, but you cannot draw a diagram of a process without deciding what the steps are and how they connect. Choosing what to draw is a thinking task. That thinking is what makes the idea stick.

Visuals also organise information. A page of text hides the structure of an idea. A diagram shows the structure at a glance: what comes first, what causes what, and how the parts fit together. That visible structure is easier to recall than a paragraph, because you can picture the whole shape of the idea at once.

## Simple Diagrams Anyone Can Draw

You only need a few diagram types to cover most topics. The first is the flowchart: boxes connected by arrows, showing steps in order. Use it for processes, methods, and any sequence where order matters. Keep each box to a few words, and make the arrows show the direction clearly.

The second is the comparison diagram: two columns or two overlapping circles, showing how two things are alike and different. Use it when a lesson compares two ideas, methods, or groups. The visual side-by-side layout makes similarities and differences obvious.

The third is the timeline: a horizontal line with events marked in order. Use it for history, life cycles, and any topic where the order of events matters. Adding a small sketch at each event makes the timeline even more memorable.

The fourth is the concept map: a central idea in the middle, with branches for each main point, and smaller branches for details. Use it for topics with many connected ideas. Concept maps show the whole topic on one page, which makes them excellent for revision.

The fifth is the labelled diagram: a simple drawing with labels and arrows pointing to each part. Use it for objects, systems, and structures. The labels carry the words, and the drawing carries the layout. Together, they make the structure easy to remember.

## Label Everything You Draw

Labels are what turn a doodle into a study tool. Every part of your diagram should have a label in your own words. A box in a flowchart should say what happens at that step. An arrow should say what leads to what. A part in a labelled diagram should say what it is and what it does. Without labels, a diagram is just shapes. With labels, it is a complete explanation.

Write labels briefly. A label of three to seven words is usually enough. If a label needs a whole sentence, the idea may need two boxes instead of one. Short labels keep the diagram readable at a glance, which is the point of drawing it.

Use your own words in labels, not copied textbook sentences. Your own words connect the diagram to your own understanding. When you review the diagram later, your own labels will remind you of the reasoning you did when you drew it, and that reasoning is what you are trying to remember.

## Turn Notes Into a One-Page Visual Summary

One of the best uses of dual coding is the one-page visual summary. After studying a topic, take a blank page and summarise the whole topic with a mix of diagrams and short labels. Put the main idea in the centre or at the top. Add the key points as branches, boxes, or a flowchart. Include one small sketch for each major idea. Keep words short and let the visuals carry the structure.

A one-page summary is powerful for revision. Before a test, you can review a whole topic by studying one page instead of rereading ten pages of notes. The page also shows you what you do not yet know: if you cannot draw a part of the topic, that part needs more study.

Make the summary after you have studied, not during the first read. First, learn the topic. Then, close your notes and build the summary from memory. Checking it against your notes afterwards shows you exactly what you missed. This combination of dual coding and recall is far stronger than copying a diagram from the textbook.

## Common Dual Coding Mistakes

The most common mistake is copying diagrams without thinking. A copied diagram looks neat, but it teaches little, because someone else did the thinking. Always draw your own version, even if it is rougher. Your own diagram carries your own decisions, and those decisions are the learning.

Another mistake is making diagrams too complicated. A diagram crammed with tiny text and tangled arrows is hard to read and hard to remember. Keep diagrams simple: a few boxes, clear labels, and plenty of white space. If a diagram gets crowded, split it into two simpler diagrams.

A third mistake is drawing without words, or writing without pictures. Dual coding needs both. A picture without labels is vague. Words without a picture miss the visual path. Check every study page: does it have both a visual and a few words explaining it? If one is missing, add it.

## Conclusion

Dual coding works because two paths to a memory are stronger than one. Draw simple flowcharts, comparisons, timelines, concept maps, and labelled diagrams for what you study. Label everything in your own words, and turn each topic into a one-page visual summary from memory. You do not need to be an artist. You only need to think in pictures as well as words.

## FAQ

### Do I need to be good at drawing to use dual coding?

No. Simple boxes, arrows, stick figures, and rough sketches work perfectly. The learning comes from deciding what to draw and how to label it, not from artistic quality. A messy diagram you made yourself beats a beautiful one you only looked at.

### What subjects work best with dual coding?

All of them. Science and geography suit labelled diagrams and processes. Language study suits word maps and comparison charts. History suits timelines. Maths suits worked diagrams and flowcharts. Any topic with structure, steps, or parts can be drawn.

### How is dual coding different from just adding pictures to notes?

Decoration is not dual coding. Dual coding means the picture and the words carry the same content, so each strengthens the other. A diagram that shows the same idea as your notes, with labels in your own words, is dual coding. A random picture beside your notes is not.

### How often should I make visual summaries?

Make a one-page visual summary at the end of each topic, and review it during spaced revision. The summary takes ten to fifteen minutes to build and saves much more time later, because it lets you review a whole topic from a single page.
