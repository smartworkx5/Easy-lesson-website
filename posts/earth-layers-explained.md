Layers of the Earth explained simply start with a familiar comparison: the Earth is like an onion, made of layers stacked one inside another. From the outside in, those layers are the crust, the mantle, the outer core, and the inner core. Each layer is different in thickness, temperature, and material. Nobody has ever travelled to the centre of the Earth, yet scientists have worked out what each layer is like by studying earthquakes, volcanoes, and rocks.

## The Crust: The Thin Outer Skin

The crust is the outermost layer of the Earth, the thin skin we live on. It is made of solid rock, and it is much thinner than the other layers. Under the oceans, the crust is only a few kilometres thick. Under the continents, it is thicker, reaching several tens of kilometres. Compared with the whole Earth, the crust is like the skin of an apple: a very thin covering over a much larger inside.

The crust is broken into large pieces called tectonic plates, which fit together like a jigsaw puzzle over the surface. These plates move very slowly, and their movement causes earthquakes, builds mountains, and opens oceans. The rocks of the crust include the soil, the seabed, and the rocks of every mountain and valley you have ever seen.

## The Mantle: Hot Rock That Slowly Moves

Below the crust lies the mantle, a very thick layer of hot rock that makes up most of the Earth's volume. The mantle is far hotter than the crust, and although its rock is mostly solid, it is soft enough in places to flow extremely slowly, like thick honey moving over millions of years. This slow movement carries heat upward from the deep Earth toward the surface.

The upper part of the mantle, together with the crust, forms the rigid plates that move across the surface. The deeper mantle is hotter and under greater pressure. Heat from the mantle drives the movement of the plates and powers volcanic activity. When magma, which is molten rock from below, reaches the surface through a volcano, it is called lava, and it cools into new rock.

## The Outer Core: Liquid Metal

Below the mantle lies the outer core, a layer of liquid metal, mostly iron mixed with nickel. It is extremely hot, far hotter than anything on the surface. The outer core is liquid because the temperature there is high enough to melt the metal, even though the pressure is enormous. This liquid metal is always moving, stirred by heat and by the rotation of the Earth.

The movement of the liquid outer core is very important. It creates the Earth's magnetic field, which acts like a giant invisible shield around the planet. The magnetic field protects us from harmful particles streaming from the Sun, and it is what makes compasses point north. Without the moving liquid outer core, the Earth would have no magnetic field and life on the surface would face far harsher conditions.

## The Inner Core: A Solid Ball of Metal

At the very centre of the Earth is the inner core, a solid ball of iron and nickel. It is the hottest part of the planet. Even though it is hotter than the outer core, it stays solid because the pressure at the centre is so enormous that the metal cannot melt. Imagine being squeezed from every side by the weight of the entire planet: that pressure holds the inner core's atoms tightly together as a solid.

The inner core is relatively small compared with the other layers, but it plays a key role in the Earth's story. It slowly grows as the Earth cools, and heat released from the inner core helps drive the motion of the liquid outer core. Together, the inner and outer cores are the engine room of the planet.

## How Scientists Know About the Layers

No one has drilled to the centre of the Earth. The deepest holes ever drilled reach only a tiny fraction of the way through the crust. So how do scientists know about the layers? The main tool is the study of earthquakes. When an earthquake happens, waves of energy travel through the Earth. These waves, called seismic waves, speed up, slow down, bend, or stop depending on the material they pass through. By recording how the waves arrive at stations around the world, scientists can work out the structure inside the planet.

Volcanoes and mountain rocks also give clues. Lava brings up material from below, and some rocks that were once deep inside the Earth have been pushed to the surface by plate movements. Laboratory experiments, in which scientists squeeze and heat materials to mimic deep-Earth conditions, add further evidence. Put together, these lines of evidence give a consistent picture of the four layers.

## Conclusion

The Earth is made of four layers: the thin solid crust we live on, the thick hot mantle, the liquid metal outer core, and the solid metal inner core. Each layer differs in material, temperature, and behaviour, and together they drive plate movement, volcanoes, and the magnetic field.
