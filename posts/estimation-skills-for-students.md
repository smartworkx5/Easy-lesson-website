# Estimation Skills: Check Answers Before You Trust Them

Here is a painful but common story: a student solves a problem carefully, writes down an answer, and never notices that the answer is impossible — a person's height of 180 metres, a total cost smaller than one of its parts, a journey time longer than a day. Estimation is the skill that catches these mistakes before they cost marks. It means making a quick, rough calculation before or after the real one, to check that the answer is in the right neighbourhood. This guide teaches you to round sensibly, use order-of-magnitude checks, and build the sanity-check reflex that separates careful students from merely hardworking ones.

## What Estimation Really Is

Estimation is not guessing, and it is not being sloppy. It is deliberately trading precision for speed to get a rough answer fast — close enough to judge whether the exact answer is reasonable. If your careful calculation says 4,832 and your estimate says "about 5,000", the calculation is probably fine. If your estimate says "about 500", something went wrong, and you have caught it before handing in the work.

Think of estimation as a second, independent witness to your answer. Your main calculation and your estimate reach the answer by different routes — one precise, one rough. When two independent routes agree, confidence is high. When they disagree, you investigate. This is the same principle engineers and scientists use: no important number is trusted from a single computation. Building this habit in school trains a professional-grade instinct for whether numbers make sense.

## Rounding Sensibly

Good estimation starts with good rounding. The goal is to replace awkward numbers with friendly ones that are easy to compute mentally while staying close to the originals. Round 47 to 50, 1,973 to 2,000, 0.48 to a half. The key word is sensibly: rounding 47 to 100 would make the arithmetic easy but the estimate useless, because it is too far from reality. A good rule is to keep the first digit and round the rest — 4,731 becomes 5,000 or 4,700, never 4,000.

For multiplication and division, round one number up and one down when you can; the errors partly cancel and the estimate lands closer. Estimating 49 times 21: round to 50 times 20 and get 1,000, a fine estimate of the true 1,029. For division like 4,850 divided by 49, think 5,000 divided by 50 — about 100. With percentages, anchor to easy ones: 10 percent is just moving the decimal point, and most other percentages are combinations of 10s, 5s, and 1s. These small skills compound: students who round fluently estimate fluently, and students who estimate fluently catch errors fluently.

## Order-of-Magnitude Checks

Sometimes you do not need a close estimate — you need to know whether the answer has the right number of digits. That is an order-of-magnitude check, and it is the fastest error-catcher there is. Before or after calculating, ask: should this answer be in the tens, hundreds, or thousands? If you are multiplying two two-digit numbers, the answer must be in the hundreds or low thousands — an answer of 45 or 45,000 is impossible, no matter how careful the working looked.

Train this with the "how many zeros" game: glance at a calculation and predict the size of the answer before doing it. Dividing 7,200 by 90? About 80 — tens, not hundreds. Multiplying 0.4 by 0.3? Less than 0.4, around 0.12 — decimals multiply smaller, a fact that surprises many students until the magnitude check makes it obvious. This habit is especially powerful with calculators, because calculator errors (wrong button, missing decimal) produce answers that look authoritative but are wildly off. The student who checks magnitude is immune to the most embarrassing calculator mistakes.

## The Sanity-Check Reflex

Beyond numbers, estimation includes asking whether an answer makes sense in the real situation — the sanity check. A probability above 1 or below 0 is impossible. An average cannot be larger than every value in the set. A rectangle's diagonal must be longer than either side but shorter than their sum. These are not calculations; they are consistency rules, and running through them takes seconds.

Build the reflex with three questions after every answer: Is the size plausible? Are the units right? Does it answer what was asked? The units question alone catches a huge class of errors — an area in centimetres instead of square centimetres, a speed without a time unit. The third question catches the classic mistake of solving for the wrong thing, like finding the number of girls when the question asked for boys. Students who ask these three questions as a matter of routine hand in work with dramatically fewer silly errors, and examiners notice.

## Practising Estimation Daily

Estimation is a skill, which means it improves with short, regular practice — not with one long lesson. Build it into work you already do: estimate every answer before calculating it in homework, and compare afterwards. When you see numbers in daily life — prices, distances, times — estimate totals, changes, and journey times in your head. Shopping is a natural gym: estimate the basket total before the till tells you, and notice how close you get.

In class, volunteer estimates before exact answers when the teacher asks; the classroom is a safe place to be roughly right. Play the prediction game with past papers: cover the answers, estimate each result's magnitude, then solve and compare. Over a few weeks you will feel number sense growing — that quiet confidence about what numbers should look like. That confidence is the real product of estimation practice. The error-catching is just its most visible benefit.

## FAQ

### Isn't estimation just a less accurate way of calculating?

No — it is a different tool for a different job. Exact calculation gives the answer; estimation judges the answer. You would not use a thermometer to check whether a thermometer works, and you should not use the same computation to check itself. Estimation's value is independence: a rough second route to the answer that catches errors the precise route missed.

### When should I estimate — before or after solving?

Both, for different reasons. Estimating before solving sets your expectation, so a wrong answer feels wrong immediately. Estimating or sanity-checking after solving catches the errors that slipped through. If time is short, the after-check is the higher priority — but the before-estimate is what builds number sense fastest, because you practise predicting rather than just verifying.

### What if my estimate and my answer disagree?

Trust the estimate's warning, not the answer. Recalculate carefully, preferably by a slightly different route — redo the arithmetic, check each copied number, verify the operation. Most disagreements resolve into a slip you can find: a misplaced decimal, a wrong operation, a number copied incorrectly. If you cannot find the error, redo the problem from scratch on a clean space; fresh paper breaks the habit of re-reading your own mistake as correct.

### Does estimation help in non-maths subjects?

Yes. In science, magnitude checks catch impossible results in experiments and calculations. In geography and economics, rough figures let you judge whether a statistic is plausible before quoting it. In everyday reasoning, estimation is what tells you a claim does not add up. The habit of asking "is this roughly right?" is one of the most transferable thinking skills school teaches.

### How accurate should an estimate be?

Accurate enough for its job. For error-catching, within a factor of two or three is plenty — you are checking the neighbourhood, not the address. With practice, estimates naturally tighten. Do not chase precision in an estimate; that defeats its purpose, which is speed. A thirty-second rough check that catches one impossible answer a week has paid for itself many times over.

## The Habit That Protects Every Answer

Estimation asks for seconds and repays in marks. Start tomorrow: for every calculation in your homework, write a one-line estimate first — just the rounded numbers and the rough result. Compare it with your final answer and investigate every mismatch. Within a month, the sanity-check reflex will be running in the background of all your work, quietly catching the errors that used to slip through. Careful students calculate well. Wise students check.
