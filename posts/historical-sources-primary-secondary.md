Primary vs secondary sources is one of the most important ideas in history. A primary source comes from someone who was there: a diary, a letter, a photograph, an official record, or an object made at the time. A secondary source comes later: a textbook, an encyclopedia entry, or a documentary made by someone who studied the event afterwards. Knowing which kind of source you are reading changes how you judge it.

Historians use both kinds of sources. Primary sources give direct evidence from the time. Secondary sources organize that evidence, explain it, and place it in a wider story. This guide shows you how to tell them apart and how to use each one well.

## What Is a Primary Source?

A primary source was created during the time being studied, by someone with direct experience of it. Examples include diaries, letters, speeches, official records, newspapers from the period, photographs, maps, and objects such as tools, coins, or clothing. A diary entry written on the day of an event is a classic primary source.

Primary sources are valuable because they are close to the event. They can show what people thought, felt, and saw at the time. But they are not automatically correct. A letter writer may have misunderstood events, remembered them wrongly, or described only one side of the story.

## What Is a Secondary Source?

A secondary source was created later by someone who studied primary sources and other evidence. Textbooks, biographies, encyclopedia articles, and most history documentaries are secondary sources. Their authors did not live through the events; they gathered evidence, compared accounts, and wrote an explanation.

Secondary sources are useful because they put events in context. A textbook can explain how one event connects to others across years or decades, which a single diary entry cannot do. But secondary sources depend on the author's choices: which evidence to include, which to leave out, and how to interpret it. Always check a secondary source against the textbook and other evidence.

## Diary Versus Textbook: A Simple Example

Imagine studying a great flood that struck a town long ago. A diary written by a resident during the flood is a primary source. It might describe rising water, people leaving their homes, and fear in the streets. A textbook chapter written years later is a secondary source. It might explain what caused the flood, how many areas were affected, and what changed afterwards.

![Diary versus textbook](/assets/images/blog/historical-sources-primary-secondary-diagram-1.svg)

Neither source alone tells the full story. The diary gives feeling and detail but only one person's view. The textbook gives the big picture but loses the personal experience. Good history uses both, and checks each against the other.

## How Historians Check Evidence

Historians do not accept a source at face value. They ask several questions. Who created it, and when? Why was it created? What did the author know, and what might they have missed? Does other evidence agree with it? These questions help judge reliability.

One useful habit is to compare sources. If two independent accounts agree, the shared details are more trustworthy. If they disagree, look for reasons: different viewpoints, missing information, or a mistake in one account. If you are building a timeline for [how to study history effectively](/blog/how-to-study-history-effectively.html), note which sources each event comes from, so you can see how strong the evidence is.

## Using Sources in Your Schoolwork

When you write a history answer, name your sources and explain what each one shows. For example: a diary entry shows what people felt at the time, while a textbook explains the causes. This shows your teacher that you understand evidence, not just facts.

Be careful with sources you find online. Anyone can publish a claim, so check who wrote it, when, and what evidence it cites. A page that copies a textbook without naming sources is weaker than one that quotes primary evidence. When in doubt, verify against your textbook, since AI tools can make mistakes about sources.

## Primary, Secondary, and Everything Else

Some sources sit between the two kinds. A memoir written years after an event is based on personal experience, so it is close to primary, but memory fades over time. A news report written the next day is close to the event but still written by an observer. Rather than worrying about a perfect label, ask the key question: how close is this source to the event, and how trustworthy is it?

You can practice this skill with any topic. ## FAQ

### Is a primary source always more reliable?

No. A primary source is closer to the event, but the author may have been mistaken, biased, or poorly informed. Reliability depends on the author, the purpose, and whether other evidence agrees.

### Is a textbook a primary or secondary source?

A textbook is a secondary source. It was written later by authors who studied primary sources and other evidence to explain events. Textbooks are useful for overviews but should be checked against evidence.

### Can a photograph be a primary source?

Yes, if it was taken at the time of the event it shows. A photograph of a ceremony taken on the day is a primary source. A photograph in a modern textbook illustrating that ceremony is still primary evidence, but the caption around it is secondary.

### How do I cite sources in a history answer?

Name the source, say whether it is primary or secondary, and explain what it shows. For example: this diary entry, a primary source, shows how residents felt during the flood. Then compare it with the textbook account.
