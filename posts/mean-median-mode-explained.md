Mean median mode sounds like one topic, but it is really three different ways to answer the same question: "What is a typical value in this data?" Students often mix them up because all three are called "averages". This guide takes one small data set and compares all three averages on it, so you can see exactly what each one measures, when each is most useful, and how an outlier can push the mean around while leaving the median alone.

## Mean Median Mode: One Data Set, Three Averages

Consider five test scores: 4, 7, 7, 9, 13. These five numbers will be our running example. The mean is 8, the median is 7, and the mode is 7. Three different answers from the same data — and all three are correct, because each measures "typical" in a different way.

The mean is the arithmetic average: add everything up and divide by how many numbers there are. The median is the middle value when the data is sorted. The mode is the value that appears most often. Knowing all three, and knowing which one a question wants, is the whole skill.

Before calculating, always sort the data from smallest to largest. Sorting is essential for the median and helpful for spotting the mode. Our example is already sorted: 4, 7, 7, 9, 13. Get into the habit of sorting first, even when the list looks short.

## The Mean: Add Them Up and Divide

To find the mean, add all the values and divide by the count. For 4, 7, 7, 9, 13: the sum is 4 + 7 + 7 + 9 + 13 = 40. There are 5 numbers, so the mean is 40 ÷ 5 = 8. The mean is 8.

The mean uses every value in the data, which is both its strength and its weakness. Because every value counts, the mean reflects the whole data set — but it also means one extreme value can drag the mean up or down. If the highest score were 43 instead of 13, the sum would be 70 and the mean would jump to 14, even though four of the five scores stayed the same. That sensitivity is called the outlier effect, and it is the main reason the median exists.

A quick check for the mean: it must sit between the smallest and largest values. Here the mean 8 sits between 4 and 13, which is sensible. A mean outside that range means the arithmetic went wrong.

## The Median: The Middle Value

To find the median, sort the data and take the middle value. For 4, 7, 7, 9, 13, there are 5 numbers, so the middle one is the 3rd: 7. The median is 7. Half the values sit at or below the median, and half sit at or above it.

When there is an even count of numbers, there is no single middle value, so the median is the mean of the two middle values. For 3, 5, 8, 10, the two middle values are 5 and 8, so the median is (5 + 8) ÷ 2 = 6.5. The median can be a decimal even when all the data values are whole numbers.

The median ignores extreme values. If the 13 in our example became 43, the median would still be 7, because the middle position does not change. That stability is why the median is the best "typical" measure for data with outliers — for example, incomes in a town where a few very high earners would drag the mean far above what most people earn.

## The Mode: The Most Frequent Value

The mode is the value that appears most often. In 4, 7, 7, 9, 13, the number 7 appears twice and every other number appears once, so the mode is 7. Finding the mode is mostly a counting job, and sorting first makes the repeats easy to spot.

A data set can have no mode, one mode, or several. The set 3, 5, 8, 10 has no mode, because every value appears exactly once. The set 2, 2, 5, 9, 9, 9 has mode 9, because 9 appears three times. A set like 2, 2, 5, 5, 9 has two modes (2 and 5), called bimodal.

The mode is the only average that works for categories as well as numbers. If a survey asks for favourite colours and "blue" is chosen most often, then blue is the mode — even though colours have no mean or median. That versatility makes the mode useful far beyond number work.

## Worked Example: Comparing All Three on a New Data Set

Take the data set 2, 2, 5, 9, 9, 9. First sort it — it is already sorted. Mean: the sum is 2 + 2 + 5 + 9 + 9 + 9 = 36, and there are 6 numbers, so the mean is 36 ÷ 6 = 6. Median: with 6 numbers, take the mean of the 3rd and 4th values: (5 + 9) ÷ 2 = 7. Mode: 9 appears three times, more than any other value, so the mode is 9.

So this data set has mean 6, median 7, and mode 9 — three genuinely different answers. Which is "typical"? The mode (9) reflects the most common value, the median (7) reflects the middle position, and the mean (6) reflects the balance point of all values. A good answer to "which average is best?" always depends on the question being asked and the shape of the data.

Notice how the mean is pulled down by the two small values (2, 2) while the mode sits at the top of the range. When data is lopsided like this, the three averages spread apart. When data is symmetric, they cluster together — for 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, the mean, median, and mode pattern gives mean 8 and median 8, with no mode.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid with Averages

The most common mistake is forgetting to sort before finding the median. The median of unsorted data is meaningless: the middle of a shuffled list is just a random value. Sort first, every time, even for short lists.

Another mistake is dividing by the wrong count when finding the mean. Count the values carefully, including repeats. In 2, 2, 5, 9, 9, 9 there are 6 values, not 4 — the repeats count separately. Dividing by 4 would give 9, which is wrong.

A third mistake is claiming a mode when every value appears once. If no value repeats, there is no mode — saying "no mode" is the correct answer, not a failure. Similarly, do not invent a mode by picking the biggest or smallest value.

## Conclusion

The mean adds and divides, the median finds the middle, and the mode counts the most frequent value. One data set can give three different answers, and each is right for a different purpose. Use the mean when every value should count, the median when outliers might distort the picture, and the mode for the most common value or for categories. Sort first, count carefully, and check that the mean sits between the smallest and largest values.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between mean, median, and mode?

The mean is the arithmetic average: add all values and divide by the count. The median is the middle value of the sorted data. The mode is the value that appears most often. All three describe a "typical" value, but each measures it differently.

### When should you use the median instead of the mean?

Use the median when the data has outliers — extreme values that would drag the mean away from the typical value. For example, one very high income in a small group inflates the mean but leaves the median unchanged, so the median gives a fairer picture of the typical income.

### Can a data set have more than one mode?

Yes. If two or more values tie for the highest frequency, the data set has multiple modes. A data set with two modes is called bimodal. If every value appears exactly once, the data set has no mode at all.

### How do you find the median of an even number of values?

Sort the data and take the mean of the two middle values. For 3, 5, 8, 10, the two middle values are 5 and 8, so the median is (5 + 8) ÷ 2 = 6.5. The median can be a decimal even when all data values are whole numbers.

### Does the mean always fall between the smallest and largest values?

Yes. The mean is an average of all the values, so it can never be smaller than the smallest value or larger than the largest. If your calculated mean falls outside that range, the arithmetic went wrong — recheck the sum and the count.
