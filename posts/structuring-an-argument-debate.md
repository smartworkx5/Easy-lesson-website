A strong argument is not a loud opinion. It is a clear structure that a listener can follow and test. Structuring an argument debate speakers rely on most often has three parts: a point, a reason, and evidence. When all three are present and linked together, an argument holds up under questions. When one is missing, even a true claim can sound weak. This guide shows you how to build the three-part structure and use it in debates, essays, and class discussions.

## Start With a Point You Can Actually Defend

Your point is the claim you want your audience to accept, stated in one sentence. The most common mistake is making the point too big or too vague. "Technology is bad" cannot be defended in a short speech, because it covers everything at once. "Schools should teach students how to check information online" is a point you can actually support: it is specific, and a listener can see what would count as evidence for or against it.

Test your point before you build on it. Ask yourself: could a reasonable person disagree with this? If nobody could disagree, it is not an argument, it is a fact everyone accepts, and there is nothing to debate. Also check that your point answers the question being debated. Many speakers lose marks not because their argument is weak, but because they argue a point nobody asked about.

## Add a Reason That Explains Why

A reason answers the question "why should we believe this point?" It is the logic connecting your claim to your evidence. For example, if your point is that students should learn to check information online, a reason might be that much of what people read online is produced quickly and not always checked, so readers need their own checking skills. Notice what the reason does: it explains why the point matters and why it follows from something the audience already accepts.

One strong reason beats three weak ones. Beginners often pile up reasons, hoping quantity will persuade, but a long list of thin reasons actually weakens an argument: the audience remembers the weakest link. Choose the single reason that matters most for your audience, state it plainly, and spend your time supporting it well. If you have a second genuinely strong reason, it can become a second argument, built the same way.

## Support It With Evidence

Evidence is the material that shows your reason is true, not just plausible. It can take many forms: a statistic from a trustworthy source, an example that illustrates the point, an expert opinion, or a comparison. What matters is that the evidence really supports the reason, and that you say where it comes from. Evidence without a source is just another claim, and a sharp opponent will point that out.

Be honest about what your evidence shows. If a study found something in one group of people, do not present it as true for everyone. If an example is unusual, say so. Audiences trust speakers who show the limits of their evidence, and opponents find it much harder to attack an argument that has already admitted its own boundaries. Never invent evidence: a fabricated statistic destroys your credibility the moment anyone checks it, and in a debate, someone usually does.

## Structuring an Argument Debate: Linking the Parts So They Flow

Three good parts do not make a good argument unless they connect. Linking words do this job: "because" joins a point to its reason, "for example" introduces evidence, and "therefore" draws the conclusion. Practise saying your argument in one breath: "We should teach checking skills because online information is not always checked — for example, false stories spread quickly — therefore students need their own checks." If you cannot say it smoothly, the structure probably has a gap.

A useful check is the "so what?" test. After your point, ask "so what?" — your reason should answer it. After your reason, ask "how do you know?" — your evidence should answer that. After your evidence, ask "what follows?" — your conclusion should answer it. If any question has no answer, you have found the part to strengthen before you speak.

## Mistakes That Weaken Good Arguments

The most damaging mistake is attacking the person instead of the argument. It may feel powerful, but it convinces nobody who was undecided, and it usually costs you credibility with judges and audiences. Another common mistake is exaggeration: "everyone knows" and "always" invite a single counterexample to collapse your claim. Softening to "often" or "in many cases" costs nothing and makes your argument far harder to defeat.

A third mistake is ignoring the other side entirely. A strong speaker briefly acknowledges the best opposing point and answers it, which shows confidence and fairness. Pretending the other side has nothing to say makes your argument look sheltered. Finally, avoid reading a long script word for word: know your point, reason, and evidence well enough to speak them naturally, glancing at notes only for exact figures or quotations.

## FAQ

### How long should each part of an argument be?

In a short speech, aim for roughly one sentence for the point, two or three for the reason, and a short piece of evidence with its source. The balance matters more than the length: an argument with a long point and no evidence feels empty, however long you speak.

### Can I use a personal story as evidence?

Yes, if it genuinely illustrates your reason and you present it honestly as one person's experience. A story is vivid and memorable, but it proves less than broader evidence, so it works best alongside another form of support rather than alone.

### What if I cannot find evidence for my reason?

Then either the reason needs rethinking, or you need more research time. Never invent evidence to fill the gap. A weaker but honest argument always beats a strong-sounding one built on something false.

### How many arguments should I make in one speech?

Two or three well-built arguments usually beat five rushed ones. Each argument needs its own point, reason, and evidence, and the audience needs time to absorb each one. Depth persuades more reliably than a long list.

### Does this structure work in writing too?

Yes. Essays, reports, and even persuasive emails use the same structure: a claim, a reason it should be believed, and evidence. Learning it for debate automatically improves your writing, because the thinking underneath is identical.

## Conclusion

Point, reason, evidence: learn that structure until it feels automatic, and every argument you make will be clearer and harder to dismiss. Start with a defensible point, give one strong reason, support it with honest evidence, and link the parts so they flow. Practise on everyday topics before your next debate, and you will feel the difference immediately.
