When study time is short, practice questions are usually the first thing cut. Reading feels faster, notes feel safer, and questions feel like a test you have not prepared for yet. So students read the chapter again, tidy their notes, and promise to try the questions later, a later that often never arrives. This is the costliest study mistake, because practice questions are not a final check. They are the learning itself.

Practice is feedback. Every question you attempt tells you something no amount of reading can: whether you can actually use the material. A correct answer confirms real understanding. A wrong answer reveals a gap while there is still time to fix it. Skipping practice removes that feedback entirely, which means you walk into the test with confidence built on familiarity rather than evidence.

## Why Students Skip Practice Questions

The main reason is discomfort. Practice questions expose what you do not know, and that exposure feels bad. Reading feels good, because nothing challenges you. So the brain, given a choice between comfort and evidence, chooses comfort, and study sessions fill up with the activities that feel best rather than the ones that teach most.

Another reason is the belief that practice comes after learning, as if you must fully understand a topic before attempting its questions. This gets the order backwards. Attempting questions is one of the fastest ways to understand a topic, because each question forces you to retrieve, connect, and apply ideas. You learn by trying, failing, and correcting, not by waiting until you feel ready.

Time pressure makes it worse. When a test is close, students reason that reading covers more material faster than answering questions. But coverage without feedback is an illusion. Reading ten topics you cannot apply is worth less than practising five topics you can, because tests reward usable knowledge, not pages covered.

## Practise Equals Feedback: How It Works

A practice question does three jobs at once. First, it forces retrieval, which strengthens memory far more than re-reading. Second, it diagnoses your understanding, showing exactly which ideas are solid and which are shaky. Third, it trains the skill the test actually measures: producing an answer under conditions where the book is closed.

This is why wrong answers are valuable rather than shameful. A wrong answer is a precise diagnosis. It points to the exact idea you misunderstood, the step you skipped, or the term you confused with another. Students who practise early collect these diagnoses while there is still time to treat them. Students who skip practice collect them all at once, on test day, when treatment is impossible.

The feedback loop is simple: attempt, mark honestly, study the mistakes, retry. Each loop makes the next attempt stronger. After a few loops, the topics that once produced wrong answers become the topics you know best, because they received the most attention. Practice turns weaknesses into strengths, but only if you attempt the questions in the first place.

## How to Practise Without Dreading It

Start small and start early. Attempt a few questions right after learning a topic, while it is fresh, rather than saving all practice for the end. Early attempts feel less threatening because the stakes are low and the material is recent, and they give you immediate feedback on whether your study method is working.

Mark your answers honestly, even when it hurts. Peeking at the answer before attempting, or marking a near-miss as correct, destroys the feedback that makes practice valuable. An honest wrong answer is data. A dishonest right answer is a lie you will pay for later.

Then study the mistakes, not just the score. For every wrong answer, write one sentence explaining what went wrong: a missed step, a confused term, a misread question. This error log becomes a powerful revision sheet, because it contains exactly the ideas you personally find hardest, which are the ideas most worth revisiting.

## Fitting Practice Into a Busy Schedule

Practice does not require long sessions. Ten focused minutes of questions beats an hour of passive reading, because ten minutes of retrieval builds more memory than an hour of recognition. If you have little time, spend it on questions first and reading second, not the other way around.

Mix topics when you practise. Answering questions from several topics in one session, rather than one topic at a time, forces you to choose the right method for each question, which is exactly what a test requires. Mixed practice feels harder than blocked practice, and that difficulty is a sign it is working.

Finally, retry the questions you got wrong after a delay. A question you can answer correctly a few days after first failing it is a question you have truly learned. A question you only fixed immediately after seeing the answer may still be fragile, and a delayed retry is the honest test.

## FAQ

### Should I practise before I fully understand a topic?

Yes. Attempting questions is part of understanding, not something that waits for it. Early attempts reveal exactly what you misunderstand, which lets you study with precision instead of re-reading everything blindly.

### What should I do with questions I get wrong?

Study them, not just the score. Write one sentence for each wrong answer explaining what went wrong, then revisit those ideas and retry the question after a delay. Your error log becomes one of your most valuable revision resources.

### Is it cheating to look at the answer while practising?

Looking at the answer after an honest attempt is studying. Looking before attempting destroys the feedback, because you never discover whether you could have produced the answer yourself. Attempt first, always, then check.

### How many practice questions are enough?

Enough that you can answer the key types correctly after a delay, not just immediately after reading. Quality matters more than quantity: a small set of questions attempted, marked honestly, and retried beats a large set skimmed without feedback.
