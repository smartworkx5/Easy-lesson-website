Every strong essay follows the same basic skeleton: an introduction that sets up the argument, a body that proves it, and a conclusion that ties it together. This structure is not a cage. It is a tool that frees you to focus on your ideas, because the reader always knows where they are.

Students who struggle with essays often struggle with structure, not with ideas. They have plenty to say but no clear place to put it. Once you learn the skeleton, you can use it for almost any essay, then adapt it as your writing grows more confident.

## The Introduction: Set Up the Argument

An introduction has three jobs. First, it draws the reader in with an opening that connects to the topic. Second, it gives just enough background for the claim to make sense. Third, it ends with a thesis statement: one clear claim that the rest of the essay will support.

Keep the introduction short, roughly one paragraph for a typical school essay. Resist the urge to summarise your whole argument here. The introduction promises the argument; the body delivers it. If you find yourself writing three paragraphs of background, cut it down and save the detail for the body.

A useful test: after reading your introduction, a reader should be able to say what you are arguing and why it matters. If they cannot, your thesis statement probably needs sharpening. Our guide on [how to write a thesis statement](/blog/thesis-statement-strong-essay.html) walks through that step in detail.

## The Body: One Idea per Paragraph

The body is where the essay is won or lost. Each body paragraph should develop one main idea that supports your thesis. Start the paragraph with a topic sentence that states that idea clearly, then follow it with evidence, examples, or explanation, and finish by showing how the point connects back to your thesis.

This one-idea rule is what keeps essays readable. When a paragraph tries to make three points at once, the reader loses the thread. When each paragraph makes one point well, the argument builds step by step, and the reader can follow it without effort.

Order matters too. Arrange your body paragraphs in a sensible sequence: from the simplest point to the most complex, from the weakest evidence to the strongest, or in the order that tells the story most naturally. A short plan before you write will save you from rearranging everything later.

## Transitions: The Glue Between Paragraphs

Good essays flow. The reader should feel guided from one paragraph to the next, not dropped into a new idea without warning. Transition words and phrases do this work: however, for example, in contrast, as a result, and on the other hand each signal a different relationship between ideas.

Use transitions at the start of paragraphs and between sentences, but do not overdo them. A transition should reflect a real connection between ideas, not decorate a gap. If two paragraphs have no logical connection, the problem is usually the plan, not the wording. Fix the order or add the missing link in your thinking first.

## The Conclusion: Close, Do Not Repeat

A conclusion has one job: to leave the reader with a clear sense of what the essay proved and why it matters. The classic mistake is copying the introduction word for word. Instead, restate your thesis in fresh words, briefly gather the main points, and then zoom out to a final thought.

That final thought is what makes a conclusion memorable. It might be a wider implication of your argument, a question worth thinking about, or a practical takeaway. Keep it short and honest: do not introduce new evidence in the conclusion, and do not claim more than your essay proved. ## Planning Before You Write

The fastest way to a well-structured essay is a short plan. Write your thesis at the top of the page, then list one line per body paragraph: the main idea and the evidence you will use. Check that every line supports the thesis and that the order makes sense. This plan takes ten minutes and can save an hour of rewriting.

Planning also reveals gaps early. If you cannot find evidence for a planned paragraph, you have time to rethink it before you have written a full draft around it. Many students skip planning because it feels slow, but it is the single habit that most reliably improves essay grades.

## FAQ

### How many body paragraphs should an essay have?

As many as the argument needs, usually three to five for a school essay. One paragraph per main idea is the rule. If your thesis has three supporting points, three body paragraphs make sense. Longer essays may need more, but each paragraph should still carry exactly one idea.

### Can the introduction be more than one paragraph?

Yes, for longer essays a two-paragraph introduction can work, especially when the topic needs background. For short essays, one paragraph is usually enough. The test is whether the reader understands your thesis by the end of the introduction without feeling they have already read the essay.

### Should I write the introduction first?

Many writers draft the body first and write the introduction last, once the argument is clear. That is perfectly fine. What matters is the final order the reader sees, not the order you wrote in. If you write the introduction first, be ready to revise it after the draft.

### What should I avoid in a conclusion?

Avoid introducing new evidence, apologising for the essay, or repeating sentences word for word from the introduction. Also avoid ending suddenly after the last body paragraph. A short conclusion that restates the claim and offers a final thought gives the essay a proper finish.

## Conclusion

Introduction, body, and conclusion: that skeleton carries almost every essay you will write. Set up a clear claim, prove it one idea at a time, and close with a conclusion that gathers the argument and leaves a final thought. Learn the skeleton well, and you can adapt it to any topic with confidence.
