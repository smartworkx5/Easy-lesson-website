User interviews students can run are one of the most powerful tools in design thinking, because they reveal needs that surveys and guesses miss. A good interview does not ask people what solution they want. It asks about their real experiences: what they do, what frustrates them, what they have tried, and what a better day would look like. The answers contain stories, and stories contain the needs that a good design must serve.

Interviewing well is a skill, and like any skill it improves with preparation and practice. This guide covers how to plan questions, run the conversation, listen properly, and turn what you hear into findings your team can actually design from.

## User Interviews Students: Start With Open Questions

Open questions invite stories, while closed questions invite short answers. Asking whether someone likes their bag produces a yes or no, and teaches you almost nothing. Asking them to walk you through packing their bag this morning produces a story full of details: what they carry, what they forget, what hurts, and what they wish worked differently. Build your question list around experiences: tell me about the last time, what happens when, and how do you handle it when. Keep the list short, with a handful of core questions and room to follow interesting answers. A short list with deep follow-ups beats a long list rushed through without listening.

## Ask For Stories, Then Follow Up Gently

Stories are the richest interview material, because they show behaviour rather than opinion. When someone mentions a problem, follow up with gentle prompts: can you give me an example, what did you do next, and how did that feel. These prompts keep the person talking without steering them toward your idea. Avoid suggesting answers inside your questions, such as asking whether a bigger bag would help, because that plants your solution in their mind. Instead, stay curious about their world. If they go quiet, wait a few seconds before speaking; silence often produces the most honest next sentence. Take notes on exact phrases, because a person's own words later make findings convincing to the rest of the team.

## Listen Without Selling Your Idea

The hardest interview habit is staying neutral about your own idea. Do not explain your solution, defend it, or ask whether the person would buy or use it, because polite people will usually say yes to be kind. Your job is to understand their life, not to collect approval. If they ask what you are designing, give a brief general answer and return to their experience. Also listen for what is not said: routines people describe as normal may hide real frustration, and workarounds they mention casually often point to the biggest design opportunity. After the interview, write a short summary the same day, while details are fresh, and separate what the person actually said from what you think it means.

## Turn Answers Into Needs Your Team Can Use

Raw interview notes help nobody until they become findings. Read through the notes and highlight repeated themes, surprising stories, and exact quotes that capture a need. Group similar observations together and write each finding as a need statement: a person, a need, and the reason behind it, drawn from evidence rather than assumption. For example, a student who repacks a bag several times a day because items sink to the bottom reveals a need for quick access, not simply a bigger bag. Share findings with the team as short stories plus quotes, not as a list of statistics, because stories keep the user present in later design decisions. Check findings against more than one interview before treating them as solid.

## Interview Mistakes Students Should Avoid

Several mistakes weaken interviews. Asking about solutions instead of experiences produces wish lists rather than needs. Asking several questions at once confuses the interviewee and produces a partial answer to only one of them. Interrupting to agree or to share your own experience steals time from the person's story. Recording without permission, or sharing someone's personal details with others, breaks trust and should never happen; always explain the purpose, ask permission to take notes, and keep names out of shared findings unless the person agrees. Finally, interviewing only friends produces a narrow picture, so include people with different routines and experiences wherever possible.

## FAQ

### How many people should students interview for a project?

A small number of thoughtful interviews is enough for a classroom project, especially when the team compares findings across them. Depth matters more than volume: one detailed story about a real routine can reveal more than many shallow answers. If several interviewees describe the same problem independently, that pattern is strong evidence.

### Should students record interviews?

Notes are usually enough for a classroom project, and they are simpler and less intrusive. If the team wants to record, it must ask permission first, explain how the recording will be used, and keep it private to the team. Never share recordings or personal details beyond the team without clear permission.

### What if an interviewee gives very short answers?

Switch to story prompts: ask about the last time something happened, or what a typical day looks like step by step. Concrete situations are easier to answer than abstract opinions. If answers stay short, thank the person warmly and move on; not every interview will be rich, and that is normal.

### How do interview findings connect to the next design stage?

Findings become the evidence for defining the problem. The team reviews themes and stories, chooses the most important need, and writes a problem statement grounded in what interviewees actually said. That statement then guides brainstorming, so ideas serve real needs instead of the team's assumptions.

## Conclusion

Good user interviews are conversations built on curiosity: open questions, real stories, gentle follow-ups, and careful listening. Practise with a classmate first, then interview real users with a short question list and an open mind. The needs you discover will make every later stage of your project stronger.
