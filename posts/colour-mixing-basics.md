Colour mixing basics begin with a surprising idea: you can make a huge range of colours from just three starting colours. Those three are the primary colours: red, yellow, and blue. Mix two primaries together and you get a secondary colour. Mix a primary with a secondary and you get a whole family of in-between shades. Understanding this simple system saves paint, saves money, and gives you far more control than owning dozens of ready-made tubes. It also explains why some mixes look bright and clean while others turn dull and muddy. This guide explains the system step by step, with practical habits you can use in your very next painting session.

## Colour Mixing Basics: The Three Primary Colours

The three primary colours are red, yellow, and blue, and they cannot be made by mixing other colours together. Everything else in painting starts from them. Yellow is the lightest and brightest of the three, red sits in the middle, and blue is usually the darkest and strongest. When you buy paints, choose one warm and one cool version of each primary if you can, because that gives you more mixing options later. Before you mix anything for a painting, try a simple test: paint a small swatch of each primary on paper and label it. This reference strip helps you remember exactly what each colour looks like on its own, which makes it easier to judge what happens when you start combining them.

## Mixing Secondary Colours

Secondary colours are made by mixing two primaries in roughly equal amounts. Red and yellow make orange. Yellow and blue make green. Blue and red make purple. The exact shade depends on the amounts you use: more yellow in an orange mix gives a lighter, warmer orange, while more red gives a deeper, darker one. Try mixing each pair on a practice sheet, changing the ratio a little each time, and label each swatch with what you mixed. You will quickly see that secondary colours are not single fixed colours but whole ranges. This is useful in real paintings, because a landscape rarely needs one flat green; it needs several greens, from light yellow-green leaves to deep blue-green shadows, and all of them come from the same two primaries.

## Warm Colours Versus Cool Colours

Colours are often grouped into warm and cool families, and this grouping affects the mood of a painting. Warm colours are reds, oranges, and yellows: they tend to feel energetic, bright, and close to the viewer. Cool colours are blues, greens, and purples: they tend to feel calm, distant, and quiet. A single colour can lean warm or cool depending on what it is mixed with. A yellow mixed with a little red feels warmer than a yellow mixed with a little blue. Painters use this deliberately: warm colours in the foreground and cool colours in the background can make a scene feel deep, even on a flat page. When you plan a painting, decide its mood first, then lean your mixes toward the warm or cool family that matches it.

## Tints, Shades, and Tones

Once you can mix a colour, the next skill is changing how light or dark it is. Adding white makes a tint, which is a lighter version of the colour, such as pink from red. Adding black makes a shade, which is a darker version, but black can easily dull a colour, so use it sparingly. A gentler way to darken is to add a tiny amount of the colour opposite it on the colour wheel, known as its complement, which darkens without turning the mix grey. Adding grey makes a tone, which is a softer, quieter version of the colour. Practise this with one colour at a time: make a row of swatches from lightest tint to darkest shade and label each step. This row becomes a ready-made palette you can reuse in paintings.

## Why Mixes Turn Muddy, and How to Avoid It

Muddy mixes are the most common frustration in colour mixing, and they usually have one of three causes. The first is mixing too many colours at once: three or more strong colours often cancel each other out into brown or grey. The second is using dirty water or a dirty brush, which drags old colour into a fresh mix. The third is over-mixing on the paper until the paint loses its freshness. To keep mixes clean, mix only two colours at a time, rinse your brush between mixes, and stop mixing as soon as the colour looks right. If a mix does go muddy, do not try to rescue it by adding more colours; start a fresh mix instead. Clean habits matter more than expensive paint for bright results.

## A Simple Mixing Chart to Practise With

A mixing chart is a practical record of what your paints actually do, and making one is one of the best exercises for a beginner. Draw a grid on a page, list your primaries along the top and down the side, and fill each square with the mix of that row and column. Label the squares as you go. You will discover useful surprises: some reds and blues make a rich purple, while others make a dull brown, depending on whether each paint leans warm or cool. Keep the finished chart beside you while you paint, and use it to predict mixes instead of guessing. Over time you will need it less, because the results will live in your memory, but it remains a handy reference whenever you try a new paint.

## FAQ

### What are the three primary colours in painting?

The three primary colours are red, yellow, and blue. They cannot be made by mixing other colours, and every other colour in painting is mixed from them in some combination. Starting with just these three gives you a surprisingly wide palette.

### How do I mix a bright green instead of a dull one?

Use a cool yellow and a cool blue, and mix only those two colours together. Adding red, or using a warm yellow or blue, pulls the green toward brown. Clean water and a clean brush also help, because leftover colour in the brush dulls a fresh mix quickly.

### Why does adding black sometimes ruin a colour?

Black is very strong and can flatten a colour into grey if you add too much. For a gentler darkening, add a tiny amount of the colour opposite it on the colour wheel, or add a darker version of a related colour. Add darkening colours a little at a time and test on scrap paper first.

### Do I need many paint tubes to paint well?

No. A small set built around the primaries, plus white, can mix a wide range of colours. Learning to mix well matters more than owning many tubes, and a mixing chart made from your own paints is more useful than a shelf of colours you have never tested.

## Conclusion

Colour mixing is a system, not a mystery: three primaries make secondaries, warm and cool families set the mood, and tints, shades, and tones give you light and dark versions of any colour. Mix two colours at a time, keep your brush clean, and record your results on a mixing chart. With those habits, you can mix almost any colour you need from a small set of paints.
