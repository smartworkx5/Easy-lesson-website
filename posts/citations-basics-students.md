When you use someone else's idea, words, or findings in your work, a citation is how you give credit. It is a short note that tells the reader where the material came from, so they can check it themselves. Citations are not decoration. They are the basic honesty system of academic writing.

Many students find citations confusing because every teacher and subject seems to want a slightly different format. The good news is that the underlying idea never changes: show what is yours, show what came from elsewhere, and give enough detail for the reader to find the source. Once you understand that, the formatting becomes a detail you can look up.

## When Do You Need to Cite?

Cite whenever you use material that is not your own original thinking. That includes direct quotes, paraphrased ideas, facts or figures you found in a source, and images or diagrams you did not create. If you learned it from a book, article, video, or website, and it is not common knowledge, cite it.

Common knowledge does not need a citation. That means facts most educated readers would already know, such as the fact that the seasons change through the year. But be careful: if you are unsure whether something counts as common knowledge, cite it. An unnecessary citation costs nothing; a missing one can cost marks or trust.

You also need to cite when you paraphrase, not only when you quote word for word. Changing a few words in someone else's sentence does not make the idea yours. Our guide to [paraphrasing without copying](/blog/paraphrasing-without-copying.html) explains how to rewrite ideas properly and still give credit.

## The Two Parts of a Citation

Most citation systems have two parts. The first is a short in-text marker inside your writing, usually the author's name and the year, or a number that points to a list. The second is a full entry in a reference list at the end of your work, with the title, publisher or website, and date.

The in-text marker tells the reader exactly which claim comes from which source. The reference list gives the full details so the reader can find it. Both parts matter. A reference list without in-text markers leaves the reader guessing which ideas came from where, and markers without a list leave them unable to check anything.

Different subjects favour different styles, and your teacher will usually name the one to use. Whatever the style, keep it consistent throughout the essay. Mixing styles looks careless and confuses the reader.

## How to Record Sources While You Research

The easiest way to make citations painless is to record sources as you go. Every time you read something useful, note the author, title, date, and page or web address, plus a short note about what you took from it. Do this immediately, while the source is open in front of you.

Students who skip this step pay for it later, hunting through browser history and books trying to remember where a fact came from. A simple notes document or index card per source is enough. Some students keep a running reference list from the first day of research and simply tidy it at the end.

This habit also protects you from accidental plagiarism. When every note is labelled with its source, you always know which ideas are yours and which came from elsewhere.

## Common Citation Mistakes

One common mistake is citing only direct quotes and forgetting paraphrases. Another is citing a source you did not actually read, because you saw it mentioned somewhere else. If you did not read it, do not cite it as if you did; cite the source you actually used.

A third mistake is a reference list that does not match the in-text markers: entries with no matching marker, or markers pointing to nothing. Before handing in your work, check each marker against the list, and each list entry against the markers. This five-minute check catches most errors.

## Citations and Your Own Voice

Some students worry that citing often makes their essay look unoriginal. The opposite is true. Citations show that you have read widely, understood the material, and built your argument on solid ground. Your own voice comes through in how you select, arrange, and respond to sources, not in pretending they do not exist.

Think of citations as joining a conversation. You acknowledge what others have said, then add your own point. An essay with honest citations and a clear argument is far stronger than one that hides its sources.

## FAQ

### Do I need to cite ideas I put in my own words?

Yes. Paraphrasing changes the wording, not the ownership of the idea. Whenever the idea came from a source, cite it, even if every word is your own. The citation tells the reader where the thinking came from, which is the honest thing to do.

### What happens if I forget a citation?

A missing citation can look like plagiarism, even when it was an accident, and it can cost marks. If you catch the mistake before handing in your work, add the citation. If you realise afterwards, tell your teacher honestly. Teachers are usually far more understanding about an admitted mistake than about one they discover themselves.

### How do I cite a source I found through another source?

The honest approach is to cite the source you actually read. If you cannot read the original, say so in your citation according to your teacher's preferred style. Never pretend you read a book or article you only saw mentioned somewhere else.

### Do I need to cite images and diagrams too?

Yes, if you did not create them. Credit the source beneath the image or in your reference list, following your teacher's style. Images, charts, and diagrams are other people's work just as much as written words are.

## Conclusion

Citations are simply honest bookkeeping for ideas. Record your sources as you research, mark every borrowed idea or quote, keep your reference list matched to your text, and use one style consistently. Do that, and citations stop being a chore and become proof of careful, trustworthy work.
