Reports from October 2026 describe school online safety programmes returning with fresh energy: free interactive sessions for students from primary through secondary level, plus whole-school assemblies, covering cyberbullying, privacy, social media, scams, digital footprints, artificial intelligence, and healthy technology use, with tip sheets and an anonymous reporting tool for students who need help.

The timing is right. Students today live much of their social and school life online, often before anyone has taught them the rules of that environment. They know how to post, message, and stream; they are less sure how to handle a cruel comment, a suspicious message, a privacy setting, or a stranger asking questions. A programme that teaches these skills directly, in school time, closes a gap that homework and hope never will.

## Cyberbullying: what to do, not just what to avoid

The most valuable part of these sessions is practical: what to do when something goes wrong. Students learn to stop engaging, save evidence with a screenshot, block the account, and tell a trusted adult — in that order. They also learn the bystander skill that matters most: supporting the person targeted, privately if speaking up publicly feels unsafe, rather than joining in or silently watching.

Schools reinforce this with anonymous reporting tools, so a student who fears retaliation can still ask for help. The message worth repeating at home is simple: reporting is not telling tales, it is how the community protects itself. Every student should know the reporting route in their school before they ever need it.

## Privacy and digital footprints

Digital footprint is the idea students grasp fastest once it is explained well: everything posted, liked, or shared leaves a trace that can resurface years later, in contexts the poster never imagined. Sessions teach students to check privacy settings, think before sharing photos or locations, and understand that even deleted posts may have been copied.

A useful home exercise is a privacy audit together: go through one app's settings, turn off location sharing for posts, review who can see what, and remove old posts that no longer represent the student. Done calmly and without judgement, this teaches control rather than fear, and students usually enjoy discovering settings they never knew existed.

## Scams, AI, and healthy tech use

Modern safety programmes now include scams and AI, because both target young users. Students learn the signs of a scam message: urgency, a prize or threat, a request for personal details or money, and a link from an unknown sender. The rule is easy to remember: stop, check with an adult, never click in a hurry. AI enters the picture through deepfakes, fake images, and chatbots that sound human — students learn to ask whether what they see is real, and to verify surprising claims before sharing them.

Healthy technology use rounds out the programme: balanced screen time, keeping devices out of bedrooms at night, and noticing when scrolling replaces sleep, homework, or time with friends. Framed as self-care rather than punishment, these habits stick far better.

## Why short interactive sessions work

The relaunched programmes use short, interactive sessions rather than long lectures, and the format is the point. A 45-minute session with scenarios, discussion, and role-play lets students practise responses instead of memorising rules. A student who has rehearsed what to say to a scam message or a cruel comment is far more likely to act well when it happens for real.

Parents can extend the session at home with one question a week: what would you do if someone you do not know messaged you asking for a photo? What if a friend shared an embarrassing picture of someone else? These conversations, kept short and calm, build judgement over time without turning the home into a second classroom.

## A shared responsibility

Online safety works best when school, home, and student pull in the same direction. Schools provide the teaching and the reporting routes. Parents provide the conversation and the boundaries. Students provide the honesty to say when something has gone wrong. No single part works alone, and blame helps nobody — the goal is a student who knows how to stay safe and who trusts an adult enough to ask for help.

For study life specifically, digital literacy also means academic honesty: knowing the difference between using AI to understand and copying AI output as your own work, and checking AI answers against the textbook rather than trusting them blindly. The same critical thinking protects both safety and learning.

## FAQ

### What topics do school online safety programmes cover?

Cyberbullying, privacy, social media, scams, digital footprints, artificial intelligence, and healthy technology use. Good programmes use short interactive sessions with scenarios and discussion, plus tip sheets and an anonymous reporting tool so students can ask for help safely.

### What should a student do about cyberbullying?

Stop engaging, save evidence with a screenshot, block the account, and tell a trusted adult. Bystanders can help by supporting the person targeted privately if speaking up publicly feels unsafe, rather than joining in or ignoring it.

### How can families check privacy settings together?

Do a calm privacy audit of one app at a time: turn off location sharing for posts, review who can see what, and remove old posts that no longer represent the student. Done without judgement, this teaches control and confidence rather than fear.
