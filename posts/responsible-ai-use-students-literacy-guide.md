Alongside new grading tools showcased in October 2026, an AI literacy programme aimed to teach students how to use AI responsibly. That pairing is exactly right: every new AI capability needs a matching literacy — the judgement to use it well. Responsible AI use is not a list of bans. It is a set of habits that protect learning, honesty, and privacy at the same time.

The core idea is simple: AI should make you a better learner, not a better-looking one. If your grades improve while your understanding stays flat, something has gone wrong. If your understanding grows because AI explained, quizzed, and challenged you, something has gone right — even if the grade takes a little longer to follow.

## Check answers, never just accept them

The first habit is checking. AI tools can state wrong facts confidently, mix up similar concepts, and invent plausible details. A responsible student treats every AI answer as a claim to verify, not a fact to copy. Check against the textbook first, because the textbook is the authority for your class. Check against class notes second. Ask a teacher when sources disagree.

Checking is also a learning technique in disguise. When you compare an AI explanation with the textbook and find a difference, you have to decide which is right — and deciding forces you to understand the idea properly. Students who check regularly end up understanding more deeply than students who never needed to check, because they practised judgement on every answer.

## Do not copy: the honesty rule

The second habit is honesty about authorship. Using AI to understand a topic, brainstorm ideas, or review your own draft is help. Submitting AI-generated text, answers, or solutions as your own work is copying, even if you changed a few words. The test is straightforward: if you removed the AI, could you still produce and defend the work? If not, it is not yours yet.

This rule protects you, not just the system. Copied work creates a gap between your record and your knowledge, and that gap eventually shows — in an oral question, an in-class task, or a later topic that builds on the one you skipped. Honest work builds a record you can stand behind, which is worth far more than one easy grade.

## Protect your privacy

The third habit is privacy. AI tools learn from what you type, so never share personal information: your full name, address, school details, passwords, photos of yourself, or private conversations. Use school-approved tools where possible, read the basic privacy settings, and remember that anything pasted into a public tool may be stored beyond your control.

Privacy also means respecting others: do not paste someone else's work, photos, or personal information into an AI tool, and do not use AI to create misleading images or messages about other people. Responsible use treats other people's privacy the same as your own.

## Use AI without undermining learning

The fourth habit is the most subtle: use AI in ways that keep the learning with you. Ask the AI to explain, then close it and explain the idea yourself. Ask it to quiz you, not to answer for you. Ask it to review your draft and point out weak parts, then revise those parts yourself. Each of these keeps you as the thinker and the AI as the assistant.

Watch for warning signs. If you open the AI before reading the question, you are skipping thinking. If you cannot explain your submitted answer, you did not learn it. If you feel uneasy submitting the work, that unease is information — listen to it, and redo the part you cannot defend. Good habits feel slower at first and faster later, because real understanding compounds.

## A literacy checklist for every AI session

Before using AI, ask: what am I trying to learn, and what will I do myself? During use, ask: does this match the textbook, and can I explain it in my own words? After use, ask: what did I produce myself, and would I be comfortable showing my teacher how I used the tool? If the answers are honest, the session was responsible.

Schools and families can reinforce this checklist by rewarding the process: praise a student who found and corrected an AI error, who asked a sharp follow-up question, or who admitted a gap and fixed it. When honesty is rewarded more than polish, responsible use becomes the easy choice, not the hard one.

## FAQ

### What does responsible AI use mean for students?

It means checking AI answers against the textbook, not submitting AI output as your own work, protecting your privacy and others', and using AI in ways that keep the learning with you — explaining, quizzing, and reviewing, rather than replacing your own thinking.

### How can students tell whether their AI use is honest?

Ask whether you could produce and defend the work without the AI. If you can explain every answer in your own words and would be comfortable showing your teacher how you used the tool, your use was honest. If not, redo the part you cannot defend.

### What personal information should students never share with AI tools?

Full name, address, school details, passwords, photos of yourself, private conversations, and anyone else's personal information or work. Use school-approved tools where possible and remember that pasted content may be stored beyond your control.

## Conclusion

Responsible AI use comes down to four habits: check every answer, never copy, protect privacy, and keep the thinking with yourself. Students who build these habits now will use every future AI tool well, because the tools will change but the judgement will not.
