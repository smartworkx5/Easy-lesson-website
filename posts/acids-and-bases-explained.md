Acids and bases explained in plain words come down to a simple scale and a few everyday examples. Acids taste sour and can sting, bases feel slippery and taste bitter, and the pH scale tells you how strong each one is. Lemon juice and vinegar are acids. Soap and baking soda solution are bases. Pure water sits in the middle. Once you understand the pH scale, you can sort almost any common substance into its place.

## What Is an Acid?

An acid is a substance that tastes sour and can react with many materials. Common acids include the citric acid in lemons and oranges, the acetic acid in vinegar, and the acid in fizzy drinks that gives them their sharp taste. Strong acids, such as the acid in a car battery, are dangerous and can burn skin and damage materials. Weak acids, such as vinegar, are safe in small amounts and are used in cooking and cleaning.

In chemistry, acids are often described as substances that release hydrogen ions when dissolved in water. You do not need to memorise that definition for a basic answer, but it helps to know that the sour taste and the stinging feeling come from the same chemical behaviour. When a question asks for an example of an acid, a safe everyday answer is lemon juice, vinegar, or a fizzy drink.

## What Is a Base?

A base, sometimes called an alkali when it dissolves in water, is the chemical opposite of an acid. Bases taste bitter and feel slippery or soapy between your fingers. Common bases include soap, baking soda solution, toothpaste, and many cleaning products. Just like acids, bases can be weak or strong. Weak bases, such as baking soda solution, are safe in everyday use. Strong bases, such as oven cleaner, are dangerous and must be handled with care.

Bases are useful because they neutralise acids. When an acid and a base meet, they react and cancel out each other's strongest properties, producing a salt and water. This reaction is called neutralisation. It is why a base such as baking soda can calm an acid, and why antacid tablets, which contain a base, can settle excess stomach acid.

## The pH Scale from 0 to 14

The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a substance is. It runs from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral, meaning the substance is neither acidic nor basic. Pure water has a pH of about 7. Numbers below 7 mean acidic, and the lower the number, the stronger the acid. Numbers above 7 mean basic, and the higher the number, the stronger the base.

The scale is not like an ordinary ruler. Each step on the pH scale represents a tenfold change in strength. That means a substance with pH 3 is ten times more acidic than one with pH 4, and a hundred times more acidic than one with pH 5. This is why small differences in pH can matter a great deal. For everyday questions, though, you mainly need to remember: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is basic.

## Everyday Examples on the pH Scale

Everyday substances spread across the whole pH scale. Near the acidic end are stomach acid and battery acid, which are very strong acids. Lemon juice and vinegar sit in the mildly acidic range. Fizzy drinks and fruit juices are also acidic, which is why they taste sharp. In the middle, near pH 7, are pure water, milk, and blood, which is kept close to neutral by the body. Toward the basic end are baking soda solution, soap, and cleaning products.

You can test pH with indicator paper or a liquid indicator. Indicator paper changes colour depending on the pH of the substance it touches. Red colours mean acidic, green means neutral, and blue or purple means basic. A more precise tool is a pH meter, which gives a number reading. In class experiments, indicator paper is the usual tool, and matching the colour to a chart is how you read the pH.

## Neutralisation: When Acids Meet Bases

When an acid and a base react, they neutralise each other. The products of neutralisation are usually a salt and water. A salt in chemistry is not only table salt; it is the name for a whole family of compounds formed when an acid reacts with a base. The exact salt formed depends on which acid and base are used.

Neutralisation has many everyday uses. Farmers add lime, a base, to soil that is too acidic so that crops grow better. Antacid tablets neutralise excess acid in the stomach. Baking soda can neutralise an acid spill. In each case, the goal is the same: bring the pH closer to neutral. If a question asks what happens when an acid and a base mix, the answer is neutralisation, producing a salt and water.
