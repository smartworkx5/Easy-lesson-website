Most students practise problems the way they eat snacks: whenever they feel like it, in whatever order appears. The result is the same in both cases. You feel busy, but nothing much changes. A practice routine fixes this by giving your problem-solving a structure: what you practise, when you practise it, and how you review your mistakes.

This guide gives you a weekly routine built on four parts: mixed-topic sets, timed sprints, error-log review, and spaced re-attempts. It works for maths, science, languages, and any subject with problems to solve.

## Mixed-Topic Sets: Train Like a Real Test

Real tests never serve problems in neat chapters. A test might follow a fractions question with a geometry question and then a word problem. Yet most students practise one chapter at a time, which builds a fragile skill that breaks when the order changes.

Mixed-topic sets fix this. Build each practice set from three or four different topics you have already covered. The shuffle forces your brain to do the most important step: identify the problem type before solving. That identification step is exactly what exams test, and it only improves when you practise it. Aim for sets of eight to twelve problems, and resist the urge to group by type. The discomfort you feel when switching topics is the feeling of the skill growing.

## Timed Sprints

Untimed practice teaches you to solve. Timed practice teaches you to solve under pressure, which is a different skill. A timed sprint is short, intense, and honest: pick five problems, set a timer for twenty minutes, and work without stopping.

Sprints do two things. First, they reveal which steps eat your time. Maybe you solve fine but spend five minutes choosing a method. That is useful information, not failure. Second, they build exam calm. The tenth time you watch a timer tick down, it stops feeling like an emergency and starts feeling like background noise. Do one or two sprints per week. Track your scores and watch them climb as your methods get faster and your identification gets sharper.

## The Error Log

Your mistakes are your best teachers, but only if you collect them. An error log is a simple notebook, or a document, where you record every problem you get wrong, plus three things: what you did, what the correct move was, and what kind of error it was.

Error kinds matter more than individual errors. Common kinds include misreading the question, choosing the wrong method, arithmetic slips, and stopping one step too early. When you see that half your errors are the same kind, you have found your real weakness, and fixing one weakness improves every future set. This is far more efficient than redoing random problems. Ten minutes of error-log review beats an hour of blind repetition.

## Spaced Re-Attempts

Redoing a problem the next day feels productive, but you are mostly remembering the solution, not rebuilding it. Spacing fixes this. Re-attempt old problems after three days, then after a week, then after two weeks. Each gap forces genuine reconstruction, which is what builds lasting skill.

Pull your re-attempts straight from the error log. Old errors are the perfect re-attempt material because they mark exactly where your understanding was weak. If you solve it correctly on the second attempt, the gap is closed. If you fail again, you have discovered a deeper misunderstanding worth investigating properly. This cycle, fail, review, space, re-attempt, is the engine of steady progress. It is slow in week one and powerful by week four.

## A Sample Week

Here is how the four parts fit together. Monday: a mixed-topic set of ten problems, forty minutes. Wednesday: a timed sprint, five problems in twenty minutes. Friday: error-log review, fifteen minutes of studying your mistakes and naming the error kinds. Sunday: spaced re-attempts of problems from two weeks ago, thirty minutes.

That is under two hours a week of structured practice, on top of your normal homework. Keep it for a month before judging it. The routine works because every part feeds the next: sprints generate errors, the log organises them, and spaced re-attempts close them. ## FAQ

### How many problems should I do per week?

Quality beats quantity. Ten mixed problems, one sprint, and a proper error-log review, roughly twenty to thirty problems total, done consistently, outperforms a hundred rushed ones. Add more only when the routine feels easy.

### What if I keep failing the same re-attempts?

That is a signal, not a verdict. It means the concept underneath has a gap, not just the problem. Go back to the textbook explanation, or photograph the problem for a step-by-step AI walkthrough, and rebuild the concept before re-attempting again.

### Can this routine work for language subjects?

Yes. Mixed sets become mixed exercises: vocabulary, grammar, and reading in one session. Sprints work for timed writing. The error log records recurring grammar slips, and spaced re-attempts review old exercises. The structure is the same.

### How long before I see results?

Most students feel more organised within two weeks and see measurable improvement, fewer repeated errors and faster sprints, within four to six weeks. The key is not missing the Friday error-log review. That is where the learning compounds.

## Putting It Together

Steady progress comes from structure, not from more hours. Mix your topics so identification gets trained. Sprint against the timer so pressure stops scaring you. Log every error and name its kind. Re-attempt old problems on a spaced schedule until the gaps close. Two hours a week, done consistently, will transform your problem-solving within a month. When a problem still resists, photograph it and get a clear step-by-step breakdown, then verify each step against your textbook.
