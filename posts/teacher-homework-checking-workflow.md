Checking homework is one of those jobs that quietly eats whole evenings. A single class set can take an hour or more, and when there are several classes, the pile never seems to shrink. The problem is rarely effort. Most teachers check homework the slow way: opening each paper at the top, reading every line, and writing a long comment at the end. A better workflow changes the order of the work so the same care takes less time.

This workflow has three ideas at its heart: sort before you read, check in passes instead of all at once, and write feedback students will actually use. None of it requires new tools or extra preparation. It is a way of organising the checking you already do so that your evenings get shorter and your feedback gets better.

## Why Homework Checking Eats Evenings

Homework checking takes so long because it mixes several jobs into one. When you read a single paper from top to bottom, you are simultaneously checking answers, judging method, hunting for errors, deciding on a mark, and composing a comment. Switching between those jobs on every paper is tiring, and it slows you down far more than the reading itself.

Long comments add to the problem. A paragraph at the end of a paper feels thorough, but students often read only the first line, or only the grade. The effort that went into the rest of the comment buys almost nothing. Short feedback aimed at one clear next step is both faster to write and more likely to be used.

The fix is to separate the jobs. Check answers in one pass, look at methods in another, and write feedback last, when you already know what the paper needs. Each pass is faster than the combined job, because your brain is doing one thing at a time. The total time drops, and the quality of each pass goes up.

## Sort First: What Needs Your Eyes

Before you read a single answer, sort the pile. The fastest sort is into three groups: complete and looks correct, complete with visible problems, and incomplete or missing. You can do this sort by flipping through the papers quickly, without reading them in detail. It usually takes a few minutes, and it changes everything that follows.

The first group needs only a light check. Spot-check a few answers, confirm the method is sound, and move on. These papers do not need a paragraph of feedback; a tick and a short note are enough. The second group is where your real checking time goes: these students tried, but something went wrong, and finding the pattern matters. The third group needs a different response altogether — a conversation, not marking — because the issue is completion, not correctness.

Sorting also protects your energy. Checking the strongest papers first builds momentum and reminds you what good work looks like. Checking the hardest papers while you are fresh means they get your best attention. Saving the quick wins for the end, when you are tired, is the slowest way to do it. ## A Three-Pass Checking Workflow

The core of this workflow is three passes over the same set of papers, each with a single job.

Pass one is answers only. Work through the pile with your answer guide and mark each answer right or wrong. Do not stop to study methods, and do not write comments yet. This pass is fast because it has one decision per question. By the end of it, you know the overall picture: which questions most students got right, and which ones caused trouble.

Pass two is methods, and only for the papers that need it. Look at the papers where answers were wrong and ask one question: where did the method break down? Was it a misread question, a skipped step, a wrong method, or a small slip? You are not re-marking the paper. You are diagnosing it. Most errors fall into a few patterns, and once you see the pattern, the feedback almost writes itself.

Pass three is feedback, and it is the shortest pass. For each paper that needs a comment, write one short, specific note pointing to the next step. "Show the second step — that is where the answer changed" is more useful than a paragraph. Students can act on one clear instruction. They cannot act on five.

## Use Answer Guides Without Copying Them

A good answer guide is the single biggest time-saver in checking. Before you mark, write or draft a guide for the homework: the correct answer, the key steps, and the partial-credit points for each question. With a guide beside you, pass one becomes a quick comparison instead of a fresh round of problem-solving.

If you use an AI tool to draft an answer guide, treat it the same way you would treat any draft: check every solution yourself before you use it. A wrong answer in the guide is worse than no guide at all, because it makes every paper look wrong in the same way. Solve the questions yourself first, then use the draft guide to catch anything you missed.

Keep your guides. A guide for a homework set you give every year is an investment that pays off each time you reuse the task. Even a rough guide with just answers and key steps is worth keeping. Next year, you start from the guide instead of from a blank page.

## Give Short Feedback That Students Read

Feedback works when students read it and act on it. That means short, specific, and forward-looking. A useful feedback note has three qualities: it names what was done well, names the one thing to fix, and says what to do next. "Good method on questions 1 to 3. In question 4, check the second step. Redo it and bring it tomorrow." That is a complete feedback note.

Avoid writing the same long comment on many papers. If half the class made the same error, the fix belongs in the next lesson, not in twenty identical comments. Write the pattern on the board, work through one example together, and give students a chance to correct their own work. The lesson does the job that twenty comments could not.

Also, separate feedback from grades where you can. When a grade sits next to a comment, students read the grade and stop. If the homework is for practice rather than assessment, consider giving feedback first and the grade later, or feedback alone. Students who read feedback without a grade in the way are more likely to use it.

## Track Patterns Instead of Every Error

You do not need a detailed record of every error on every paper. You need a record of patterns. Keep a simple tally while you check: a short list of the questions or skills that caused the most trouble, and a rough sense of how many students struggled with each. This takes seconds per paper and gives you a clear picture of what the next lesson should cover.

That tally is also the fairest basis for deciding what to reteach. If three students missed a question, it is an individual issue. If most of the class missed it, the question or the teaching needs another look. Without a tally, it is easy to spend the next lesson reteaching the wrong thing, or to miss a widespread misunderstanding because the papers were checked one by one.

Over a term, these tallies become a useful record of your class. You can see which topics keep causing trouble, which errors fade after reteaching, and where a homework set needs redesigning. That is the kind of record that makes next year's planning easier too.

## Conclusion

Checking homework faster is not about rushing. It is about sorting before you read, checking in three passes with one job each, and writing one short, usable note per paper. Add a checked answer guide and a simple tally of patterns, and the same pile takes less of your evening while giving students feedback they can actually use.

## FAQ

### How long should checking one class set take?

It depends on the length of the homework, but a three-pass workflow with an answer guide is usually much faster than reading each paper top to bottom. The biggest savings come from sorting first and writing short feedback instead of long comments.

### Should I write comments on every paper?

No. Papers that are complete and correct need only a brief acknowledgment. Save detailed comments for papers where a specific next step will help. If many students made the same error, address it in the next lesson instead of repeating the comment.

### What do I do with incomplete homework?

Treat it as a completion issue, not a marking issue. A short conversation about what got in the way is more useful than marking a half-finished paper. If incomplete homework is a pattern, look for the cause: too much work, unclear instructions, or a topic that needs more support.

### How do I keep feedback consistent across a large pile?

Use an answer guide with key steps and partial-credit points, and reuse short feedback phrases for common patterns. Checking in passes also helps, because you compare all papers against the same standard in each pass instead of resetting your judgment on every paper.
