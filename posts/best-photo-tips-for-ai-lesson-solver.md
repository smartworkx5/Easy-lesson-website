A blurry, tilted, or incomplete photo leads to guesses, missing steps, and confusion. The good news is that great photos come from a few simple habits, not from expensive equipment. ## Rule One: Use Bright, Even Light

Light is the foundation of a good photo. Work near a window in daylight, or under a bright lamp in the evening. The goal is even light across the whole page, with no dark corners and no bright glare. Place the lamp to the side so your body and phone do not cast a shadow over the page.

Avoid flash when you can. Flash often creates a bright spot and makes nearby text hard to read. If the page is glossy, tilt it slightly until the shine disappears. If the room is dim, move closer to the light rather than relying on flash. A well-lit page looks ordinary to your eyes, but it makes a large difference to the tool.

## Rule Two: Keep the Page Flat and the Phone Level

A flat page and a level phone prevent distortion. Place the book or paper on a flat desk, open it fully, and smooth the page gently. Hold your phone directly above the page, with the screen parallel to the paper. This keeps lines straight and symbols in their true shape.

If the book will not stay open, hold the edges down lightly away from the question, or place a light object on the margins. Avoid photographing from the side, because side angles stretch the text and shrink one edge. A straight, level photo is far easier to read than a tilted one, even if the tilted one looks acceptable at a glance.

## Rule Three: Frame the Full Question

Include the complete question in every photo. That means the instruction, the numbers or passage, any diagram, table, labels, and units. If the question continues on the next line, include that line too. A missing instruction or unit can change the whole meaning of a question.

Leave a small margin around the edges so nothing is cut off. At the same time, avoid including unrelated pages, clutter, or notes that might confuse the reading. Fill the frame with the question you want help with. If the question is long, take two photos: one of the full question for context, and one closer photo of the hardest part.

## Rule Four: Focus on the Text Before You Shoot

Focus is easy to forget and easy to fix. Before you take the photo, tap the screen on the most important line of the question. Wait for the image to sharpen, then take the photo. If your hands shake, rest your elbows on the desk and hold the phone with both hands. Hold your breath for a second as you tap the shutter.

After you take the photo, zoom into the preview and read the question from the image itself. If any word looks soft or blurred, retake the photo. A few seconds of focus checking saves a confusing answer later. Sharp text is the single clearest sign of a photo that will produce good help.

## Rule Five: Photograph Diagrams and Tables with Extra Care

Diagrams and tables carry information that words alone cannot. Photograph them with the same care as the text. Include the title, all labels, any caption, arrows, and scale markers. Check that no label is hidden in a fold, shadow, or glare.

If the diagram is detailed, take a closer second photo of the labels. If the table is wide, make sure every row and column is visible. Diagrams that look fine at a glance often hide a blurry label when you zoom in. A quick zoom check catches that problem before you send the photo. AI can make mistakes, so always verify solutions against your textbook.

## Rule Six: Keep Handwriting Neat and Spaced

If you photograph handwritten work, neatness matters as much as lighting. Write with clear, consistent letter shapes, steady size, and clear gaps between words. Keep lines straight and leave space between lines. Avoid heavy crossing out, and rewrite any word that is hard to read.

If your working is crowded or messy, rewrite the original question neatly on a clean page and photograph that version. The act of rewriting often helps you understand the question better, and the clean photo produces a cleaner explanation. ## Rule Seven: Check, Send, and Review the Answer

The final rule is to check before and after. Before you send the photo, ask: Is every word sharp? Is the full question visible? Is the diagram complete? Is the photo the right way up? If the answer to any question is no, retake the photo.

After you receive the explanation, review it with the same care. Read each step, compare it with your textbook, and try the question yourself without looking. If a step looks wrong, check whether the photo included the full question and whether any symbol was misread. Good photos plus careful review produce the most accurate and useful help.

## FAQ

### What is the most common photo mistake?

The most common mistake is sending a photo that is blurry, shadowed, or cut off. Use bright, even light, hold the phone steady above the page, and zoom into the preview to check every word before you send it.

### Should I use flash for lesson photos?

Avoid flash when possible, because it often creates glare. Use daylight or a bright lamp placed to the side. If the room is dark, move closer to the light source instead of using flash.

### How do I photograph a question that spans two pages?

Photograph both pages. Take one photo of each page, making sure any continued line or diagram is fully visible. Start with the page that holds the main question and the instruction.

### What if my photo is clear but the answer still looks wrong?

Compare each step with your textbook and check whether the photo included the full question. Rework the question yourself, and if the difference remains, ask your teacher about the step where the methods split.
