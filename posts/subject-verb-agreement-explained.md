Subject-verb agreement is one of those grammar rules that sounds simple and causes endless trouble. The rule is short: a singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb. The trouble is that real sentences hide the subject behind extra phrases, tricky word pairs, and either-or constructions.

This guide gives you simple rules with clear examples, so you can get agreement right every time.

## The One Rule That Matters

Strip the sentence down and the rule is easy. The cat sleeps. The cats sleep. One subject, one verb, and they match. Problems start when extra words sit between the subject and the verb, because your eye matches the verb to the nearest noun instead of the real subject.

The core technique is isolation. Find the verb, then ask who or what performs it. Everything between the subject and the verb that is not the subject, especially phrases starting with of, in, with, or along with, is decoration. Ignore the decoration. One of the boys is here, not are, because the subject is one, not boys. Master isolation and most agreement problems disappear.

## Tricky Subjects

Some subjects look plural but are singular, and some look singular but are plural. Each, every, everyone, somebody, and nobody are singular. Each of the students is ready. Everybody has finished. Your ear may expect are and have, but the grammar says is and has.

Collective nouns like team, class, and family are usually singular when they act as one unit. The team is winning. Treat them as singular unless the sentence clearly means the members acting separately. Indefinite amounts like news, mathematics, and physics are singular too. The news is surprising. When in doubt, ask whether the subject names one thing or many. One thing means a singular verb.

## Either-Or and Neither-Nor

Sentences with either-or and neither-nor follow a proximity rule: the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it. Either the teacher or the students are responsible. Neither the students nor the teacher is responsible. The nearest subject decides.

This rule surprises students, but it is mechanical once you know it. Find the or or nor, look at the subject right before the verb, and match the verb to that one. Either my friends or my brother is coming. The verb matches brother because brother stands closest. Do not let the first subject distract you. Only the nearest one counts.

## Phrases That Mislead

The biggest trap is the phrase between subject and verb. Along with, as well as, in addition to, and together with never change the number of the subject. The captain, along with the players, was praised. The subject is captain, singular, so the verb is singular.

Read the sentence backwards to find the real subject: reasons is plural, so are is correct. Training yourself to find the true subject, not the nearest noun, is the single most valuable agreement skill. When you proofread, circle each subject and draw a line to its verb. Mismatches become visible instantly.

## FAQ

### How do I find the real subject in a long sentence?

Find the verb first, then ask who or what performs it. Cross out phrases starting with of, with, along with, and as well as. What remains is usually the true subject.

### Are collective nouns singular or plural?

Usually singular, because they name one group acting as a unit. The committee has decided. Only treat them as plural when the sentence clearly shows members acting individually, which is rare in student writing.

### What about either-or with one singular and one plural subject?

Use the proximity rule. Match the verb to whichever subject stands nearest to it. Either the players or the coach is speaking. Neither the coach nor the players are speaking.

### Does this rule matter in exams?

Yes. Agreement errors are among the most penalised mistakes in written answers because they are easy to spot. Clean agreement makes your writing look controlled and confident in every subject.

## Putting It Together

Agreement is simple once you see through the disguises. Isolate the true subject, remember that each and everyone are singular, match the nearest subject after either-or, and ignore misleading phrases between subject and verb. Practise the circle-the-subject proofreading trick on your next essay and watch the errors vanish.
