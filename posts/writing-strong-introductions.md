The introduction is the first thing your reader meets, and it decides whether they read on with interest or with effort. A strong introduction does three things in a short space: it catches attention, gives enough background to understand the topic, and ends with a clear thesis statement. That is all. Everything else belongs in the body.

Many students overwrite introductions, stuffing them with background, definitions, and early arguments. Others underwrite them, jumping straight to the thesis with no setup at all. Both mistakes come from the same confusion: not knowing what an introduction is for. Think of it as opening a door, not furnishing the whole house.

## Move One: The Hook

The hook is the opening sentence or two, designed to make the reader want to continue. Good hooks connect to the reader's experience or curiosity. A surprising observation, a vivid everyday example, a short question, or a brief scenario can all work.

A hook about reading might start with a familiar scene: a student staring at the same page for twenty minutes, having read nothing. That image works because the reader recognises it. What does not work is a grand, empty opening like since the beginning of time, people have studied. Readers skip past openings that could belong to any essay on any topic.

Keep the hook short and honest. One or two sentences is enough. Its only job is to earn the next sentence, not to impress the reader with vocabulary.

## Move Two: Background, Briefly

After the hook, give just enough background for the thesis to make sense. Define a key term if the reader needs it, sketch the situation in a sentence or two, or explain why the topic matters. The test is simple: could a reader who knows nothing about the topic understand your thesis after this background? If yes, stop. If no, add one more sentence.

The most common introduction mistake lives here: the background that never ends. Students write three paragraphs of general information before reaching their claim, and by then the reader has lost the thread. Save the detail for the body, where each point gets its own paragraph and evidence. The introduction only needs the runway, not the whole journey.

## Move Three: The Thesis Statement

The introduction ends with the thesis statement: one clear claim that the essay will support. Placing it at the end of the introduction is a kindness to the reader, because it tells them exactly where the essay is going before the evidence begins.

A thesis that appears here should be the polished version of your claim, not a vague promise. Compare this essay will discuss study methods with spaced practice improves long-term recall more than rereading notes does. The second version gives the reader something to test, and gives you, the writer, a filter for every paragraph that follows. Our guide on [writing a strong thesis statement](/blog/thesis-statement-strong-essay.html) walks through building one step by step.

## Matching the Introduction to the Essay

An introduction should match the essay it introduces, in tone and in scope. A light, chatty hook followed by a serious, formal argument feels off. A huge, sweeping claim in the introduction followed by a narrow essay feels dishonest. Read the introduction and the conclusion together when you revise: they should feel like the same essay, with the conclusion showing what the introduction promised.

Length matters too. For a typical school essay, one paragraph is usually right. Longer essays can take two, especially when the topic needs real background. If your introduction is longer than your shortest body paragraph, it is probably doing too much.

## Openings to Avoid

A few openings weaken almost every essay they appear in. The dictionary definition opening, according to the dictionary, is tired and tells the reader nothing about your argument. The apology opening, I am not an expert but, undermines you before you begin. The sweeping history opening, throughout history, people have always, is too broad to be useful.

Also avoid announcing your essay: in this essay I will discuss. Just discuss it. The reader knows they are reading an essay; they do not need a tour guide announcing each section. Strong writing shows its structure through clear ideas, not through announcements.

## Writing the Introduction Last

Here is a practical secret: many experienced writers draft the introduction last. It is hard to introduce an argument you have not fully worked out yet, and easy to introduce one you have already written. If you struggle with openings, write the body first, then return and write an introduction that fits the essay you actually produced.

This is not cheating or laziness. It is good process. The reader sees the finished order, not the order you wrote in, and an introduction written last is usually sharper, shorter, and more honest about what the essay really argues.

## FAQ

### How long should an introduction be?

For a typical school essay, one paragraph is usually right. Longer essays may need two paragraphs when the topic requires background. A good rule: the introduction should be shorter than any body paragraph that carries real evidence.

### Can I start with a question?

Yes, a genuine question that connects to the topic can be a good hook. Avoid questions with obvious answers or questions you never answer in the essay. The question should lead naturally into your background and thesis, not sit apart from them.

### Should the thesis statement always be the last sentence?

Usually yes, because it gives the reader a clean handover into the body. In very short essays the thesis can appear earlier, but the end of the introduction is the standard and safest place. Readers expect it there.

### What if I cannot think of a hook?

Start with the background and thesis, write the body, and come back to the hook later. Often the essay itself suggests the best opening once it exists. A clear, simple opening beats a forced clever one every time.

## Conclusion

A strong introduction needs only three moves: a hook that earns attention, brief background that makes the thesis understandable, and a clear thesis statement at the end. Keep it short, match it to the essay, and do not be afraid to write it last. Get the opening right, and the reader will follow you gladly into the rest.
