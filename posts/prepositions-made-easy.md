Prepositions are small words that cause big headaches. In, on, and at seem interchangeable until you use the wrong one and the sentence sounds wrong. The usual advice, just memorise the rules, does not work well because there are too many rules and too many exceptions.

There is a better approach. Learn prepositions as patterns and chunks, not as rules. This guide shows you the core patterns for time and place, the fixed phrases worth learning whole, and a notebook method that makes prepositions stick.

## In, On, At for Time

Think of time as a ladder from large to small. Use in for the largest units: in 2026, in October, in the morning. Use on for medium units, days and dates: on Monday, on October 20, on my birthday. Use at for the smallest units, precise times: at 7 o clock, at noon, at midnight.

This one ladder covers most time prepositions students need. When you hesitate, ask how big the time unit is. Year or month means in. Day or date means on. Exact clock time means at. There are exceptions, but far fewer than students fear, and the ladder gets you right the large majority of the time. Practise by sorting times into the three groups until the ladder feels automatic.

## In, On, At for Place

Place works like time, with a ladder from large to small. Use in for enclosed or large areas: in the room, in the city, in the bag. Use on for surfaces and lines: on the table, on the wall, on the bus. Use at for precise points: at the door, at the bus stop, at home.

Again, ask how big the place is. Inside something or a large area means in. A surface you can touch means on. An exact spot means at. This ladder explains why you are in a car but on a bus: a bus is treated as a public surface you board, while a car encloses you. Do not memorise that fact. Memorise the ladder and let it generate the answers.

## Fixed Phrases: Learn by Chunk

Some prepositions cannot be derived from any ladder. They are simply fixed phrases that the language has settled on: interested in, good at, afraid of, depend on, arrive at, listen to. Trying to reason out why it is interested in but good at wastes time. There is no why. It is convention.

Learn these as whole chunks, never as single words. Do not learn depend. Learn depend on. Do not learn interested. Learn interested in. When you meet a new one in reading, copy the whole phrase into your notebook with an example sentence. Over months, your chunk collection grows into natural-sounding English, because you are learning the language the way it is actually used.

## The Preposition Notebook Method

Dedicate a notebook section to prepositions with three columns: the chunk, an example sentence, and the pattern it follows. Every time a preposition surprises you in reading or in a corrected exercise, add it. Review the notebook weekly, reading each chunk aloud with its sentence.

The reading aloud matters. Prepositions live in your ear as much as in your head. When you have heard interested in a hundred times, interested at will sound wrong without any rule needed. This is how native speakers do it, by feel built from exposure. Your notebook accelerates that exposure by concentrating it. Ten minutes a week keeps the collection growing and your feel sharpening.

## FAQ

### Why are prepositions so hard?

Because they are mostly convention, not logic. Ladders cover time and place well, but fixed phrases must be learned as chunks. Accepting this saves you from hunting for rules that do not exist.

### Should I memorise preposition rules?

Learn the two ladders, then learn the rest as chunks. Pure rule memorisation fails because exceptions outnumber the rules. Pattern plus chunk learning matches how the language actually works.

### How do I stop mixing up in and on?

Use the ladder. Enclosed or large means in. Surface means on. When unsure, picture the place: are you inside it or on top of it? The picture usually gives the right answer.

### How many fixed phrases should I learn?

Collect them as you meet them rather than targeting a number. A few dozen well-known chunks cover most student writing. Quality and regular review beat a long list you never revisit.

## Putting It Together

Prepositions stop being scary when you switch from rules to patterns. Use the time ladder, large to small, and the place ladder, enclosed to surface to point. Learn fixed phrases as whole chunks with example sentences. Keep the three-column notebook and read it aloud weekly. Within a couple of months your ear will guide you, and that is exactly how confident users of the language do it. If a textbook exercise still confuses you, photograph it for a clear explanation, and verify the pattern against your textbook.
