Teachers spend hours writing feedback that students never read. A long comment at the end of a paper, however thoughtful, often gets the same treatment: a glance at the grade, then the paper goes into a bag. The problem is not that students do not care. It is that most feedback is written to explain the past, when students need help with the future. Feedback students actually use is short, specific, and pointed at the next step.

This guide shows how to write that kind of feedback, how to give students time to act on it, and how to keep the workload manageable. The goal is not more feedback. It is feedback that changes what a student does next.

## Why Most Feedback Goes Unread

Feedback goes unread for three common reasons. The first is length. A long comment looks like a chore, and a student who is already tired will not read a paragraph about work they have finished. The second is vagueness. Comments like "be more careful" or "needs more detail" do not tell a student what to do differently. A student who reads them has no action to take, so the comment changes nothing. The third is timing. Feedback that arrives weeks after the work, or after the class has moved on, feels like a verdict on something the student has already forgotten.

There is a fourth reason that matters even more: feedback is often given without a chance to act on it. A student reads a comment, agrees with it, and then moves on to a new topic where the comment no longer applies. Feedback without a next step is like directions without a road. The student knows where they went wrong but has no way to practise doing it right.

The fix for all four reasons is the same: make feedback short, specific, forward-looking, and paired with a chance to use it. When feedback leads directly to an action, students read it, because it is useful rather than decorative.

## Make Feedback Short and Specific

Short feedback is not shallow feedback. It is feedback with the filler removed. A useful feedback note names exactly what the student did and exactly what to change. "Your introduction states the main idea clearly. In the second paragraph, add one example to support your point." That note is two sentences long, and a student can act on it immediately.

Specificity is what makes short feedback work. Compare "check your working" with "in question 3, show the step between lines two and three." The first comment could mean anything. The second points to a single place in the student's work. A student who receives the second comment knows what to look at, what to fix, and when they have fixed it.

A practical rule is one strength and one next step. Name something the student did well, so the feedback starts with recognition rather than criticism. Then name the single most important thing to improve. One next step is enough. If a paper has five problems, pick the one that matters most and let the student master it. Five corrections at once overwhelm; one correction at a time builds skill.

## Point to the Next Step

The most useful feedback answers the question: "What should I do next?" A comment that only describes what went wrong leaves the student stuck. A comment that points to the next step gives the student a job. That job should be concrete: redo a question, add an example, show a step, or explain a choice in one sentence.

Make the next step small enough to complete in a few minutes. "Rewrite the whole essay" is not a next step; it is a punishment. "Rewrite the second paragraph with one example" is a next step. Small steps let students succeed quickly, and that success builds the habit of using feedback instead of fearing it.

It also helps to show what the improved version looks like. A short model — one worked step, one example sentence, one corrected line — gives students a target. They do not have to guess what "better" means. They can compare their work to the model and see the gap for themselves. Showing a model once is faster than explaining the same gap twenty times.

## Separate Feedback From Grades

When feedback sits next to a grade, the grade wins. Students read the grade, feel good or bad about it, and stop. The comment beside it may as well not exist. If the work is practice rather than assessment, consider giving feedback without a grade, or giving the feedback first and the grade later. Students who receive feedback alone are far more likely to read it, because there is nothing else to look at.

This does not mean grades disappear. It means the order changes. Feedback first, grade later, or feedback on practice work and grades on assessed work, keeps each one doing its own job. Practice work exists for learning, so it should carry feedback. Assessed work exists for judging, so it can carry a grade.

Another way to separate them is to let students earn the grade by acting on the feedback. A student who revises their work using the feedback and resubmits it has used the feedback in the most direct way possible. The revised work is usually better, and the student has practised the exact skill the feedback named. ## Let Students Act on Feedback in Class

Feedback works best when students use it the same day they receive it. Build a short "feedback time" into the lesson after work is returned. Ten minutes is enough. Students read their feedback, make the correction, and show you the result. That loop — feedback, action, check — is what turns a comment into learning.

During feedback time, your job changes. Instead of teaching the whole class, you circulate and help the students who are stuck. Most students will manage the correction alone, because the feedback was short and specific. The few who need help get your full attention. The class learns that feedback is not a verdict; it is the start of the next piece of work.

Students can also use feedback in pairs. A student reads their partner's feedback and checks whether the correction matches it. This peer check keeps both students engaged, and it teaches them to read feedback critically. Over time, students start to give each other the same kind of short, specific feedback they receive from you.

## Keep the Tone Honest and Kind

Tone matters because feedback is personal. A student reads a comment on their work as a comment on themselves. Harsh feedback makes students defensive, and defensive students stop reading. Overly soft feedback is no better, because it hides the problem. The tone that works is honest and kind: clear about the problem, respectful of the effort, and confident that the student can improve.

Small word choices make a big difference. "You missed the second step" is a fact. "You always miss steps" is a judgment about the student, and it is rarely true. "This paragraph needs an example" points at the work. "This is weak" points at the student. Keep comments pointed at the work, and keep them focused on what can change.

It also helps to show that you noticed the effort. A student who tried hard and got it wrong deserves recognition for the effort and a clear next step for the error. A student who did not try deserves a conversation about the effort, not a comment about the work. Matching the feedback to the situation is part of the craft.

## Conclusion

Feedback students actually use is short, specific, and forward-looking: one strength, one next step, and a chance to act on it in class. Separate it from grades where you can, keep the tone honest and kind, and let students prove the feedback worked by correcting their own work. When feedback becomes a loop instead of a verdict, students start to read it — because it helps them get better.

## FAQ

### How long should a feedback comment be?

One or two sentences is usually enough: one strength and one next step. Short comments are more likely to be read and acted on than long ones. If a paper has many problems, pick the most important one and let the student master it first.

### Should I give feedback on every piece of work?

Not every piece needs written feedback. Practice work benefits most from feedback, because students can still act on it. For short routine work, a quick class-level note about the common error is often more efficient than twenty individual comments.

### What if students ignore feedback even when it is short?

Give them class time to act on it. A ten-minute feedback slot where students make the correction turns feedback into a task instead of a suggestion. Students who use feedback once are far more likely to use it again.

### Is it better to give feedback in writing or in person?

Both work, and they suit different moments. Written feedback is useful because students can reread it. Spoken feedback is useful for quick, in-the-moment corrections. The best feedback is the kind the student can act on right away, in whichever form it arrives.
