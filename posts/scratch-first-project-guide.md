Scratch is one of the friendliest ways to start coding, because you build programs by snapping colourful blocks together instead of typing code. This scratch first project guide will take you from a blank screen to a working project in about ten minutes: a cat that moves across the stage when you click a button. You need no experience, no typing skills, and no special software beyond a web browser. By the end, you will understand the three ideas at the heart of Scratch: sprites, blocks, and the stage.

## Scratch First Project Guide: Meet the Stage, Sprites, and Blocks

Open Scratch and you will see three main areas. The stage is the rectangular area on the right where your project runs and where the audience sees the action. Sprites are the characters and objects that appear on the stage. Every new project starts with one sprite, a cheerful orange cat. Blocks are the coloured instruction pieces on the left, grouped by what they do: motion, looks, sound, events, control, and more. To program, you drag blocks into the middle scripting area and snap them together like puzzle pieces. When blocks snap together, they form a script, which is simply a set of instructions the sprite follows.

Two details make Scratch forgiving for beginners. First, blocks only snap together in ways that make sense, so many syntax mistakes are impossible. Second, you can test your project at any moment by clicking the green flag above the stage. If something looks wrong, you change a block and test again immediately. That fast feedback loop is why Scratch is such a good first step into coding.

## Step One: Make the Cat Move

Start with motion. Drag a block that says move 10 steps from the motion group into the scripting area. Click the block itself and watch the cat shuffle to the right. You have just run your first instruction. Now add an event block on top, the one that says when green flag clicked. Snap it above the motion block so the script reads: when the flag is clicked, move 10 steps. Click the green flag and the cat moves once. Change 10 to 50 and click again. The cat jumps further. You have already learned a core coding idea: a program is a sequence of instructions that run in order.

Try stacking three move blocks with different numbers. Click the flag and watch the cat travel in three hops. If the cat walks off the edge of the stage, drag it back to the centre with your mouse. That is normal. Experimenting freely is part of learning, and nothing you do here can break anything permanently.

## Step Two: Add a Loop So the Cat Keeps Walking

Moving once is fine, but real programs repeat actions. Find the control group and drag a repeat block around your motion block. A repeat block wraps other blocks and runs them several times. Set it to repeat 10 times with a move 10 steps block inside, then click the flag. The cat now walks across the stage in ten small steps instead of one jump. You have just built your first loop, one of the most important ideas in all of programming, and you did it by dragging a single block.

Add a wait 1 second block inside the loop, after the motion block. Now the cat walks slowly, one step per second, like it is actually strolling. Changing the wait time changes the speed, which shows you something powerful: small numbers in your code create visible changes in behaviour. Play with the numbers. Try repeat 20 with move 5, or wait 0.5 seconds. Watching cause and effect like this is how programmers build intuition.

## Step Three: Give the Cat a Voice and a Reaction

A moving cat is good. A cat that reacts is better. Add a sound block that plays a meow when the flag is clicked, or a looks block that makes the cat say hello for two seconds. Place the say block at the start of your script so the cat greets the audience before it starts walking. Then add a second event: when this sprite clicked, make the cat say something funny or play a sound. Now your project responds to the audience, not just to the green flag. Interactivity is what turns a moving picture into a program.

You can also add a second sprite, perhaps a ball or a star, from the sprite library. Give it its own simple script, such as moving when clicked. Suddenly you have two characters behaving independently, which is how games and animations are built: many small scripts running at the same time, each controlling one sprite.

## Step Four: Save, Name, and Share Your Project

Give your project a name that describes what it does, such as My Walking Cat. Save your work so you can return to it later. If your Scratch account allows sharing, you can share the project so others can try it and even look inside to see how you built it. Looking inside other people's projects is one of the best ways to learn: you can see which blocks they used and remix their ideas into your own work, giving credit where it is due.

Before you share, test once more from the beginning. Click the green flag, watch the whole performance, and click the sprite to check its reaction. If anything behaves oddly, that is not failure. That is debugging, and fixing small surprises is exactly how programmers improve.

## Ideas for Your Second Project

Once the cat walks, the next steps are wide open. Make the cat bounce back and forth forever using a forever loop and an if on edge, bounce block. Build a simple game where clicking a sprite scores a point. Create a short animated story with two sprites taking turns to speak. Animate a sprite's costume so it looks like it is dancing. Each of these projects reuses what you just learned: events start scripts, loops repeat actions, and sprites respond to the audience. Progress in Scratch comes from combining a small set of ideas in new ways.

## FAQ

### Do I need to install anything to use Scratch?

No. Scratch runs in a web browser, so you can start on most computers or tablets without installing software. Create a free account if you want to save and share projects, or try it without an account for a quick first experiment.

### What age is Scratch for?

Scratch is designed for beginners of all ages, and it is widely used by school-age learners. Adults learning to code for the first time also find it helpful, because it teaches real programming ideas like loops, events, and conditions without the distraction of typing syntax.

### What if my blocks do not snap together?

Blocks in Scratch are shaped to fit only where they make sense. If a block will not snap, it probably belongs somewhere else in the script, or it needs a different kind of block around it. Try dragging it to a different position, and check whether you are placing it inside a loop or event block correctly.

### Can Scratch teach real coding?

Yes. Scratch teaches genuine programming concepts: sequences, loops, conditionals, variables, and events. Those ideas transfer directly to text-based languages later. The blocks are simply a friendlier way to write the same logic while you are learning.

### How long should my first project take?

About ten minutes for a simple moving-cat project, including experimenting with numbers and sounds. Do not rush. Playing with the blocks, changing values, and watching what happens is the learning itself, not a distraction from it.

## Conclusion: Ten Minutes to Your First Working Program

You started with a blank stage and a cat, and you now know how events start scripts, how sequences run in order, how loops repeat actions, and how sprites react to clicks. That is a real foundation in programming, built in ten minutes with no typing and no jargon. Keep the momentum going: change one thing in your project, add a second sprite, or try a forever loop, and watch what happens. Small experiments are how programmers grow.
