How to talk about art is a skill many students want but few are taught directly. Standing in front of a picture, it is easy to run out of words after saying that you like it or that it looks nice. Yet there is much more to say: what you actually see, how the artist made it, what it might mean, and whether it works well. Learning a simple framework and a set of useful words turns vague reactions into clear, confident comments. This guide teaches the four-step framework of describe, analyse, interpret, and judge, with sentence starters and practice tips you can use in class discussions, written responses, and gallery visits.

## Describe: Say What You Actually See

Description is the foundation of talking about art, and it means stating only what is visible, without opinions or guesses. Name the subject, the colours, the shapes, the lines, and the overall composition. Useful description words include bright, muted, rough, smooth, curved, angular, crowded, spacious, dark, and light. A good description sentence sounds like this: the picture shows a tall tree in the centre, painted in dark greens, with a winding path leading toward a small house. Notice how that sentence contains no judgement and no guess about meaning; it simply reports what is there. Practise description by covering any title or label and listing ten things you can see in thirty seconds. This habit stops you from jumping to opinions before you have really looked, and it gives your later comments solid evidence to stand on.

## Analyse: How the Artist Made It

Analysis moves from what you see to how the artwork was put together. Here you discuss the choices the artist made: the arrangement of shapes, the balance of colours, the use of light and shadow, the texture of the materials, and the sense of movement or stillness. Useful analysis words include balance, contrast, emphasis, repetition, pattern, focal point, and harmony. A good analysis sentence sounds like this: the artist uses strong contrast between the dark tree and the bright sky to make the tree the focal point, while the repeated curves of the path lead the eye through the picture. Analysis connects visual evidence to artistic decisions, which is why it sounds more informed than simple likes and dislikes. When you analyse, always point to something specific in the work that supports what you say.

## Interpret: What It Might Mean

Interpretation asks what the artwork might mean or how it might make a viewer feel, and it is the step where personal response becomes valuable. Different viewers may interpret the same picture differently, and that is normal, as long as each interpretation is supported by something visible in the work. Useful interpretation language is careful rather than absolute: this suggests, this feels like, this could represent, this reminds me of. For example, the winding path and the small distant house could suggest a long journey home, which gives the picture a hopeful feeling. Notice the word could: interpretation offers a thoughtful reading, not a claim about what the artist definitely intended. Strong interpretations connect a feeling or idea to visual evidence, such as colour, light, or composition, rather than floating free of the picture entirely.

## Judge: Is It Successful, and Why

Judgement is the final step, and it means giving an informed opinion about whether the artwork works well, supported by reasons from the earlier steps. A weak judgement says only that a picture is good or bad. A strong judgement says why: the picture works well because its clear focal point and balanced colours guide the viewer calmly through the scene, though the crowded corner slightly distracts from the main subject. Useful judgement words include effective, successful, convincing, balanced, original, memorable, and confusing, always paired with a reason. Judgement is not about being harsh or generous; it is about being fair and specific. You can admire a work while noting a weakness, and you can dislike a work while recognising a strength, and both responses count as good criticism when they are explained.

## Sentence Starters That Keep You Going

Sentence starters are a practical rescue when you stall mid-comment. For description, try: I notice, the most prominent feature is, in the foreground, in the background. For analysis, try: the artist uses, this creates a sense of, the contrast between, the repetition of. For interpretation, try: this suggests, this gives a feeling of, this could represent, this reminds me of. For judgement, try: overall, this works well because, one strength is, one weakness is, this would be stronger if. Keep a small list of these starters in your notebook and practise combining them: one description sentence, one analysis sentence, one interpretation sentence, and one judgement sentence make a complete short critique. With practice, the starters fall away and the structure becomes natural.

## Practise Critique in Pairs and Groups

Talking about art improves fastest in conversation, because other people notice things you missed. In pairs, take turns: one person describes and analyses for a minute while the other listens, then swap roles for interpretation and judgement. In groups, agree on simple discussion manners first: comment on the artwork, not the person; support opinions with visible evidence; and welcome different interpretations. A useful group routine is to look silently for a minute, share one description each, then build toward interpretation together. End by asking what question you would ask the artist if you could. That question often reveals what you found most interesting or puzzling, and it makes a strong closing line for a written critique as well.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between describing and analysing art?

Describing states only what you can see, such as subjects, colours, and shapes, without opinions. Analysing explains how the artist arranged those elements, such as balance, contrast, and focal point, and what effect those choices create. Description gives the evidence; analysis explains the decisions behind it.

### Can two people interpret the same artwork differently?

Yes, and both interpretations can be valid if each one is supported by visible evidence in the work. Interpretation is a thoughtful reading, not a single correct answer. Use careful language such as suggests or could represent, and connect your reading to colours, composition, or subject matter.

### How do I judge art without sounding rude?

Focus on the artwork and give reasons, not personal attacks. Pair strengths with weaknesses, use specific evidence, and phrase suggestions as possibilities, such as this would be stronger if. Fair, specific criticism is respectful, even when it points out a weakness.

### What should I do if I do not know much about art?

Start with description, which needs no special knowledge, only careful looking. Then use the four steps in order, leaning on the sentence starters in this guide. Your comments will sound informed because they are built on observation and evidence, not on background knowledge.

## Conclusion

Talking about art becomes much easier with a framework: describe what you see, analyse how it was made, interpret what it might mean, and judge how well it works, with reasons at every step. Use the sentence starters when you stall, practise in pairs, and always support opinions with visible evidence. With that habit, you will move from saying a picture looks nice to explaining exactly why it matters.
