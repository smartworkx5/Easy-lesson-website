Pythagoras theorem explained with a picture is one of the most useful lessons in all of geometry. It connects the three sides of a right triangle with a single neat equation, and it lets you find a missing side whenever you know the other two. This guide explains the theorem with a picture — squares built on the sides of the triangle — and then works through three examples, including how to find a missing shorter side.

## Pythagoras Theorem Explained: What the Theorem Says

In any right triangle (a triangle with one 90-degree angle), the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides. The longest side, opposite the right angle, is called the hypotenuse and is labelled c. The other two sides are labelled a and b. The theorem is written as: a squared + b squared = c squared.

The word "square" here is doing double duty, and that is part of the beauty. Squaring a number means multiplying it by itself: 4 squared is 16. But you can also draw an actual square on each side of the triangle. The theorem says the area of the square on the hypotenuse equals the areas of the two smaller squares added together. That picture is worth remembering, because it turns an abstract formula into something you can see.

The theorem only works for right triangles. If the triangle has no right angle, the equation does not apply. Always check for the small square mark that indicates the right angle before using the formula.

## The Squares-on-the-Sides Picture

Draw a right triangle with legs 3 and 4. Now draw a square on each side. The square on the leg of length 3 has area 3 times 3 = 9. The square on the leg of length 4 has area 4 times 4 = 16. Add them: 9 + 16 = 25. The square on the hypotenuse must therefore have area 25, which means the hypotenuse is 5, because 5 times 5 = 25.

This is the famous 3-4-5 triangle, the simplest whole-number example of the theorem. Carpenters and builders have used it for thousands of years to check right angles: mark 3 units along one edge and 4 along the other, and the distance between the marks must be 5 if the corner is truly square.

Another whole-number triple is 5-12-13: 5 squared is 25, 12 squared is 144, and 25 + 144 = 169, which is 13 squared. And 6-8-10 works too: 36 + 64 = 100 = 10 squared. Spotting these patterns can speed up problems, but the formula works for every right triangle, not just the neat ones.

## Worked Example: Finding the Hypotenuse (Legs 6 and 8)

A right triangle has legs 6 and 8. Find the hypotenuse. Write the formula: a squared + b squared = c squared. Substitute a = 6 and b = 8: 6 squared + 8 squared = c squared. Compute the squares: 36 + 64 = c squared, so c squared = 100. Take the square root: c = 10. The hypotenuse is 10.

Check that the answer is sensible: the hypotenuse must be the longest side, and 10 is longer than both 6 and 8. It must also be shorter than the sum of the other two sides (6 + 8 = 14), because a straight line is the shortest path. The answer 10 passes both checks.

When the final square is not a perfect square, leave the answer as a square root or round as the question asks. For example, legs 2 and 3 give c squared = 4 + 9 = 13, so c = the square root of 13, about 3.61. Do not force a whole number where none exists.

## Worked Example: Finding a Missing Shorter Side

Pythagoras' theorem also works backwards. A right triangle has a hypotenuse of 13 and one leg of 5. Find the other leg. Write the formula: a squared + b squared = c squared, with c = 13 and a = 5. Substitute: 5 squared + b squared = 13 squared, so 25 + b squared = 169. Subtract 25 from both sides: b squared = 144. Take the square root: b = 12. The missing leg is 12.

The key difference from the first example is the subtraction step. When you know the hypotenuse and one leg, you subtract the known leg's square from the hypotenuse's square. When you know both legs, you add. Mixing up these two cases is the most common error with this theorem, so always ask first: "Am I finding the hypotenuse, or a shorter side?"

Check: 5 squared + 12 squared = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13 squared. The numbers balance, so the answer is verified. Also check the size: the missing leg 12 is shorter than the hypotenuse 13, as it must be.

## Worked Example: A Real-Life Ramp Problem

A ladder leans against a wall. Its foot is 9 metres from the wall, and it reaches 12 metres up the wall. How long is the ladder? The wall and the ground form a right angle, so the ladder is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 9 and 12.

Apply the formula: 9 squared + 12 squared = c squared. That is 81 + 144 = 225, so c squared = 225. The square root of 225 is 15, so the ladder is 15 metres long. Check: 15 is longer than both 9 and 12, and shorter than 9 + 12 = 21. Both checks pass.

Problems like this appear constantly in real life: ramps, ladders, diagonals of rectangles, and distances on maps. Whenever you see a right angle and two known lengths, Pythagoras' theorem is usually the tool you need. Drawing the triangle and labelling the sides before calculating prevents most mistakes.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid with Pythagoras

The most common mistake is using the theorem on a triangle without a right angle. Look for the right-angle mark. Without it, the formula does not apply, and the answer will be wrong even if the arithmetic is perfect.

Another mistake is forgetting to take the square root at the end. If c squared = 100, then c = 10, not 100. Similarly, if b squared = 144, then b = 12, not 144. Always finish with the square root step.

A third mistake is labelling the wrong side as c. The hypotenuse is always c, and it is always the side opposite the right angle and the longest side. If you label a shorter side as c, the arithmetic will give a nonsense answer, often a negative number under the square root.

## Try It Yourself: Practice with Answers

Try these three problems, then check your answers below.

1. A right triangle has legs 5 and 12. Find the hypotenuse.
2. A right triangle has a hypotenuse of 10 and one leg of 6. Find the other leg.
3. A right triangle has legs 9 and 12. Find the hypotenuse.

Answers: (1) 5 squared + 12 squared = 25 + 144 = 169, so the hypotenuse is 13. (2) 10 squared minus 6 squared = 100 − 36 = 64, so the other leg is 8. (3) 9 squared + 12 squared = 81 + 144 = 225, so the hypotenuse is 15. If all three worked, you have the theorem. If the middle one felt tricky, remember: finding a shorter side means subtracting.

## Conclusion

Pythagoras' theorem says that in a right triangle, a squared + b squared = c squared — the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides. Add the squares to find the hypotenuse; subtract to find a shorter side; always finish with a square root. Draw the triangle, label the hypotenuse as c, and check that your answer is the longest side but shorter than the sum of the other two. With that routine, right-triangle problems become quick and reliable.

## FAQ

### What is Pythagoras' theorem in simple words?

In a right triangle, the square of the longest side (the hypotenuse) equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides. Written as a formula: a squared + b squared = c squared, where c is the hypotenuse.

### Does Pythagoras' theorem work for all triangles?

No. It only works for right triangles — triangles with one 90-degree angle. For other triangles, the relationship between the sides is different, and using the formula would give a wrong answer.

### How do you find a missing shorter side?

Put the hypotenuse and the known leg into the formula, then subtract: unknown leg squared = hypotenuse squared minus known leg squared. For a hypotenuse of 13 and a leg of 5, the other leg squared is 169 − 25 = 144, so the leg is 12.

### What is a Pythagorean triple?

A Pythagorean triple is a set of three whole numbers that satisfy the theorem, such as 3-4-5, 5-12-13, and 6-8-10. They are useful for quick checks, but the formula works for every right triangle, including ones with non-whole-number sides.

### Why is the hypotenuse always the longest side?

The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle, which is the largest angle in the triangle (90 degrees, while the other two must add to 90). In any triangle, the longest side sits opposite the largest angle, so the hypotenuse is always the longest side.
