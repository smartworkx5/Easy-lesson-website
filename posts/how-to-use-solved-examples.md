Solved examples are everywhere: in textbooks, in class notes, and in the worked solutions teachers write on the board. They look like the easiest study tool in the world. You read the example, you understand it, you move on. But many students discover that reading a solved example feels like learning, while actually teaching very little.

The reason is that a solved example shows you the path, but walking the path yourself is a different skill. Copying the steps trains your hand. Rebuilding the steps from memory trains your brain. The difference shows up the moment a new problem appears.

This article shows you how to use solved examples properly: the cover-try-compare method, how to explain each step, and how to transfer the skill to problems you have never seen.

## Why Copying Feels Like Learning

When you read a solved example, every step makes sense. The logic is clear, the answer is correct, and your brain nods along. That feeling of sense-making is real, but it is not the same as being able to produce the solution yourself. Psychologists call this the illusion of understanding: the solution is clear while you look at it, and it vanishes the moment you look away.

Copying makes the illusion worse. Your hand writes the steps, your eyes follow them, and the finished page looks like work. But your brain did not make a single decision. It did not choose the method, it did not decide what comes next, and it did not check whether each step was right. Those decisions are the actual learning, and copying skips all of them.

The test for whether an example taught you anything is simple: cover it and redo it. If you can, you learned. If you cannot, you only watched.

## The Cover-Try-Compare Method

The cover-try-compare method turns passive reading into real practice in three steps. First, study the example: read it slowly, and make sure you understand what each step does and why. Pay special attention to the first step, because choosing how to start is usually the hardest part of a new problem.

Second, cover the solution completely. With the example hidden, solve the same problem yourself from scratch. Do not peek. Struggle for a few minutes if you need to. The struggle is the point: it forces your brain to rebuild the reasoning instead of replaying the picture of the page.

Third, compare your attempt with the worked solution, step by step. Where did you match? Where did you diverge? If you made an error, find the exact step where your reasoning went off track, and understand why. Then cover the solution again and redo the problem until you can complete it without looking.

One example studied this way teaches more than five examples copied in a row.

## Explain Each Step in Your Own Words

After you can redo an example, go one level deeper: explain each step. For every step, ask yourself two questions. What is this step doing? And why is it the right step here?

Explaining why is what separates students who can solve one problem from students who can solve many. A student who knows the steps can repeat the example. A student who knows why each step is taken can adapt the method when the problem changes. When a step has no clear why in your mind, that step is a gap. Go back to your notes or textbook and fill it before moving on.

A practical habit: after studying an example, write a short why-note beside each step, in your own words. These notes become a personal guide that is far more useful than the bare steps, because they carry the reasoning, not just the motions.

## Transfer: From One Example to New Problems

The real goal is never the example itself. It is the next problem, the one you have not seen, where no worked solution is waiting. Transfer, applying a learned method to a new problem, is the skill that matters, and it needs deliberate practice.

After mastering one example, try a similar problem with one detail changed. Then try one with two details changed. Then try a problem that looks different on the surface but uses the same method underneath. Each variation forces your brain to recognise the method in a new disguise, which is exactly what tests require.

A useful exercise is the method card. On a card, write the name of the method, when to use it, the steps in your own words, and one warning about the most common mistake. Review your method cards before tackling new problems. Over time, you build a small library of methods you can actually use, instead of a notebook of examples you once copied.

## Common Mistakes With Solved Examples

Four mistakes waste the value of solved examples. The first is reading too many and doing too few. Three examples studied deeply beat ten skimmed. The second is peeking during the try step. One peek collapses the whole exercise back into copying, so cover the solution properly and resist the urge.

The third is skipping the comparison. If you redo an example and never compare, you may practise a wrong method and cement it. Comparison is where the learning is corrected. The fourth is studying only the examples you already find easy. Your time is best spent on the examples that challenge you, because those are the ones with a skill you do not yet own.

Finally, remember that examples in books and online are sometimes wrong, and AI tools can make mistakes too. If a step in an example looks inconsistent, verify it against your textbook before you memorise it. A wrong example practised diligently teaches a wrong method.

## FAQ

### How many solved examples should I study for one topic?

Start with two or three, studied deeply with cover-try-compare. If you can redo all of them from memory and explain each step, move on to fresh problems. If you cannot, study one more and repeat. Depth beats quantity: five copied examples teach less than one example rebuilt from memory.

### What should I do if I get stuck during the try step?

Struggle for a few minutes first, because the effort is part of the learning. If you are truly stuck, uncover just the next step, not the whole solution, then cover it again and continue. This hint-and-retry approach keeps you in charge of the solution instead of sliding back into copying.

### Is it cheating to use solved examples?

No, as long as you use them to learn rather than to copy. Studying an example, covering it, redoing it, and then solving fresh problems is honest, effective studying. Copying a solved example and presenting it as your own work is not learning at all, and it leaves you helpless when a new problem appears.

### What if my textbook has very few solved examples?

Work the ones it has deeply, then create your own practice. Take a homework problem you have already solved, cover your solution, and redo it a day later. Ask a classmate or teacher for one extra example of the same method. The cover-try-compare method works with any solved problem, not just textbook examples.

## Conclusion

Solved examples are a powerful tool, but only when you use them actively. Study the example, cover it, solve it yourself, compare step by step, and explain why each step is taken. Then transfer the method to fresh problems with changed details. Do that, and examples stop being something you copy and start being something you own.
