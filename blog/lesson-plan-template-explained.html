A Lesson Plan Template That Works for Any Subject

A lesson plan template is only useful if you actually use it, and most templates fail that test because they are too long. A template with twenty boxes, three pages of prompts, and a separate section for every possible detail will be abandoned within a week. The best template is a single page with a handful of sections that force you to make the few decisions that matter: what students will learn, how they will practise it, how you will check it, and what you need ready. This guide explains each section of a template that works for any subject, from maths to languages to science, and shows you what to leave out.

What a Good Template Must Do

A good template does three jobs. First, it makes the goal visible, so you never teach a lesson without knowing what it is for. Second, it keeps the lesson in order, so the opener, the teaching, the practice, and the check each get their place. Third, it is fast to fill in, so planning stays a daily habit instead of a weekend chore. If a section does not help with one of these three jobs, it does not belong on the template. That is why long templates fail: they ask for background reading, long rationales, and detailed scripts that no teacher has time to write every day. A template should feel like a checklist for a pilot, not an essay. You glance at it, fill it in, and teach from it. When the lesson ends, you can scribble one line about what worked and what did not, and that note makes tomorrow's plan better.

The Six Sections That Matter

A template that works for any subject needs only six sections. First, the goal: one sentence saying what students will be able to do by the end. Second, materials: a short list of what you and the students need. Third, the opener: how you will start and connect to prior learning. Fourth, the main teaching and activity: what you will explain and what students will do. Fifth, the check: how you will find out whether the goal was met. Sixth, the close and follow-up: how the lesson ends and what homework or next step follows. Six sections fit comfortably on one page. They also work in any subject because they describe the shape of learning, not the content. A language lesson and a science lesson look different inside these sections, but the sections themselves stay the same. Write the Goal First, Always

The goal section sits at the top of the template for a reason: everything below it should serve it. Write the goal as an observable action, using verbs like solve, explain, write, compare, or design. "Students will compare two poems and explain which image is stronger" is a usable goal. "Students will understand poetry" is not, because you cannot observe understanding directly. When the goal is observable, the check for understanding writes itself: you ask students to do the thing the goal describes. The goal also protects you from overload. If a planned activity does not serve the goal, the template makes that obvious, and you can cut the activity before it eats lesson time. Many teachers find that writing the goal first is the single change that most improves their planning. For a deeper guide to this skill, see writing clear learning objectives.

Keep the Activity Section Short and Concrete

The main activity section should read like instructions you could hand to another teacher. Write what students will do, in what arrangement, and for how long. "In pairs, students solve problems 1-6, then compare methods for 5 minutes" is concrete. "Students practise" is not. Include the exact problems, page numbers, or task names so you do not have to search for them during the lesson. Note the key questions you will ask while students work, because good questions keep an activity focused. If the activity needs differentiation, add one line for support and one line for extension, not a separate plan. For example: "Support: worked example on the board. Extension: two challenge questions." Short and concrete beats long and vague every time. A teacher picking up your plan with five minutes of notice should be able to teach the activity from this section alone.

The Check and the Close Deserve Their Own Boxes

Many templates bury the check for understanding inside the activity section, and that is why it gets skipped. Give the check its own box so it cannot be forgotten. The check can be small: three questions on the board, a show of hands with a quick explanation, an exit ticket, or a single problem solved alone. What matters is that every student shows you something, not just the confident volunteers. The close deserves its own box too. A lesson that simply stops when the bell rings leaves learning unfinished. The close restates the key point, links it to the next lesson, and gives students a moment to record what mattered. Even two minutes of closure makes the lesson more memorable. When both boxes are on the template, they become part of your habit instead of extras you add when time allows.

What to Leave Off the Template

A template is defined as much by what it leaves out as by what it includes. Leave off long background explanations, because you already know your subject. Leave off a full script, because bullet points are faster and more flexible. Leave off elaborate behaviour plans, unless a specific class genuinely needs one, and then keep it to a line. Leave off anything you fill in with the same words every day; if a box always says the same thing, delete the box. The test for every section is simple: does filling it in change what I will do in the lesson? If the answer is no, remove it. A lean template also leaves room for a short reflection line at the bottom: "What worked? What will I change?" One honest sentence after each lesson builds better plans over time than any elaborate form.

Conclusion

Advertisement

A lesson plan template that works for any subject is short, goal-first, and built around the shape of learning: goal, materials, opener, main activity, check, and close. Keep it to one page, write the goal as an observable action, make the activity concrete, and give the check and the close their own boxes. Trim anything that does not change what you will do. The best template is the one you will still be using in a month.

FAQ

How long should a lesson plan be?

One page is enough for most daily lessons. A single page keeps the goal, the activity, and the check visible at a glance. Longer plans are fine for special lessons, but a daily plan should be short enough to write quickly and teach from directly.

Does one template really work for every subject?

Yes, because the sections describe the shape of learning, not the content. The goal, opener, activity, check, and close exist in every subject. What changes is what you write inside each section, not the sections themselves.

Should students see the lesson plan?

Sharing the goal and the shape of the lesson helps students. A short note on the board, such as "Today: solve two-step equations. Check: three problems alone," tells students what to aim for. The full template is for the teacher, but the goal can be public.

How often should I change my template?

Keep the same template for at least a few weeks so it becomes a habit. Change it only when a section proves useless or something important is missing. Small, occasional adjustments beat frequent redesigns.
