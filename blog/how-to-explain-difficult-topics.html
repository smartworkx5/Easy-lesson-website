How to Explain Difficult Topics So Students Get It

Every teacher knows the feeling: you explain a topic carefully, the class nods, and then the first practice question shows that almost nobody understood it. The problem is rarely the students, and it is rarely the topic itself. Usually the explanation moved too fast, skipped a step the teacher no longer notices, or assumed a piece of knowledge the students did not have. Explaining a difficult topic is a design problem, and it can be solved with a clear method.

The method in this guide has a simple shape: find the real sticking point, start from what students already know, break the idea into small steps, explain it more than one way, and check understanding before moving on. None of these steps is complicated. Together, they turn a hard topic into a series of small, passable steps.

Find the Real Sticking Point

Before you explain a topic, ask what makes it hard. Difficult topics are usually difficult in one specific place: a new word with an everyday meaning, a step that feels backwards, a rule with exceptions, or a jump from concrete examples to an abstract idea. When you name that sticking point, you can aim the whole explanation at it instead of explaining everything at the same level of detail.

A practical way to find it is to look at past mistakes. Which question do students get wrong most often? Where do they stop, guess, or copy the previous example without understanding it? The answers point to the sticking point. If students can do the first two steps of a process but always fail the third, the third step is where your explanation needs to slow down.

It also helps to ask students directly. A quick question at the start — "What part of this topic feels hardest?" — often surfaces the sticking point in their own words. Their answers may surprise you. The part you thought was hard may be fine, and the part you thought was easy may be the real barrier.

Start From What Students Already Know

New ideas stick when they connect to something students already understand. Before introducing a difficult topic, name the connection out loud. "You already know how to do this. Today we add one new step." That single sentence lowers the fear that comes with a hard topic and gives students a place to stand.

Good starting points are everyday experiences, earlier lessons, and ideas from other subjects. A new science concept might connect to something students have seen at home. A new grammar rule might connect to a pattern they already use when they speak. A new maths method might be one step beyond a method they already trust. The connection does not need to be perfect. It needs to be familiar enough that the new idea has somewhere to land.

Watch for the opposite error: starting from the definition. Definitions are compact summaries, and they make sense after you understand the idea, not before. If you start with a formal definition, students memorise words they cannot use. If you start with a familiar example and build toward the definition, the definition arrives as a summary of something they already grasp.

Break It Into Small Steps

Difficult topics usually contain several small ideas packed into one big idea. Unpack them. Teach one step at a time, and let students practise each step before the next one arrives. A five-step process taught as five short lessons beats the same process taught as one long explanation, because students can succeed at each step along the way.

Make the steps visible. Write them on the board in order, and leave them there while students practise. Number them. When a student gets stuck, you can point to the exact step instead of re-explaining the whole topic. "You did steps one and two well. Look at step three." That kind of pointer is far more useful than starting over from the beginning.

Small steps also make it easier to spot where understanding breaks. If students succeed at steps one to three and fail at step four, you know exactly where to reteach. Without visible steps, the same failure looks like "they did not get it," and the reteaching starts from scratch. For ideas on checking understanding with questions, see Classroom Questioning Techniques That Get Everyone Thinking.

Use More Than One Explanation

No single explanation works for everyone. Some students need a picture, others need a story, others need to see the steps worked through, and others need to try it themselves. When you explain a difficult topic in two or three ways, you give every student a second chance to catch it.

A useful trio is: show it, say it in everyday words, and show it working. A diagram or model makes the idea visible. An everyday-words version strips away jargon: "In plain words, this means..." A worked example shows the idea in action, step by step. Students who missed the idea in one form often catch it in another.

Analogies help too, but they need care. A good analogy maps cleanly onto the topic: each part of the analogy matches a part of the idea. A loose analogy can create new confusion, because students remember the story and forget which parts of it were the point. When you use an analogy, name its limits: "This comparison helps with this part. It does not cover that part."

Let Students Explain It Back

The strongest test of understanding is whether students can explain the idea themselves. After your explanation, ask students to put the idea into their own words — to a partner, in writing, or to the class. What comes back tells you more than nodding ever will. A student who can explain it in their own words understands it. A student who can only repeat your words may not.

Pair explanations work well for this. Give students two minutes to explain the idea to a partner, then ask partners to report one thing their partner said. The reporting step keeps both students honest, because each one knows they may be asked to share. It also gives you a quick sample of the class's understanding without calling on every student.

Writing works too. A one-sentence summary — "In my own words, this topic means..." — takes a minute and produces a record you can scan. If most summaries are accurate, move on. If most share the same error, reteach that point before it hardens into a habit.

Check Understanding Before Moving On

The most common explanation mistake is moving on too soon. Nodding is not understanding. Quiet is not understanding. The only reliable checks are ones where every student has to produce something: an answer, a step, a summary, or a question. Build a check into the end of every explanation, and use what it tells you.

Quick checks can be very simple: three practice questions, a show of hands for which step was hardest, or a short task that uses the new idea once. The point is not to grade. The point is to find out whether the class is ready for the next step. If half the class is not ready, the next step will fail no matter how well you explain it.

When the check shows a gap, reteach the gap, not the whole topic. Point to the step that failed, explain it a different way, and check again. Short reteaching loops like this keep a difficult topic from becoming a difficult week.

Conclusion

Explaining a difficult topic well is not about talent. It is about finding the sticking point, starting from what students know, breaking the idea into visible steps, explaining it more than one way, and checking understanding before moving on. When students explain the idea back to you in their own words, you know the explanation worked — and the topic stops being difficult.

FAQ

Why do students nod even when they do not understand?

Nodding is often politeness, not understanding. Students may not want to slow the class down or may believe they understood until they try a question. That is why checks that require every student to produce an answer are more reliable than asking "Do you understand?"

How many steps should I break a topic into?

As many as the topic needs for each step to feel doable. A good test is that a student can practise each step on its own and succeed. If a step still causes widespread failure, it probably contains two steps hiding inside one.

What if only a few students are stuck?

Reteach the sticking point briefly for the whole class, then give the students who are stuck a supported task while the rest move to a stretch task. A short small-group explanation is often enough once the class explanation has covered the main idea.

Should I avoid definitions when explaining hard topics?

No, but do not start with them. Build understanding with examples and everyday words first, then introduce the definition as a summary. A definition that arrives after understanding is a useful label; one that arrives before it is a phrase to memorise.
