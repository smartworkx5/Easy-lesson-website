Your First CV as a Student: What to Put On It Guide

Writing your first CV can feel impossible. How do you fill a page about your working life when you have barely started working? The good news is that a student CV is not supposed to look like an experienced professional's CV. Its job is different: to show who you are, what you can do, and what you care about, using school, projects, activities, and volunteering as evidence. Done well, a one-page student CV is a powerful tool for internships, part-time jobs, and applications.

CV stands for curriculum vitae, a Latin phrase meaning course of life. In practice, it is a short, clear summary of your education, skills, and experience. This article walks you through each section, so you can build a first CV that is honest, tidy, and genuinely impressive.

Start With the Basics

Every CV begins with your name and contact details: your full name, a sensible email address, and a phone number if you are comfortable sharing one. Keep it simple and professional. An email address based on your name looks far better than a joke address from years ago. You do not need to include your full home address, your date of birth, or a photograph unless specifically asked.

Below your details, add a short personal statement of two or three sentences. This is not a life story. It is a snapshot: who you are, what you are interested in, and what you are looking for. For example, a student might describe themselves as a keen science student interested in practical problem-solving, seeking a first work experience placement. Short, specific, and honest beats long and vague every time.

Education: More Than a List of Subjects

The education section comes next, and for students it is the most important part. List your current school, the years you have attended, and the subjects you are studying. If you have completed qualifications or notable results, include them. You can also mention a favourite subject and why it interests you, but keep it brief.

Do not just list subjects. Add a line about something you achieved or learned. Did you complete a challenging project, improve steadily in a subject, or take part in a competition? Those details turn a flat list into evidence of effort and growth. If space allows, mention relevant coursework, such as a science investigation or a design project, especially when it connects to the opportunity you are applying for.

Skills: Show Them, Do Not Just Claim Them

Every student CV has a skills section, and this is where many CVs go wrong. Writing that you are hardworking, a team player, and a good communicator means little on its own, because everyone writes that. Skills become convincing when paired with evidence. Instead of only listing communication, add where you used it, such as presenting a group project or helping run a school event.

Think broadly about skills. Digital skills count, from word processing to basic coding or design tools. Language skills count, including any additional languages you speak. Practical skills count too, from first aid training to organising events. Choose skills that fit the opportunity, and be honest. Claiming a skill you do not have will unravel quickly in a real workplace.

Projects, Activities, and Volunteering

This section is where a student CV comes alive. School projects show initiative and follow-through. Describe a project briefly: what you made or did, what your role was, and what the result was. A student who built a model, organised a fundraiser, or coded a small app has real experience worth describing, even if it happened at school.

Clubs, sports, and volunteering matter just as much. Team sports show teamwork and commitment. Volunteering shows responsibility and care for others. Helping at a community event shows reliability. Part-time work, babysitting, or helping in a family business all count as experience, because they taught you to be punctual, helpful, and responsible. Describe each activity in one or two lines, focusing on what you did and what you learned.

Layout, Length, and Final Checks

Advertisement

A student CV should fit on one page. Use clear headings, consistent formatting, and a readable font size. Leave white space so the page does not look cramped. Check spelling carefully, and ask a teacher, parent, or friend to read it, because fresh eyes catch mistakes you have stopped seeing.

Tailor your CV for each opportunity. If you apply for a design placement, move your art projects higher. If you apply for a shop job, emphasise customer-facing experience and reliability. Keep a master copy with everything on it, then create a shorter version for each application. Finally, save your CV as a tidy document with your name in the file name, so the reader can find it easily.

FAQ

How long should a student CV be?

One page is ideal for a first CV. Readers spend little time on each CV, so a single clear page forces you to choose your strongest points. If you genuinely have more strong experience, a second page is acceptable, but one page should be your goal.

What if I have no work experience at all?

That is normal for a first CV. School projects, clubs, sports, volunteering, and hobbies all provide evidence of skills and character. Focus on what you did, what you learned, and how you contributed, rather than worrying about job titles.

Should I include hobbies on my CV?

Yes, if they show something useful. A hobby that involves skill, commitment, or teamwork, such as playing an instrument, coding, coaching younger students, or running a blog, adds real value. A list of passive interests adds little, so choose hobbies that say something about you.

Do I need a different CV for every application?

You need the same core CV, adjusted for each opportunity. Move the most relevant experience higher, adjust your personal statement slightly, and emphasise the skills that matter most for that role. Small, thoughtful changes make a CV feel written for the reader, not copied blindly.

Conclusion

Your first CV does not need work experience to be strong. It needs a clear personal statement, honest education details, skills backed by evidence, and projects, activities, and volunteering that show who you are. Keep it to one page, check it carefully, and tailor it for each opportunity. Start with a master copy today, and improve it every time you learn something new.
