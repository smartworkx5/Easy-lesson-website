Density Tower: Layers of Liquids You Can See

A density tower experiment turns an invisible idea into something you can see. When several liquids are poured gently into a tall glass, they settle into separate layers instead of mixing into one colour. The order of those layers is not random: it shows which liquids are denser and which are lighter, and it gives you a vivid way to remember what density really means.

Materials and Steps for the Density Tower Experiment

You need a tall, clear glass, a spoon, and three or four liquids of different densities, such as syrup or honey, coloured water, washing-up liquid, and cooking oil. Keep the glass on a tray and ask an adult to help with anything sticky or staining. Pour the densest liquid first and let it settle, then pour each next liquid very slowly down the side of the glass or over the back of a spoon. Fast pouring stirs the layers together and breaks the tower.

Testing Objects in Your Tower

Once the layers are stable, gently drop small objects in one at a time and watch where each stops. A coin usually sinks to the bottom, a grape may stop on a denser layer below the water, and a light object may float or rest between two layers. This shows that floating and sinking are not about weight alone: an object settles where its density matches the liquid around it. Record each stopping point and compare it with your prediction.

Why the Density Tower Experiment Works

Liquids form layers because denser liquids sink below lighter ones, just as a stone sinks in water. Poured gently, each liquid finds its own level according to its density, and oil and water stay separate because they do not mix well, keeping their boundary sharp. The tower also explains everyday sights, from oil floating on soup to a stone sinking in a pond. It is a simplified model, though: temperature and dissolved substances can shift results slightly, and noting that shows careful thinking.

Variables You Can Test Next

Change one thing at a time: dissolve salt in the water layer and see whether objects float higher, try a different oil or a wider glass, or test the same object in each liquid separately before testing it in the tower. Repeat each test, because a single lucky pour can mislead. If an object stops in a different place on the second try, note what changed instead of picking the result that matches your prediction.

Staying Safe and Recording Your Results

Adult supervision is needed, especially when handling glass or staining liquids. Keep the experiment away from eyes and electronics, work on a tray, and wash your hands afterwards. Never drink or taste liquids once mixed in the tower, even if every ingredient started in the kitchen. Record a labelled drawing of each layer and where each object stopped, and check your explanation against your textbook, remembering that AI tools can make mistakes.

FAQ

Why did my layers mix instead of stacking?

Fast pouring is the usual cause, since it stirs liquids together before they settle. Pour each new liquid slowly over the back of a spoon and let the previous layer settle fully. Liquids with very similar densities also make sharp layers harder to achieve.

Does the order of pouring matter?

Yes, a great deal. Always pour the densest liquid first and the lightest last, so each new liquid settles on a denser layer below. Pouring a dense liquid on top of a light one makes it sink through and mix, spoiling the tower.

Why does oil float on water?

Oil is less dense than water, so the same volume of oil weighs less and settles above it. Oil and water also do not mix well, which keeps their boundary clear and the oil layer neatly on top.

Can I keep my density tower for a long time?

It may last a while if left completely still, but layers slowly blur at the edges, especially if the glass is moved or warmed. For a display, build the tower shortly before it is needed and photograph it straight away.

Conclusion
