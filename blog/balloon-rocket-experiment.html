Balloon Rocket: Action, Reaction and a String

A balloon rocket experiment is one of the fastest ways to feel a physics law in action. Blow up a balloon, let it go along a string, and it zooms off in one direction while air rushes out the other way. That pairing, air pushing one way and balloon moving the other, is Newton's third law: every action has an equal and opposite reaction. You can measure distance and time, and test what changes when balloon size, string angle, or load changes.

Materials and Setup for the Balloon Rocket Experiment

You need a long piece of string, two fixed points to tie it to, such as chairs or door handles, a drinking straw, tape, and several balloons of the same size and type. A tape measure and a stopwatch help you record distance and time. Choose a clear, straight run with nothing in the way. Thread the string through the straw before tying it tightly, and pull it as straight as you can, since a sagging string slows the rocket. Tape the balloon to the straw, leaving the neck free to inflate and hold closed until launch.

Launching and Measuring Your Rocket

Blow up the balloon, pinch the neck closed, and slide the rocket to one end of the string. Let go of the neck: air rushes out backwards and the balloon shoots forwards until the air runs out or the string ends. Measure distance and time, then repeat the same launch at least twice.

What to Observe During the Flight

Record distance, time, and a note on the flight: straight, wobbly, or slow at the end. From distance and time you can estimate average speed, letting you compare designs fairly even when they travel different distances. A rocket that goes far but slowly differs from one that goes fast over a short distance, and both patterns tell you something.

Why the Balloon Rocket Experiment Works

When the neck is released, pressurised air rushes out backwards. That escaping air is the action, and the reaction pushes the balloon forwards with an equal and opposite force. The straw keeps the balloon on the string, turning that push into straight-line motion instead of wild spinning. The rocket slows and stops because the air runs out, so the push fades, while friction between straw and string, plus air resistance, drags on the balloon. A bigger balloon holds more air but is heavier and faces more air resistance, which is why changing one variable at a time matters.

Variables You Can Test Next

Try inflating the balloon to different sizes and comparing distance and speed, keeping string and balloon type the same. Try a sloped string, one end higher than the other, and see whether uphill runs travel less far than downhill runs. Or tape a small load, such as a paper clip, to the rocket and test how extra weight changes the flight. Change only one variable per set of tests and keep the launch method identical, or you cannot tell which change caused the result.

Staying Safe and Recording Your Results

Adult supervision is sensible, especially for younger children, because balloons can pop loudly and startle, and broken pieces must be kept away from small children. Never aim a balloon rocket at a face, keep the string away from necks, and clear the area of anything breakable. Record results in a table and conclude by explaining the flight with action and reaction, friction, and air resistance. Check your explanation against your textbook, remembering that AI tools can make mistakes.

FAQ

Why does my balloon spin instead of flying straight?

The straw is probably loose, the balloon is taped off-centre, or the string is sagging. Tape the balloon firmly along the straw, centre it carefully, and pull the string tight before each run. A straight, taut string and a well-centred balloon give the straightest flights.

Why does a bigger balloon not always go further?

A bigger balloon holds more air, which helps, but it is also heavier and pushes more air out of its way. At some point the extra weight and air resistance cancel out the benefit of extra air. That trade-off is a genuine result worth reporting rather than hiding.

What is the action and what is the reaction here?

The action is air rushing out of the balloon neck in one direction. The reaction is the equal and opposite push driving the balloon the other way. The two forces act on different things, the air and the balloon, which is why both move in opposite directions.

How is this like a real rocket?

Real rockets also move by pushing material out one way and being pushed the other way, though they burn fuel to create hot gas instead of releasing air from a balloon. The balloon rocket is a model: it shows action and reaction clearly, but not fuel, steering, or flight in open air.
