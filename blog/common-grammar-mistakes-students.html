Common Grammar Mistakes Students Make (and How to Fix Them)

Grammar mistakes cost marks in every subject that involves writing, which is nearly all of them. The frustrating part is that most students keep making the same few errors. They are not making a hundred different mistakes. They are making about five mistakes, a hundred times each.

This guide targets the most frequent student errors with clear before-and-after examples, then gives you a self-editing checklist that catches them all in one pass.

Mistake 1: Subject-Verb Mismatch

The verb must agree with its subject, but students often match the verb to the nearest noun instead. Before: The list of items are on the table. After: The list of items is on the table. The subject is list, which is singular, even though items sits closer to the verb.

Fix it by finding the true subject first. Cross out prepositional phrases between the subject and verb, phrases like of items, in the box, with my friends, and the agreement usually becomes obvious. This single fix clears up a huge share of agreement errors. For the full rules, see Subject-Verb Agreement Explained with Simple Rules.

Mistake 2: Run-On Sentences

A run-on jams two complete thoughts together without proper connection. Before: The experiment worked we were all surprised. After: The experiment worked, and we were all surprised. Or: The experiment worked. We were all surprised.

Students create run-ons when they write the way they talk, stringing thoughts together with commas or nothing at all. The fix is mechanical. Read your draft and mark every place where a full sentence ends. Then check the join: a comma alone is never enough. You need a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a joining word like and, but, or so. Read the sentence aloud. If you naturally pause and take a breath where there is no punctuation, that is a run-on hiding.

Mistake 3: Tense Shifts

Tense shifts happen when a paragraph starts in one time and drifts into another. Before: She walked to the lab and picks up the sample. After: She walked to the lab and picked up the sample. The story is in the past, so every verb should stay in the past.

This mistake usually comes from editing. You rewrite one sentence, change its tense, and forget the neighbours. The fix is a consistency sweep. After drafting, scan your verbs and ask whether each one matches the time of the paragraph. Narratives about past events stay in past tense. General truths and explanations use present tense. Scientific reports describing methods use past tense, while conclusions often shift to present. Pick the right tense for the job and hold it steady.

Mistake 4: Misplaced Modifiers

A modifier must sit next to the word it describes, or it describes the wrong thing. Before: Covered in mud, the teacher saw the boots by the door. After: The teacher saw the boots, covered in mud, by the door. In the first version, the teacher is covered in mud.

These errors are funny to readers and costly in marks. The fix is a distance check. Find every descriptive phrase and ask what it touches. If a phrase can attach to two different words, move it until only one reading is possible. This check takes seconds per sentence and prevents some of the most embarrassing writing mistakes students make.

The Self-Editing Checklist

Write first, edit later, and edit in passes. Pass one: read only for run-ons, checking every sentence join. Pass two: scan verbs for tense consistency. Pass three: check subject-verb agreement by crossing out phrases between subject and verb. Pass four: check modifier placement.

Do not try to fix everything in one read. Your brain cannot hunt five error types at once, so give it one target per pass. The whole checklist takes about five minutes on a typical homework essay, and it catches the errors that separate good writing from sloppy writing. Keep the checklist visible while you edit until it becomes automatic.

FAQ

Why do I keep making the same grammar mistakes?

Because you have never trained the specific fix. Awareness alone does not change habits. Pick one error type, apply its fix deliberately for a week using the checklist, and only then move to the next one.

Should I fix grammar while I draft?

No. Drafting and editing use different mental modes. Write freely first, then run the checklist in passes. Fixing grammar mid-draft slows your thinking and usually produces worse ideas.

Do these mistakes matter in science and maths answers?

Yes. Examiners in every subject reward clear expression, and unclear grammar can make a correct idea look wrong. Short written explanations in science are marked partly on clarity.

How long until my writing is clean?

Most students see a clear drop in repeated errors within three to four weeks of using the checklist consistently. The errors do not vanish forever. They fade as the checklist becomes habit.

Putting It Together

Stop fighting a hundred imaginary mistakes and fix the real five. Match verbs to true subjects, join sentences properly, hold your tenses steady, and keep modifiers next to what they describe. Then run the four-pass checklist on every piece of writing. Cleaner writing is not talent. It is a system.
