AI Homework Scores Up, Exam Scores Down: What the New Study Really Means

AI homework scores are climbing in classrooms where students use generative AI, yet exam scores are falling — and a recent study reported in October 2026 says both things can be true at the same time. That sounds like a contradiction, but it makes sense once you understand what homework measures and what exams measure. Homework usually measures the quality of the finished work you hand in. Exams usually measure what you can actually do on your own, without help, under time pressure. When AI does part of the work, the first number can rise while the second one falls.

The findings come from the World Bank's World Development Report 2026, in a section on generative AI and learning. The study followed secondary-school students and reported that self-reported AI use rose from almost zero to around 80% between 2022 and 2025. Six months after students began using AI, their homework scores were up a reported 18%, while their monthly in-class examination scores fell a reported 20%. Students who had used AI also scored on average 5-7% lower in later school and college entrance examinations. These are striking numbers, but they come with important cautions that matter just as much as the numbers themselves.

Why AI Homework Scores Rose While Exam Scores Fell

The simplest explanation is also the most honest one: AI made the homework look better than the learning behind it. When a student pastes a question into an AI tool and submits a polished answer, the homework score reflects the AI's output, not the student's understanding. The student may never have wrestled with the hard part of the problem — the part where real learning happens. Then, in an exam room without AI, that missing practice shows up as a lower score. The homework went up because the work got help. The exam went down because the learning did not keep up.

Researchers also observed that students spent less time on homework even as their scores improved. That detail matters. Less time plus higher scores sounds like efficiency, but in learning, time spent struggling is often where understanding is built. When you skip the struggle, you skip the part that makes knowledge stick. A shortcut that saves ten minutes today can cost you far more in the exam hall later.

What the Study Actually Measured

It helps to be precise about what was measured. The study tracked self-reported AI use, homework scores, monthly in-class examination scores, and later entrance examination results for secondary-school students. Self-reported use means students themselves said whether and how much they used AI, which is useful but not perfect — people do not always report their habits accurately. Homework scores and exam scores measure different things, as described above, so a gap between them is a warning sign, not a mystery.

The study also compared students who had used AI with students who had not. That comparison produced the reported 5-7% gap in later entrance examinations. A gap like that is worth taking seriously, but it does not prove that AI alone caused every point of the difference. Students who use AI heavily may differ in other ways too — in motivation, in study habits, or in how much support they get at home. The study points to a real pattern, but it does not settle every question about cause and effect.

The Reported Numbers, With Their Caveats

Here is the part many headlines skip: this study has not yet been peer-reviewed, and it describes an early phase of AI adoption. Preliminary research is valuable — it tells us where to look — but it is not the final word. Early adoption phases are messy. Students, teachers, and schools were all figuring out these tools at the same time, often without clear guidance on how to use them well. The results describe what happened when AI arrived quickly and largely unguided, not what must happen when AI is used carefully.

That is why the report's own framing matters. The report's team noted that the evidence looks more encouraging when AI is used by teachers — for example, to personalise instruction — than when it is used directly by students. In other words, the same technology can help or hurt depending on who uses it and how. A teacher using AI to adapt explanations, spot common misunderstandings, and give better feedback is using the tool to strengthen teaching. A student using AI to skip the work is using the same tool to weaken learning. The tool is not the whole story. The way it is used is the story.

Why Spending Less Time on Homework Matters

The finding that students spent less time on homework deserves a closer look, because it explains the mechanism behind the numbers. Learning a subject is not mainly about producing correct answers. It is about building mental pathways: connecting a new idea to what you already know, practising it until it becomes familiar, and learning to spot your own mistakes. That work takes effort, and effort takes time. When AI removes the effort, it can also remove the learning.

Think of it like watching someone else lift weights. You can watch perfect form all day, but your muscles only grow when you lift. Homework is the lifting. If AI lifts for you, your homework looks strong while your understanding stays weak. The exam is the moment you have to lift on your own. This is why students who felt confident about their homework were sometimes surprised by their exam results — their confidence was based on assisted work, not independent skill.

What This Means for How You Study

The study is not a reason to panic, and it is not a reason to ban AI from your life. It is a reason to change how you use it. Use AI after you have tried the problem yourself, not before. Use it to explain a concept you found confusing, to check your reasoning, or to quiz you on material you have already studied. Do not use it to produce the answer you submit. If you cannot explain your homework answer without looking at the screen, you have not learned it yet — no matter what score it received.

Teachers and parents can help by asking a simple question: can you show me how you got this answer? That question separates assisted output from real understanding. And students can help themselves by treating homework as practice, not performance. The score on a piece of homework is a rough signal. The real test is whether you could solve a similar problem tomorrow, alone, without help.

Conclusion

The study's message is uncomfortable but useful: AI can raise your homework scores while lowering your exam scores if it replaces your thinking instead of supporting it. The findings are preliminary and describe an early, largely unguided phase of AI adoption, so they are a warning, not a verdict. Used wisely — after your own effort, as a tutor and checker rather than an answer machine — AI can still be a genuine study aid. The goal is not a higher homework score. The goal is knowledge that stays with you when the screen is gone.

FAQ

Does using AI for homework always lower exam scores?

No. The study reported an average pattern, not a rule for every student. It described an early phase of AI adoption in which many students used AI to get answers directly. Students who use AI as a tutor — explaining concepts, checking reasoning, quizzing — may see very different results. The key difference is whether AI replaces your effort or supports it.

Why did homework scores go up if learning went down?

Because homework scores measure the finished work, not the learning behind it. AI can produce polished, correct-looking answers, so the submitted work earns a higher mark. Exams measure what you can do alone, without help. When AI did the thinking, the homework improved while the underlying understanding did not.

Is this study proven?

Not yet. It has not been peer-reviewed, and it covers an early phase of AI adoption, so the findings are preliminary. That does not mean they should be ignored — the pattern is consistent and the mechanism is plausible — but it does mean we should treat the numbers as early evidence, not as settled fact.

How can I use AI without hurting my exam results?

Try every problem yourself first. Then use AI to explain what confused you, to check your working, or to quiz you on the topic. Never submit an answer you cannot explain on your own.
