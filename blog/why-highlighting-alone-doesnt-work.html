Why Highlighting Alone Doesn't Work (and What to Do Instead)

Open almost any student's textbook and you will find pages glowing with colour. Highlighting is the most popular study technique in the world, and also one of the weakest. It feels productive, looks impressive, and yet often leaves students unable to recall what they marked. Understanding why it fails is the first step to replacing it with methods that actually work.

This article explains the problem honestly, shows when a small amount of highlighting can still help, and gives you a practical routine that turns marked pages into real knowledge.

Why Highlighting Feels Like Learning

Highlighting feels effective because it creates fluency. As your pen moves across a sentence, the words pass through your attention and feel familiar. Later, when you see the highlighted sentence again, recognition kicks in instantly, and recognition feels like knowledge. The brain confuses I have seen this before with I know this.

The trouble appears when the text is taken away. A test does not show you highlighted sentences and ask whether they look familiar. It asks you to produce ideas, explanations, and solutions from memory, and highlighting never practised that skill. Fluency during study is a poor predictor of recall during a test.

The Over-Marking Trap

Most students highlight far too much. Early in a chapter, everything seems important, so large blocks turn yellow. By the end, half the page glows, and no idea stands out from any other. A page where everything is emphasised emphasises nothing.

Over-marking also happens at the wrong time. Students usually highlight while reading for the first time, before they know which ideas matter most. They mark details that later prove minor and miss the central idea that ties the section together. Highlighting first and understanding second puts the tool before the thinking.

When Highlighting Can Still Help

Highlighting is not useless; it is simply incomplete. It can help in two narrow situations. First, as a navigation aid: a few marks can help you find key definitions or formulas quickly during revision. Second, as a first step toward active work: a marked idea can become a question, a flashcard, or a summary point.

The rule is selectivity. Mark only after reading a section, and mark only the ideas you would need to answer questions about it. If more than a small fraction of a page is marked, you have marked too much. Put the pen down and ask which three ideas truly matter.

The Missing Step: Turn Marks Into Tests

The real fix is to convert every highlight into a test of yourself. For each marked idea, write a question on a separate card or sheet, with the answer hidden. What does this term mean? Why does this process happen? How would you solve this type of problem? Then answer from memory before checking.

This single change transforms highlighting from passive marking into active recall. The highlight becomes a prompt rather than a decoration, and each attempt strengthens the memory it points to. Students who test themselves on marked material remember far more than students who simply re-read the marks.

A Better Routine: Read, Mark, Test, Review

Use this four-step routine for any chapter. First, read the section without marking, focusing on understanding the main ideas. Second, mark only a few key points, ideally no more than a handful per section. Third, close the book and turn each mark into a question you answer from memory. Fourth, review your mistakes: note which questions you missed and restudy those ideas before your next session.

Repeat the test step after a gap of a day or more. Spaced retrieval is what converts short-term familiarity into lasting knowledge, and it reveals which ideas still need work while there is time to fix them.

Combining Highlighting With Other Methods

Highlighting works best as one small part of a larger toolkit. Pair it with summaries written from memory, practice problems, and teaching the idea aloud to someone else. Each method exercises the same knowledge differently, and the combination is far stronger than any single technique.

If you enjoy highlighting, keep it, but demote it. Let it be the first five minutes of a study session, not the whole session. The learning happens in the testing and thinking that follow the marks, not in the marks themselves.

FAQ

Should I stop highlighting completely?

Not necessarily. Keep highlighting small and selective, and always follow it with self-testing. The problem is highlighting alone, not highlighting as a minor step.

How much of a page should I highlight?

Aim for a small fraction: a few key terms, definitions, or conclusions per section. If you cannot quickly say why each mark matters, you have marked too much.

Is highlighting better than nothing?

It beats doing nothing, because it at least directs attention to the text. But it is far weaker than self-testing, and students with limited time should spend that time on recall practice instead.

What should I do with old highlighted notes?

Turn them into questions now. Work through your marked pages, write a question for each mark, and test yourself. This rescues the value of past highlighting without requiring you to re-read everything.

Conclusion

Highlighting alone does not work because it builds recognition instead of recall, and because over-marking buries the ideas that matter.
