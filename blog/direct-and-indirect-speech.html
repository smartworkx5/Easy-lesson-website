Direct and Indirect Speech Explained with Examples

Direct speech reports the exact words someone said. Indirect speech reports what someone said without repeating the exact words. Learning both forms helps you write dialogue, summarise conversations, and report information accurately.

This guide explains direct and indirect speech with examples, covers reporting verbs and tense shifts, and shows how pronouns, time words, and question forms change.

What Is Direct Speech?

Direct speech uses quotation marks to show the exact words of a speaker. "I am ready," said Leila. The words inside the quotation marks are exactly what Leila said. The reporting clause, said Leila, tells the reader who spoke. A comma usually separates the quoted words from the reporting clause.

Direct speech is common in stories and conversations. It makes writing lively because the reader hears the speaker's own voice. When you write direct speech, start a new paragraph each time a new person speaks. Keep the punctuation inside the quotation marks and use a capital letter at the start of the quoted sentence.

Direct speech can also include thoughts and short exclamations. "Wow! That is a clever idea," said Omar. The exclamation mark stays inside the quotation marks. If the reporting clause comes first, use a comma after it: Leila said, "I am ready." These punctuation patterns are consistent, so once you learn them, you can apply them to any dialogue.

What Is Indirect Speech?

Indirect speech, also called reported speech, reports the meaning of what someone said without using their exact words. Leila said that she was ready. There are no quotation marks. The word that often introduces the reported clause, although it can sometimes be left out: Leila said she was ready.

Indirect speech is common in summaries, reports, and retellings. It helps you condense a long conversation into a few sentences. Instead of quoting every line, you report the main points. For example, a long discussion can become: "The group agreed that the plan was clear and that they would start the next day." This form is efficient and keeps the focus on meaning rather than exact wording.

When you change direct speech to indirect speech, you usually need to change pronouns, verb tense, and time words. The changes depend on whether the reporting verb is in the past or the present. If the reporting verb is in the present, the tense often stays the same. If it is in the past, the tense usually shifts back one step.

Reporting Verbs and Tense Shift

Reporting verbs introduce indirect speech. Common reporting verbs include said, told, asked, explained, promised, and suggested. Said and told have different patterns. You say something to someone, but you tell someone something. "She said that the lesson was clear" and "She told us that the lesson was clear" are both correct. Told needs a listener, while said does not.

When the reporting verb is in the past, the tense in the reported clause usually shifts back. Present simple becomes past simple: "I like this book" becomes "She said that she liked that book." Present continuous becomes past continuous: "I am reading" becomes "She said that she was reading." Present perfect becomes past perfect: "I have finished" becomes "She said that she had finished." Past simple becomes past perfect: "I finished" becomes "She said that she had finished."

Some verb forms do not change. Past perfect stays past perfect. Modal verbs such as could, would, might, and should usually stay the same. Will becomes would, and can becomes could. For example, "I will help you" becomes "She said that she would help me." Learning these shifts step by step is easier than memorising them all at once. Practise with one tense at a time.

Pronouns, Time Words, and Place Words

Pronouns change to match the new speaker and listener. "I am happy" spoken by Sara becomes "Sara said that she was happy." The first-person pronoun I becomes she because the report is about Sara. Similarly, "We will start" becomes "They said that they would start" when reported by someone else. Always check pronouns from the perspective of the reporter, not the original speaker.

Time and place words also change. Now becomes then, today becomes that day, tomorrow becomes the next day, yesterday becomes the previous day, here becomes there, and this becomes that. For example, "I will see you tomorrow" becomes "He said that he would see me the next day." These changes keep the report accurate from the new time and place of reporting.

If the reported statement is a general truth, the tense may stay the same. "The teacher said that water boils at a high temperature" keeps present simple because the fact is always true. This exception applies when the statement is still true and general. When in doubt, check whether the statement describes a timeless fact or a specific past event.

Questions, Commands, and Requests

Questions change differently in indirect speech. Yes-no questions use if or whether. "Are you ready?" becomes "She asked if I was ready." Wh-questions keep the question word. "Where is the library?" becomes "He asked where the library was." The word order changes to statement order. There is no question mark in indirect speech, and the auxiliary verb moves after the subject.

Commands and requests use an infinitive. "Close the door" becomes "She asked me to close the door." "Please help me" becomes "He asked me to help him." Negative commands use not plus the infinitive: "Do not run" becomes "She told us not to run." Verbs such as ordered, advised, and requested can replace asked to show the tone of the command.

Suggestions and offers use other patterns. "Let's start" can become "She suggested starting" or "She suggested that we start." "I will help you" as an offer becomes "She offered to help me." Learning these patterns helps you report conversations accurately. For more on punctuation in dialogue, see Punctuation Rules Every Student Needs. When you practise with textbook exercises, verify your reported sentences against the textbook. AI tools can suggest conversions, but they can make mistakes, so check the tense shift and pronouns yourself.

FAQ

What is the difference between direct and indirect speech?

Direct speech repeats the exact words inside quotation marks. Indirect speech reports the meaning without exact words and without quotation marks. Direct speech shows voice, while indirect speech shows meaning.

When does the tense shift in indirect speech?

When the reporting verb is in the past, the reported verb usually shifts back one step. Present becomes past, past becomes past perfect, and will becomes would. If the reporting verb is in the present, the tense often stays the same.

How do I report a question in indirect speech?

Use if or whether for yes-no questions and keep the question word for wh-questions. Change the word order to statement order and end with a full stop, not a question mark.

Do time words always change in indirect speech?

Time words usually change when the report happens at a different time or place. Tomorrow becomes the next day and yesterday becomes the previous day. If the report happens at the same time, the words may stay the same.

Conclusion

Direct speech shows exact words. Indirect speech shows meaning. Mastering both forms means learning reporting verbs, tense shifts, pronoun changes, and question patterns. Practise with short dialogues, check each change, and build up to longer conversations. With steady practice, reported speech will feel natural.
