Ratios and proportions are two of the most practical ideas in maths. A ratio compares two quantities. A proportion says two ratios are equal. Those two sentences are almost the whole topic — everything else is practice. Ratios show up in recipes, in maps, in mixing paint, and in sharing money fairly. This guide builds the topic through recipe scaling, the most natural ratio situation of all, with worked examples for simplifying, sharing, and solving proportions.

Ratios and Proportions: What a Ratio Is

A ratio compares two or more quantities in order. The ratio 2:3 (read "two to three") means for every 2 of the first thing, there are 3 of the second. In a fruit bowl with 2 apples for every 3 oranges, the ratio of apples to oranges is 2:3. Order matters: the ratio of oranges to apples would be 3:2, a different statement.

Ratios are not fractions, but they are close cousins. The ratio 2:3 says the first quantity is 2/5 of the total and the second is 3/5, because there are 2 + 3 = 5 parts in total. Keeping the total in mind is the key to sharing problems, as you will see below.

Ratios compare quantities in the same units. A ratio of 2 cm to 3 cm makes sense; a ratio of 2 cm to 3 hours does not, until the units are converted to match. Always check the units first. A ratio of 500 m to 2 km must be converted first: 2 km = 2000 m, so the ratio is 500:2000, which simplifies to 1:4.

Worked Example: Simplifying Ratios

Ratios, like fractions, should be simplified by dividing both parts by the same number. Simplify 12:18. Both numbers divide by 6: 12 divided by 6 is 2, and 18 divided by 6 is 3. So 12:18 simplifies to 2:3. The simplified ratio carries the same information in its neatest form.

Try another: simplify 15:25. Both numbers divide by 5: 15 divided by 5 is 3, and 25 divided by 5 is 5. So 15:25 = 3:5. If both numbers share no factor except 1, the ratio is already in simplest form — for example, 7:9 cannot be simplified.

Simplifying first makes later work easier. A recipe ratio of 12:18 is awkward to scale; the simplified 2:3 is far easier to double or triple. Always simplify before you scale or share.

Worked Example: Recipe Scaling

A recipe uses 2 cups of flour for every 1 cup of sugar — a ratio of 2:1. You want to make a triple batch. Multiply both parts by 3: flour becomes 2 × 3 = 6 cups, and sugar becomes 1 × 3 = 3 cups. The ratio stays 2:1 in spirit, because 6:3 simplifies back to 2:1. Scaling a recipe means multiplying every ingredient by the same number, which keeps all the ratios identical.

Scaling down works the same way. To make half the recipe, multiply both parts by 1/2: flour becomes 1 cup and sugar becomes 1/2 cup. The taste stays the same because the balance between ingredients has not changed. Only the total amount changes.

Real recipes have many ingredients, but the rule never changes: one multiplier for everything. If a sauce uses oil, vinegar, and herbs in the ratio 3:1:1, doubling the batch means 6:2:2. A cook who doubles only the oil and forgets the vinegar has broken the ratio — and the sauce will show it.

Worked Example: Sharing in a Given Ratio

Two friends share 30 tokens in the ratio 3:2. How many does each get? First add the parts: 3 + 2 = 5 parts in total. Then find the value of one part: 30 divided by 5 = 6. Finally, multiply: the first friend gets 3 × 6 = 18, and the second gets 2 × 6 = 12. Check: 18 + 12 = 30, and 18:12 simplifies to 3:2. Both checks pass.

The "total parts" step is the heart of the method, and skipping it is the main cause of errors. Without it, students often guess 15 each (an equal split) or divide by the wrong number. Always add the parts first.

Try a three-part share: split 36 counters in the ratio 4:5. Add the parts: 4 + 5 = 9. One part is 36 divided by 9 = 4. The shares are 4 × 4 = 16 and 5 × 4 = 20. Check: 16 + 20 = 36, and 16:20 simplifies to 4:5. Correct.

Worked Example: Solving a Proportion

A proportion is an equation between two ratios. Solve 3/4 = x/12. Cross-multiply: 3 × 12 = 4 × x, so 36 = 4x. Divide both sides by 4: x = 9. Check: 9/12 simplifies to 3/4, so the proportion balances.

Why does cross-multiplication work? Multiplying both sides of 3/4 = x/12 by 12 clears the denominators, leaving 3 × 12 / 4 = x, and multiplying further clears the remaining denominator to give 3 × 12 = 4x. It is the balance method from equation solving, compressed into one step.

Proportions solve real scaling problems. If 3 litres of paint cover 4 walls, how many litres cover 12 walls? Set up 3/4 = x/12, cross-multiply to get 36 = 4x, and find x = 9 litres. The proportion keeps the rate identical while the quantities grow.

Common Mistakes to Avoid with Ratios

Advertisement

The most common mistake is ignoring the order of a ratio. "The ratio of boys to girls is 2:3" and "the ratio of girls to boys is 2:3" say opposite things. Always read which quantity comes first and keep the same order in your working.

Another mistake is forgetting to add the parts when sharing. Sharing 30 in the ratio 3:2 is not 30 divided by 3 and 30 divided by 2 — it is 30 divided by the total parts (5), then multiplied by each part. The total-parts step is what makes the shares add up to the original amount.

A third mistake is adding instead of multiplying when scaling. Doubling a 2:1 recipe gives 4:2, not 3:2. Every part must be multiplied by the same factor. Adding the same number to both parts changes the ratio, which changes the result.

Try It Yourself: Practice with Answers

Try these three problems, then check your answers below.

  1. Share 36 counters in the ratio 4:5.
  2. Simplify the ratio 15:25.
  3. Solve the proportion 2/3 = 8/x.

Answers: (1) Total parts = 9, one part = 36 ÷ 9 = 4, shares = 16 and 20. (2) Divide both by 5: 3:5. (3) Cross-multiply: 2x = 24, so x = 12. Check: 8/12 simplifies to 2/3. If all three are right, you have the core skills. If the sharing problem felt tricky, write the total-parts step on its own line every time.

Conclusion

A ratio compares quantities in order; a proportion says two ratios are equal. Simplify ratios by dividing both parts by the same number, scale recipes by multiplying every part by the same factor, share amounts by adding the parts first, and solve proportions by cross-multiplying. Keep the order of the ratio straight and never skip the total-parts step. With those habits, ratios and proportions become reliable everyday tools rather than a source of confusion.

FAQ

What is a ratio?

A ratio compares two or more quantities in order, written with a colon, like 2:3. It means for every 2 of the first quantity, there are 3 of the second. Order matters: 2:3 and 3:2 describe different situations.

What is a proportion?

A proportion is an equation stating that two ratios are equal, such as 3/4 = 9/12. Proportions are solved by cross-multiplication: multiply the numerator of each side by the denominator of the other side, then solve the resulting equation.

How do you share an amount in a given ratio?

Add the parts of the ratio to find the total number of parts, divide the amount by that total to find one part, then multiply by each part of the ratio. To share 30 in the ratio 3:2, one part is 30 ÷ 5 = 6, so the shares are 18 and 12.

Can you simplify a ratio like a fraction?

Yes. Divide both parts of the ratio by the same number until they share no common factor except 1. For example, 12:18 simplifies to 2:3 by dividing both parts by 6. The simplified ratio means exactly the same thing.

Why does scaling a recipe keep the ratios the same?

Because every ingredient is multiplied by the same factor. Tripling a 2:1 recipe gives 6:3, which simplifies back to 2:1. The balance between ingredients is unchanged, so the result tastes the same — only the total amount grows.
