Solving Linear Equations Step by Step

Solving linear equations is a puzzle with a single unknown, and the balance method is the reliable way to solve it. The idea is simple: an equation is like a balanced scale, and every move you make must keep both sides equal. This guide walks through the method from easy one-step equations to trickier ones with brackets and variables on both sides, with fully worked examples and checks at every stage.

Solving Linear Equations with the Balance Method

The equals sign means both sides have the same value. If you add 5 to one side, you must add 5 to the other. If you divide one side by 3, you must divide the other by 3. This "do the same to both sides" rule is the entire method. Every technique in this article is just a careful application of it.

Picture a balance scale. On the left pan sits the expression with x; on the right pan sits a number. Your goal is to leave x alone on the left pan. Each move removes or simplifies something from both pans equally, so the scale stays balanced until x stands alone and its value is revealed.

The order of undoing matters. Work backwards through the operations applied to x: undo addition and subtraction first if they are the outermost layer, then multiplication and division. Think of unwrapping a parcel — the last layer added is the first one removed. This keeps the numbers simple and the working tidy.

Worked Example: 4x + 3 = 19

In 4x + 3 = 19, x has been multiplied by 4 and then 3 has been added. Undo in reverse order. First, subtract 3 from both sides: 4x + 3 − 3 = 19 − 3, which simplifies to 4x = 16. Then divide both sides by 4: 4x divided by 4 is x, and 16 divided by 4 is 4. So x = 4.

Now check by substituting x = 4 back into the original equation: 4 times 4 is 16, plus 3 is 19. That matches the right-hand side, so the answer is correct. The check is not optional decoration — it is the fastest way to catch a slip, and it takes only a few seconds.

Notice how each step has a reason. Subtracting 3 removes the constant from the left. Dividing by 4 removes the coefficient from the left. When you can say why each step happens, you are no longer guessing; you are solving.

Worked Example: 2x − 5 = 11

In 2x − 5 = 11, x has been multiplied by 2 and then 5 has been subtracted. Undo in reverse: first add 5 to both sides, because adding is the opposite of subtracting. That gives 2x = 16. Then divide both sides by 2, giving x = 8.

Check: 2 times 8 is 16, minus 5 is 11. Correct. Compare this with the previous example: the structure is identical (multiply, then add or subtract), and the method is identical too. Recognising that most equations follow a small set of patterns is what makes algebra feel manageable.

A useful habit is to write each step on its own line, with the operation noted. When working is laid out line by line, a teacher can follow it, and you can spot a mistake by re-reading. Messy working hides errors; tidy working exposes them.

Worked Example: Equations with Brackets, 3(x + 2) = 21

Brackets mean "do this group first", so they need care. In 3(x + 2) = 21, the bracket (x + 2) is multiplied by 3. The cleanest route is to divide both sides by 3 first: 3(x + 2) divided by 3 leaves x + 2, and 21 divided by 3 is 7. So x + 2 = 7. Then subtract 2 from both sides: x = 5.

Check: x + 2 = 7, and 3 times 7 is 21. Correct. An alternative route is to expand the bracket first: 3x + 6 = 21, then subtract 6 to get 3x = 15, then divide by 3 to get x = 5. Both routes give the same answer. The divide-first route is shorter when the numbers divide neatly; the expand-first route works even when they do not. Learn both and choose the neater one for each problem.

Brackets also appear with a minus sign in front, as in 10 − (x + 3) = 4. Here the bracket is subtracted as a group, so removing it changes the signs inside: 10 − x − 3 = 4, which simplifies to 7 − x = 4, so x = 3. If bracket signs confuse you, expand carefully one step at a time and check at the end.

Equations with Variables on Both Sides

Sometimes x appears on both sides, as in 5x + 2 = 3x + 10. The goal is the same — get x alone — but first gather all the x terms on one side and all the numbers on the other. Subtract 3x from both sides: 5x − 3x + 2 = 10, which simplifies to 2x + 2 = 10. Then subtract 2 from both sides: 2x = 8. Then divide by 2: x = 4.

Check: left side 5 times 4 plus 2 is 22; right side 3 times 4 plus 10 is 22. Both sides match, so x = 4 is correct. The check is especially important here because there are more steps where a slip can hide.

A good convention is to gather the x terms on the side where the coefficient ends up positive. If subtracting gives a negative coefficient, you can still solve it, but positive coefficients are easier to handle. For example, in 2x + 9 = 7x + 4, subtract 2x from both sides to get 9 = 5x + 4, then subtract 4 to get 5 = 5x, so x = 1. Check: 2 + 9 = 11 and 7 + 4 = 11. Correct.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Solving Equations

Advertisement

The most common mistake is changing only one side. If you subtract 3 from the left, subtract 3 from the right too. An equation that is unbalanced partway through will produce a wrong answer that fails the check.

Another mistake is undoing in the wrong order. In 4x + 3 = 19, dividing by 4 first gives x + 0.75 = 4.75, which is solvable but invites fraction errors. Undo the outermost operation first (the +3), then the multiplication. Reverse order keeps the numbers whole.

A third mistake is a sign slip when moving terms across the equals sign. When a term crosses the equals sign, its sign flips: +3 becomes −3, and −5 becomes +5. This "change sides, change signs" shortcut is exactly the same as doing the same operation to both sides, just written faster. If the shortcut ever confuses you, go back to writing out both sides in full.

Try It Yourself: Practice with Answers

Try these three problems, then check your answers below.

  1. Solve 5x + 2 = 27.
  2. Solve 3x − 7 = 8.
  3. Solve 2(x − 3) = 10.

Answers: (1) Subtract 2: 5x = 25, divide by 5: x = 5. Check: 25 + 2 = 27. (2) Add 7: 3x = 15, divide by 3: x = 5. Check: 15 − 7 = 8. (3) Divide by 2: x − 3 = 5, add 3: x = 8. Check: 2 times 5 = 10. If all three check out, the balance method is yours. If one failed the check, re-read the step where the sides stopped matching.

Conclusion

Solving a linear equation means isolating x while keeping both sides equal. Undo the outermost operation first, work in reverse order, gather x terms on one side when they appear on both, and always check by substituting your answer back in. With brackets, divide or expand first — whichever keeps the numbers neater. Practise the method on one-step, two-step, bracket, and both-sides equations until each type feels routine, and algebra will stop feeling like guesswork.

FAQ

What is a linear equation?

A linear equation is an equation where the variable appears to the power of 1 — no squares, no cubes, no variables in denominators. Examples are 4x + 3 = 19 and 2x − 5 = 11. Linear equations have exactly one solution (unless they are identities, which are true for every value).

Why must you do the same thing to both sides?

Because the equals sign means both sides have the same value. Doing something to one side only breaks that equality, like removing weight from one pan of a balanced scale. Keeping every operation matched on both sides is what keeps the equation true throughout.

In what order do you undo operations?

Undo in reverse order: the outermost operation first. In 4x + 3 = 19, the +3 was applied last, so subtract 3 first, then divide by 4. Think of unwrapping a parcel: the last layer added is the first one removed.

How do you solve an equation with x on both sides?

Gather all the x terms on one side and all the numbers on the other by adding or subtracting the same amount on both sides. For 5x + 2 = 3x + 10, subtract 3x from both sides to get 2x + 2 = 10, then solve as usual to get x = 4.

How do you check an equation answer?

Substitute your answer back into the original equation and evaluate both sides. If they match, the answer is correct. For x = 4 in 4x + 3 = 19, the left side is 4 times 4 plus 3 = 19, which matches the right side, so x = 4 is verified.
