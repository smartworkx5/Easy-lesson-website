Planning Lessons for Different Levels in One Class

Every class contains a range of levels. Some students arrive already confident with the topic. Others are still building the foundations. Most sit somewhere in between. A lesson planned for the middle alone will bore the first group and lose the second. Planning for different levels, often called differentiation, does not mean writing three separate lessons. It means designing one lesson with a shared core, flexible tasks, and smart support, so every student works at a level that stretches them. This guide shows you how to do that without doubling your workload.

Start From One Shared Core

The foundation of a mixed-level lesson is a single shared core that everyone learns together. The core is the essential idea or skill of the lesson, stated as one clear objective. Every student, whatever their level, works toward that core. Around it, you build layers: extra support for students who need it, and extra challenge for students who are ready. The shared core matters because it keeps the class together. Students hear the same explanation, discuss the same examples, and share a common language for the topic. Without it, a class fragments into separate groups doing separate work, and the teacher cannot teach the room. A useful test is: can I state the core in one sentence that applies to every student? If yes, you have a core. If the lesson splits into unrelated tasks, step back and find the shared idea that holds them together.

Tier Tasks by Challenge, Not by Amount

The most common differentiation mistake is giving stronger students more work and weaker students less work. More of the same is not challenge, and less of the same is not support. Instead, tier tasks by challenge: same topic, different depth. A simple three-tier shape works well. The core task is the standard version every student attempts. The support version breaks the same task into smaller steps, adds a worked example, or provides sentence starters. The extension version asks students to explain why, to solve a harder case, or to apply the idea somewhere new. All three tiers practise the same goal, so the class stays together while each student works at the right level. Write the tiers as one task with options, not as three separate worksheets. That keeps preparation manageable and avoids labelling students by handing out visibly different sheets.

Use Flexible Grouping

Grouping is a powerful tool when it is flexible, and a harmful one when it is fixed. Fixed groups based on level quickly become labels, and students in the lower group stop believing they can move up. Flexible grouping means students work in different arrangements for different purposes across the week. Sometimes pair a stronger student with one who needs support, so both benefit: explaining is one of the best ways to deepen understanding. Sometimes group students of similar level together, so you can give one group focused coaching while the other tackles a challenge task. Sometimes let students choose their own partners for a short task. The key is that no grouping is permanent, and students regularly work with different classmates. Plan grouping into the lesson in advance, and keep transitions short. A grouping plan written as one line, such as "pairs for task one, mixed groups of four for task two," is enough.

Build Support Into the Lesson, Not After It

Support works best when it is built into the lesson from the start, not added as an afterthought for students who are already struggling. Practical built-in supports include a worked example on the board that students can refer to, a short word bank or formula list, step-by-step task cards, and a clear model of the finished product. These supports help every student, not only the ones who struggle, because they reduce confusion about what to do. Another built-in support is the order of the task itself: start with a guided example the whole class does together, then move to paired work, then to independent work. That gradual release lets students build confidence before they work alone. When you circulate during independent work, spend your time with the students who need coaching, knowing the rest have the scaffolds they need to keep going.

Challenge the Students Who Are Ready

Students who finish quickly do not need more of the same task; they need a task that makes them think harder. Plan one genuine extension for every lesson, and make it a normal part of the class, not a special reward. Good extensions deepen the same goal: "Explain why this method works," "Find a second way to solve it," "What happens if we change this number?" or "Create a question that would catch someone out." Extensions should be visible to everyone, so any student can attempt them, not only those the teacher picks. This avoids the problem of students hiding their speed to avoid extra work. It also keeps fast finishers engaged without creating a separate track. If an extension turns out to be too easy, the next one should be harder. Challenge is not a punishment; it is a sign that you take a student's ability seriously.

Check Understanding at Every Level

A mixed-level lesson needs a check that tells you how every group did, not just the confident few. Use checks that let all students respond: a short written task, an exit ticket, or a quick problem solved alone. Then read the responses by level. Did the students with support meet the core objective? Did the extension group meet it and go further? If a group missed the core, plan a short revisit in the next lesson rather than moving on and leaving them behind. The check also tells you whether your tiers were pitched right. If everyone found the core task easy, it was too easy. If everyone struggled, the core or the support needs adjusting. Over a few weeks, these checks give you a much sharper picture of each student's level than any single test. Pair this with a strong close using these lesson closure activities.

Conclusion

Planning for different levels in one class comes down to a shared core, tiered tasks, flexible grouping, built-in support, genuine challenge, and a check that covers every group. You do not need three lessons. You need one lesson designed with layers, so every student works at a level that stretches them and nobody is left behind or held back.

FAQ

Does differentiation mean writing a separate lesson for each level?

No. One lesson with a shared core and tiered tasks is enough. Separate lessons triple your workload and split the class. Layers within one lesson keep everyone together while letting each student work at the right level.

How do I avoid labelling students with tiered tasks?

Make the tiers options within one task, not visibly different worksheets, and let any student attempt the extension. Use flexible grouping so students work with different classmates across the week. Avoid fixed level groups with permanent names.

What if I have a very wide range of levels?

Keep the shared core as simple as possible and make the support and extension layers strong. A clear core, a worked example, and one good extension cover most ranges. For the extremes, a short one-to-one check-in during independent work helps more than extra worksheets.

How do I know if my tiers are pitched correctly?

Use the end-of-lesson check. If the support group meets the core objective and the extension group is stretched, the tiers are right. If everyone finds the work easy or everyone struggles, adjust the core and the tiers for the next lesson.
