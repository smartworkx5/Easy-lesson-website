Multitasking While Studying: Why It Slows You Down

By Easy Lesson Team · Published October 26, 2026 · 7 min read

It feels productive to answer a message while reading, keep a video playing in the background, and switch between two assignments at once. You are doing more things, so you must be getting more done. But the brain does not truly multitask when the tasks need attention. It switches rapidly between them, and every switch carries a cost that quietly slows your learning down.

That cost has a name: switch cost. Each time you move attention from one task to another, your brain must unload the first task, load the second, and find its place again. The switch takes only seconds, but those seconds add up, and worse, the quality of your attention drops after every switch. You return to the study material with a shallower focus than you left it with, so the same page takes longer to understand and is remembered less well.

What Switch Cost Looks Like in a Study Session

Switch cost is easy to observe once you watch for it. You read a paragraph, check a message, and return to the page only to realise you have to re-read the paragraph. You solve half a problem, answer a notification, and then stare at the problem trying to remember where you were. Each return feels like starting slightly cold, because you are.

Over a full session, these small costs compound. A student who switches every few minutes can lose a large share of the session to re-orienting, without ever noticing a single big interruption. The session feels busy and tiring, because switching is mentally expensive, yet very little durable learning results from it. Tiredness without progress is the signature of a multitasked session.

Switching also damages memory directly. Learning depends on sustained attention: holding an idea in mind long enough to connect it to what you already know. Constant switching breaks those connections before they form, so material that was studied in fragments is remembered in fragments, if at all.

The Myth of the Good Multitasker

Some people believe they are exceptions, that they can multitask without cost. Research consistently finds otherwise. Even people who multitask frequently and feel confident about it show the same switch costs on attention-demanding tasks. Feeling comfortable with switching is not the same as being unaffected by it.

It helps to distinguish true multitasking from background habits. Listening to familiar music while doing routine work is very different from switching between a study task and a conversation, because a conversation demands attention and decisions. The test is simple: if the second activity needs your thinking, it is competing with your study, not accompanying it.

Background video is a common trap. A video you glance at occasionally still pulls attention at every glance, and the plot or commentary occupies working memory that your study material needs. If you need sound, familiar instrumental music is a safer choice, and silence is safest of all for difficult material.

How to Study One Thing at a Time

The fix is single-tasking: one task, one screen, one set of materials, for a defined block of time. Put the phone in another room or inside a bag, not face down on the desk, because a phone you can reach is a phone you will check. Close every tab and app unrelated to the current task. Tell yourself the messages will wait, because they will, and a focused block is usually shorter than the scattered session it replaces.

Work in focused blocks with planned breaks. Study one topic for a set period, then take a short break where switching is allowed: check messages, stretch, get water. When the break ends, return to a single task. This structure works because it respects the urge to switch instead of fighting it all evening, while keeping the study blocks themselves clean.

If two assignments both need attention, finish one before starting the other, or split them into separate blocks with a break between. Alternating paragraph by paragraph between two subjects is not efficient studying. It is two half-attention sessions wearing the costume of one busy evening.

What to Do About Genuine Interruptions

Not every interruption is a choice. Messages from family, shared rooms, and noisy surroundings are real constraints, and the goal is to reduce switching, not to achieve impossible silence. If interruptions are frequent, shorten your blocks. A clean fifteen minutes beats a scattered hour, and several short clean blocks add up to real learning.

Batch your replies. Instead of answering each message as it arrives, collect them for the next break and answer them together. Most messages do not need an instant reply, and the people messaging you will rarely notice a delay of half an hour. What you gain is the deep attention that difficult material requires.

Finally, notice your own switching triggers. If you always reach for the phone at the first hard paragraph, the phone is not the problem, the difficulty is. When you catch that urge, try staying with the hard paragraph for two more minutes before taking a break. Often the difficulty passes, and you discover the switch was an escape, not a need.

FAQ

Is listening to music while studying multitasking?

It depends on the music. Familiar, low-key music usually interferes little, but music with lyrics, a video, or anything you actively follow competes for attention. For difficult material, silence or very familiar background sound is the safer choice.

What if I need to look something up while studying?

Looking up something directly related to the current task is part of that task, not a switch. The problem is when the lookup opens a chain of unrelated browsing. Look it up, note the answer, and close the extra tabs before continuing.

How long should a single-task study block be?

Start with twenty to thirty minutes of uninterrupted work, then a short break. As your focus stamina grows, you can extend the blocks. The right length is the longest block you can genuinely sustain without switching, not the longest you can endure while miserable.

Does multitasking affect test results?

Yes, because it weakens the attention that builds durable memory. Material studied in fragments is remembered in fragments, and test questions require recall and application that fragmented study rarely supports. Single-task sessions produce stronger, more usable memory.

Conclusion

Multitasking feels efficient because you are busy, but every switch charges a cost in time, attention, and memory. Single-task study, with the phone away, one tab open, and planned breaks for everything else, is not slower. It is the fastest route to learning that actually sticks, because undivided attention is the raw material memory is made from.
