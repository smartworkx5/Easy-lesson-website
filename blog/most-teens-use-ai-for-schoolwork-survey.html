Most Teens Now Use AI for Schoolwork: What the Survey Means for You

Teens use AI for schoolwork at a scale that would have seemed impossible just a few years ago. A recent survey reported in October 2026 found that 70% of teens ages 13-17 use AI for homework and assignments. That is not a small early-adopter group — it is most teenagers. AI has gone from a novelty to a normal part of school life in a very short time, and it is now built into many school-issued devices used widely in classrooms. The question is no longer whether students use AI. It is how they use it, and what that use does to their learning.

The same survey reported a detail that matters more than the headline number: 63% of teens use AI "to get answers," while only 35% use it to "fix or improve" an answer they already knew. In other words, most teens are using AI as an answer machine, and a minority are using it as a study partner. That gap between getting answers and improving answers is where the learning is won or lost. This article explains what the survey tells us, why the habit behind the numbers matters, and how to make sure you are on the right side of it.

What the Survey Actually Found

The survey covered teens ages 13-17 and asked how they use AI for schoolwork. The headline findings were clear. A reported 70% use AI for homework and assignments. A reported 63% use it "to get answers." Only a reported 35% use AI to "fix or improve" an answer they already knew. Those numbers describe habits, not abilities. They tell us what students do with AI, not what AI does to students. That distinction matters, because habits can be changed.

The survey also included a human story that brings the numbers to life. One interviewed student said that relying on AI had slowed his thinking and made tests without AI hard, and that his test scores dropped after he began relying on an AI tool for daily schoolwork. One student's experience is not proof about all students, but it matches a pattern seen in other research: when AI does the daily thinking, the ability to think without AI weakens. Daily schoolwork is practice, and practice you skip is skill you do not build.

Getting Answers Versus Improving Answers

The most important line in the survey is the one between "getting answers" and "fixing or improving" answers. Getting an answer means AI does the work and you submit it. Improving an answer means you do the work first and AI helps you make it better. The first habit feels productive — the homework gets done — but it leaves you with nothing for the exam. The second habit is slower, but it builds the understanding that exams actually test.

Think of the difference like this. Getting answers is like asking a friend to carry your school bag every day. Your arms never get stronger, and the day your friend is absent, the bag feels heavier than ever. Improving answers is like carrying the bag yourself while a coach shows you a better way to hold it. The work is still yours, but you get stronger and smarter about how you do it. The survey suggests most teens are currently choosing the first option. The good news is that switching to the second option is entirely in your hands.

Why the Habit Spreads So Fast

AI use spreads fast among teens for understandable reasons. It is quick, it is available at any hour, and it rarely says "try it yourself first." It also fits neatly into busy student life: homework piles up, deadlines crowd together, and a tool that finishes work in seconds is hard to resist. When AI features are built into school-issued devices, using them feels normal and even expected. None of this makes students lazy. It makes the easy path very easy, and the learning path requires a deliberate choice.

Social pressure plays a part too. When most of your classmates use AI for homework, not using it can feel like falling behind. But the survey's own numbers suggest the opposite: the students who use AI to get answers may look ahead on homework while falling behind on real learning. The students who use AI to improve their own answers are building the skills that last. In a year or two, the difference will show — in exams, in confidence, and in the ability to handle new problems without help.

How to Be in the 35%

Joining the minority that uses AI to improve answers is simple, though not always easy. Start with a try-first rule: attempt every problem yourself before opening AI. Write your own answer, even a rough one. Then ask AI to check it, explain where you went wrong, or suggest how to improve it. Compare the AI feedback with your textbook, because AI can make mistakes. Finally, redo the problem alone, without looking, to confirm you can do it yourself.

Another powerful habit is to ask AI for practice, not answers. After you finish a topic, ask AI to quiz you on it, or to give you a new problem on the same idea. Quizzing yourself is one of the best-studied ways to make learning stick, and AI makes it easy to generate practice questions on demand. Use that power for testing yourself, not for replacing yourself.

What Students, Parents, and Teachers Can Do

Students can start by being honest with themselves about which habit they have. If your first move on homework is to open AI, you are in the getting-answers group, and that is worth changing. Parents can help by asking "show me how you got this answer" rather than "did you finish your homework." That one question shifts the focus from completion to understanding. Teachers can help by showing students how to use AI as a tutor — demonstrating good prompts, checking AI answers together in class, and designing homework that rewards thinking, not just correct final answers.

Schools can also set clear expectations. When everyone knows that AI is welcome as a tutor but not as a substitute for your own work, students can use it without guessing where the line is. The survey numbers are a snapshot of a moment when AI arrived faster than the guidance for using it. Better guidance, not fear, is what turns those numbers into better learning.

Conclusion

The survey tells us that most teens now use AI for schoolwork, and that most use it to get answers rather than to improve their own work. That habit is common, understandable, and changeable. The students who come out ahead will not be the ones who avoid AI, and they will not be the ones who let AI do everything. They will be the ones who try first, use AI to improve their own answers, and check their understanding by working alone. Choose the improving habit, and AI becomes an advantage instead of a crutch.

FAQ

What percentage of teens use AI for schoolwork?

A recent survey reported that 70% of teens ages 13-17 use AI for homework and assignments. It also reported that 63% use AI "to get answers" and only 35% use it to "fix or improve" an answer they already knew. These are survey findings, not exact counts of every student.

Does using AI for homework mean a student is cheating?

Not necessarily. Using AI to explain a concept, check your working, or practise is legitimate study help. Submitting AI-written answers as your own, or using AI on work your teacher said to do alone, crosses the line. The habit matters more than the tool.

Why is "getting answers" a problem if the answers are correct?

Because correct answers you did not produce do not build your understanding. Exams test what you can do without AI, so the learning has to live in your head, not in the AI's output. Copying answers also creates overconfidence, since high homework marks can hide weak understanding.

How can I switch from getting answers to improving answers?

Try every problem yourself first and write down your own answer. Then ask AI to check, explain, or suggest improvements. Redo the problem alone afterwards. Over time, this routine turns AI into a tutor that strengthens your skills instead of a shortcut that replaces them.
