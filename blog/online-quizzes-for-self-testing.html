Online Quizzes for Self-Testing: Use Them Right

Online quizzes are everywhere, and they promise quick revision with instant scores. Used well, they are one of the strongest study tools available. Used badly, they become a pleasant way to waste time: you recognise answers, score well, and learn nothing.

The difference comes down to one rule. Attempt every quiz closed-book first, from memory, before you look at any notes. A quiz only measures learning when your memory does the work. This guide shows you how to use online quizzes so that every attempt makes you stronger.

Why Testing Beats Re-Reading

Re-reading a chapter feels productive because the material looks familiar. But familiarity is not memory. When you answer a quiz question from memory, you practise the exact skill a test demands: pulling knowledge out of your head without help. Each successful retrieval makes that knowledge easier to retrieve next time, and each failure shows you precisely what to restudy.

This is why a low quiz score is good news, not bad news. It tells you where the gaps are while there is still time to fix them. A high score earned with notes open tells you nothing at all.

The Closed-Book First Rule

Before starting any quiz, close your notes, close the textbook, and put the video away. Answer every question from memory, even if you must guess. Guessing is useful: it forces you to commit to an answer, and the correction afterwards sticks far better than a passively read fact.

If a quiz platform shows hints or lets you peek at answers, resist them on the first attempt. Hints convert a memory test into a recognition test, which is much weaker practice. Save hints for a second attempt, after you have seen where you stand.

Review Every Mistake, Not Just the Score

A score alone teaches nothing. After each quiz, go through every wrong answer and ask three questions. What did I answer, what is correct, and why is it correct? Write the correct reasoning in your own words, not just the correct option letter. If you cannot explain why an answer is right, you have found a concept to restudy, not just a question to memorise.

Also review questions you guessed correctly. A lucky guess feels like knowledge but will fail you later. Mark guessed answers honestly and treat them as gaps in your next study session.

Space Your Retries Instead of Repeating Instantly

Retaking a quiz immediately feels rewarding because your score jumps, but most of that jump is short-term familiarity with the questions, not lasting learning. Space your retries instead. Try the same quiz again after a day or two, and mix it with quizzes on other topics so you must recall each subject fresh.

Spacing feels harder, and that difficulty is the point. Effortful recall builds durable memory. If you can still answer correctly after a gap, you genuinely know the material.

Use Quiz Results to Plan Your Next Session

Every quiz produces a study plan if you read it properly. Group your mistakes by topic and look for patterns. If most errors cluster in one chapter, that chapter becomes your next study target. If errors scatter everywhere, your foundation needs rebuilding with worked examples and notes before more quizzing.

Keep a simple error log: the topic, the mistake, and the correct idea in one line each. Reviewing this log before the next quiz turns repeated mistakes into fixed ones, and it shows your progress over weeks in a way a single score never can.

Choose Quality Quizzes and Avoid Answer-Memorising

Not all quizzes are equal. Prefer quizzes that explain answers, cover your actual topic closely, and vary question wording, because varied wording tests understanding rather than recognition of one phrasing. Avoid quizzes that only reward memorising answer positions or that repeat identical questions endlessly.

Be careful with quizzes that include errors. If an explanation contradicts your textbook, trust the textbook and note the discrepancy. Your tests follow your textbook, so always verify solutions against it, and remember that any online tool, including quiz platforms, can contain mistakes.

FAQ

Should I ever take a quiz with notes open?

Open-note quizzes are fine for learning new material the first time, but they do not measure memory. Use them to study, then take a closed-book attempt to check what actually stuck.

How often should I retake the same quiz?

Wait at least a day between attempts, and mix in other topics. Two or three spaced attempts usually reveal whether knowledge is lasting or just freshly seen.

What should I do if I keep failing the same questions?

Stop quizzing that topic and return to learning it: reread the explanation, work through examples, and summarise the idea in your own words. Then quiz again after a gap.

Are short quizzes or long quizzes better?

Short, frequent quizzes beat rare long ones. A ten-question quiz you take often gives you regular feedback without exhausting your attention, and it fits easily into a daily routine.

Conclusion
