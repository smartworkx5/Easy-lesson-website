How to Check If Your Answer Makes Sense

Most lost marks do not come from not knowing the material. They come from small, silly mistakes: a decimal in the wrong place, an answer in the wrong units, a result that solves the wrong question. The fix is not more studying. It is a quick final pass over every answer before you move on.

Sanity checks take seconds and catch a surprising number of errors. This guide gives you four checks that work in every subject, plus a thirty-second routine that makes them automatic.

The Units Check

Every measured answer has units, and the units must match the question. If the question asks for a distance and your answer is in seconds, something went wrong. If a recipe-scale problem gives an answer in kilograms when you were scaling up grams for a cake, check your conversion.

Make units part of your working from the start, not an afterthought. Write the unit next to every intermediate value. When you multiply or divide, carry the units through the calculation and watch them combine: speed times time gives distance, and the units should show it. If the final unit looks impossible, the number is almost certainly wrong too. This one habit catches conversion errors, formula mix-ups, and decimal slips, because wrong units usually travel with wrong numbers.

The Size Check

Ask yourself whether the answer is roughly the right size. If you estimate the cost of supplies for a small project and get a figure that could buy a house, stop. If a probability comes out larger than one, stop. If a plant-growth experiment reports metres of growth in a week, stop.

The size check needs a rough expectation, so build the habit of guessing before you calculate. Before you solve, ask: should this be big or small? More than one or less than one? Positive or negative? A thirty-second guess gives your brain something to compare the answer against. Without an expectation, any number looks plausible, and that is exactly when silly answers slip through. Estimation is a skill in its own right, so treat the guess as part of the work, not a waste of time.

The Sign Check

Signs flip quietly and ruin loudly. A negative where a positive belongs can turn a correct method into a wrong answer. This matters in maths with directed numbers, in science with charges and directions, and in any problem where increase and decrease point opposite ways.

After every calculation, ask: does the sign fit the story? A temperature dropping below freezing should be negative in the right scale. A debt should be negative in an account balance. A force pulling backwards should point the opposite way from motion. If the story and the sign disagree, recheck the step where the sign entered. It is usually one line, often a subtraction done in the wrong order, and it is far easier to fix now than after the paper is marked.

The Did-I-Answer-the-Question Check

Read the question again after you finish, not before. It sounds strange, but this is the check that catches the most expensive mistakes. Students routinely solve for the wrong thing: they find the speed when the question asked for the time, they describe a cause when the question asked for an effect, they translate the first sentence when the prompt asked for the main idea.

Underline the actual question words as you read: how many, why, compare, explain, find the difference. Then compare your answer to those words. Does your answer give the number, the reason, the comparison, the explanation? If your answer is correct but answers a different question, it earns nothing. This check takes five seconds and saves whole questions.

A 30-Second Final Pass

Turn the four checks into a ritual. After finishing each answer, pause and run through them in order: units, size, sign, question. Touch each one with your pen or finger so it becomes physical. It takes about thirty seconds per question, which is cheap insurance against errors that cost real marks.

At first it will feel slow. After a week it becomes background habit, and you will start catching errors while you write, before the final pass even begins. That is the goal: not a slower exam, but a self-correcting one. These habits also pair well with A 5-Step Problem-Solving Method for Any Subject.

FAQ

How much time should sanity checks take?

About thirty seconds per question once the habit is set. That is far less than the time it costs to lose marks on an answer you actually knew how to do. Budget it into your exam plan from the start.

What if my answer fails a check but I cannot find the error?

Go back to the last line you were sure about and rebuild from there. Most errors hide in the most recent steps. If you are stuck in an exam, mark the question and return later with fresh eyes.

Do these checks work for essay subjects too?

Yes, especially the question check. Reread the prompt after drafting and confirm every part is addressed. The size and sign checks adapt naturally: does this claim fit the evidence, and does the tone match the question?

Should I check as I go or at the end?

Both. Quick checks as you go catch sign and unit slips early. The full thirty-second pass at the end catches wrong-question answers and size problems. Neither replaces the other.

Putting It Together

Four checks, thirty seconds, and most silly mistakes disappear. Units must match the question. Size must fit your rough expectation. Signs must fit the story. And the answer must answer the actual question asked. Run this pass on every answer until it becomes reflex.
