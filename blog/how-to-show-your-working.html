How to Show Your Working (and Earn Method Marks)

Two students get the same wrong final answer. One scores near zero; the other scores most of the available marks. The difference is the working. Examiners award method marks for correct reasoning shown on the page — the right approach, the right formula, the right steps — even when a slip somewhere makes the final answer wrong. Showing your working is not busywork or neatness for its own sake; it is how you insure your marks against mistakes. This guide teaches you the layout rules, labelling habits, and examiner psychology that turn visible method into real credit.

Why Examiners Reward Method

Understanding the examiner's position changes how you write. An examiner marking hundreds of scripts cannot read your mind; they can only credit what they see. When the final answer is correct but no working is shown, most mark schemes award only the answer mark — the method marks, often the majority, are lost because there is no evidence of method. When the final answer is wrong but the working shows a correct approach with one arithmetic slip, the examiner can award nearly everything except the final accuracy mark.

There is a second, selfish reason to show working: it makes your own errors findable. A jumbled mass of numbers hides mistakes; a clean step-by-step layout exposes them. Students who show their working check their work faster, spot slips during the exam instead of after, and learn more from marked homework because the teacher can point to the exact step where thinking went wrong. Clear working helps the examiner help you — and helps you help yourself.

Layout Rules That Work

Good layout has a simple principle: one logical step per line, reading top to bottom, with each line following from the one above. Start each question on a fresh area of the page and number it clearly. Write equations aligned at the equals sign where practical, so the chain of reasoning is visible at a glance. Leave space between questions — cramped work gets misread, and misread work loses marks.

Keep related work together. If a diagram belongs to a calculation, draw it beside the calculation, not three pages away. If you need scratch work, do it in a clearly marked margin area, not interleaved with the final steps — examiners should never have to guess which of two conflicting lines is your answer. And always box, underline, or clearly label your final answer. An examiner hunting for your answer in a thicket of numbers is an examiner in a bad mood; make the answer unmissable.

Labelling Steps and Showing Decisions

Working is not just calculation; it includes the decisions behind the calculation. A short label before a step — "using the area formula", "total cost =", "let x be the number of tickets" — tells the examiner what you are doing and why. These labels are cheap to write and valuable twice over: they earn method marks by naming the method, and they keep you oriented when you reread your own work during checking.

Define your symbols. If you introduce x, say what x stands for in words. If you use a formula, write it in general form first, then substitute the numbers — this two-stage habit earns the formula mark separately from the substitution and makes substitution errors visible. Show unit conversions as their own step rather than burying them inside arithmetic. And when you make an assumption or an approximation, state it briefly. Examiners reward transparency: a stated assumption they disagree with may still earn method marks, while a hidden one that goes wrong earns nothing.

Common Working Mistakes That Cost Marks

The most expensive mistake is the answer-only response: a final number with no supporting steps. Even when you can do it in your head, write the key steps down — mental maths earns no method marks and cannot be checked. Next is the stream-of-consciousness layout: numbers scattered across the page, arrows everywhere, two attempts side by side with no indication of which counts. If the examiner cannot follow it, they cannot credit it.

Other classics: skipping the formula and jumping straight to substituted numbers (loses the formula mark if the substitution has a slip); doing unit conversions silently inside a calculation (hides conversion errors from both you and the examiner); and the disappearing diagram — referring to "the triangle" without drawing it, forcing the examiner to reconstruct your thinking. Finally, the uncorrected error: crossing out so heavily that the work beneath is unreadable, or leaving two different answers with no indication of which is final. Cross out cleanly with a single line, and always indicate your final answer clearly.

Practising Clear Working

Clear working is a habit built in homework, not summoned in exams. For the next two weeks, treat every homework problem as working practice: full layout, labelled steps, defined symbols, boxed answers — even for easy questions. It will feel slow at first; speed comes as the layout becomes automatic. Ask your teacher specifically to mark your working, not just your answers, and to point out where the layout could be clearer.

A powerful exercise: swap solutions with a study partner and try to follow each other's working without any explanation. Where your partner gets lost, your working needs labels or clearer layout. Another: take one of your old messy solutions and rewrite it cleanly, then compare how much easier the clean version is to check. Students who do this consistently report an unexpected benefit — their thinking itself becomes more orderly, because the discipline of writing steps clearly forces the discipline of thinking in steps.

FAQ

Do I really need to show working if the answer is obviously right?

Yes. Mark schemes split credit between method and answer, and the method portion usually dominates. An answer with no working can only ever earn the answer mark. Beyond marks, unwritten steps cannot be checked — and "obviously right" answers are wrong more often than students expect. The thirty seconds of writing buys both marks and certainty.

How much working is enough?

Enough that a competent reader can follow your reasoning without guessing. As a rule: write the formula or method name, show the substitution, show the key intermediate steps, and state the final answer with units. You do not need to show trivial mental arithmetic like 2 + 3, but anything involving the problem's actual method should be visible. When in doubt, show more — extra clear steps never lose marks, while missing steps always risk them.

What should I do if I make a mistake halfway through?

Do not scribble it out into illegibility. Draw a single clean line through the wrong section and continue below or beside it — examiners ignore crossed-out work, but they need to see what remains clearly. If the mistake is early and affects everything after, it is often cleaner to start the question again in a fresh space, with a note that the new attempt is the final one. Never leave two competing answers without indicating which counts.

Does neat handwriting matter for method marks?

Legibility matters; calligraphy does not. The examiner must be able to read your numbers and symbols without guessing — a 4 that looks like a 9 can genuinely cost marks. But nobody expects beautiful writing under time pressure. Focus on clarity: distinct digits, clear decimal points, uncramped lines, and labelled answers. Tidy layout impresses more than pretty handwriting anyway.

Can good working actually raise my grade?

Regularly, by a meaningful margin. In many maths and science exams, method marks make up half or more of the available credit, so a student who shows full working on every question has a much higher floor than one who does not — wrong answers still score, while blank or answer-only responses score nothing. Teachers consistently report that the single fastest way for a capable student to gain marks is not learning more content but showing the method they already have.

The Working Is the Answer

From today, treat the working as the real product and the final answer as its conclusion. Write every step as if explaining to a smart friend who cannot see inside your head — because that is exactly who the examiner is. The habit protects your marks, sharpens your checking, and deepens your understanding all at once. In the end, showing your working is not about pleasing examiners. It is about thinking clearly enough that your thinking is visible.
