How to Prioritise Assignments When Everything Feels Urgent

Everyone has had the week where everything is due at once. Three assignments, a project, a test to prepare for, and every single one feels urgent. The usual response is to panic, jump between tasks, and finish nothing. There is a better way. Prioritising is not about ignoring work. It is about deciding, calmly and honestly, what to do first, what to do second, and what can wait. When everything feels urgent, that decision matters more than extra hours. This guide gives you a simple method: a due date times weight matrix that sorts your tasks in five minutes, plus the habits that keep you moving through the list without freezing.

The Due Date Times Weight Matrix

The core idea is simple: urgency alone is not enough. A small task due tomorrow and a big project due next week both matter, but for different reasons. You need two factors: when it is due, and how much it counts. Start by listing every task with its due date. Next to each one, note roughly how much it matters: a big project counts more than a short worksheet, and a major test counts more than a quick quiz. Then sort the list by both factors together. The task that is both soon and heavy goes first. The task that is soon but light goes second. The heavy task due later goes third, with an early start so it does not become a crisis. The light task due later goes last. This takes five minutes, and it turns a fog of worry into a clear order. When everything feels urgent, a clear order is the most calming thing in the world.

Estimate Honestly, Then Add Buffer

A priority list only works if your time estimates are honest. Most students underestimate how long tasks take, often by a lot. A task you think will take thirty minutes often takes an hour once you include reading, thinking, and checking. The fix is simple: estimate, then add buffer. If you think a task takes an hour, plan for an hour and a half. If a project needs three sessions, block four. This is not pessimism. It is realism. Buffer time absorbs the interruptions, the confusing step, and the paragraph that needs rewriting. It also protects your priorities. Without buffer, one slow task eats the time meant for the next one, and the whole list collapses. With buffer, a slow task is just a slow task, and the plan survives. Track your estimates for a week and you will quickly learn how much buffer you personally need.

One Task at a Time

Once you have an order, work it one task at a time. Jumping between assignments feels busy, but it is one of the least efficient ways to study. Every switch costs focus: you have to reload the task in your mind, remember where you were, and rebuild momentum. The result is three half-finished tasks and a rising sense of panic. Instead, take the top task, set a timer for one focused block, and work on nothing else until the block ends. If a worry about another task pops up, write it on a scrap of paper and return to the task in front of you. That note is enough to quiet the worry without switching tasks. When the block ends, check the list, take a short break, and start the next task. One task at a time, in priority order, is how overwhelming weeks get finished.

What to Do When Two Deadlines Collide

Sometimes two big deadlines really do land on the same day, and no matrix can change that. When that happens, act early. Start the bigger task days ahead, even if it feels strange to work on something that is not due yet. Early starts are the only real defence against clashes. If you still cannot finish both to the standard you want, make a deliberate trade-off: finish the heavier task well, and deliver a solid, honest effort on the lighter one, rather than doing both badly. Talk to a teacher early if a clash is genuinely impossible. Teachers are far more understanding about a clash they hear about on Monday than one they hear about on the due date. And use the experience: when you spot a future clash, write it in your planner the moment you see it, and start the bigger task the same week. Future clashes are often visible weeks ahead, if you look.

Keep the List Visible

A priority list only helps if you see it. Keep one list, in one place, where you will look every day. Paper on your desk, a note on your device, or a page in your planner all work. Update it every evening: cross off finished tasks, add new ones, and re-sort the order if deadlines changed. This daily five-minute check is what keeps the system honest. Without it, the list goes stale, and you are back to guessing. The list also helps you say no to distractions. When a new request arrives, you can look at the list and decide whether it fits today or belongs later in the week. That is not being rigid. It is being fair to the work you already promised yourself. A visible, updated list turns prioritising from a one-time panic response into a calm daily habit.

FAQ

What should I do first when everything feels urgent?

List every task with its due date and how much it counts, then start with the task that is both soon and heavy. A five-minute sort beats an hour of panicked jumping between tasks.

How do I estimate how long a task will take?

Guess, then add buffer. If you think it takes an hour, plan for an hour and a half. Track your estimates for a week to learn how much buffer you usually need.

Is multitasking ever a good idea for assignments?

Rarely. Switching between tasks costs focus and leaves everything half-finished. One task at a time, in priority order, is faster and calmer.

What if two big deadlines fall on the same day?

Start the bigger one days early. If you still cannot finish both well, finish the heavier one properly and give the lighter one a solid honest effort. Tell a teacher about genuine clashes as early as possible.

How often should I update my priority list?

Every evening, for about five minutes. Cross off finished tasks, add new ones, and re-sort if deadlines changed. A stale list is no better than no list.

Conclusion

When everything feels urgent, do not work harder in a panic. Sort your tasks by due date and weight, estimate honestly with buffer, work one task at a time, and keep the list visible with a daily check. Prioritising is a skill, and like any skill it gets faster and calmer with practice. Start with the list in front of you today.
