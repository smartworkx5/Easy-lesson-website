Understanding vs Memorising: Know Which One You Need

Students often study every topic the same way: read, re-read, highlight, hope. Then they are surprised when that method works brilliantly for one subject and fails completely for another. The reason is simple. Some learning tasks need understanding, and some need memorisation. They are different jobs, and they need different tools.

Understanding means you can explain an idea in your own words, apply it to a new situation, and notice when it does not fit. Memorising means you can recall a fact, term, date, or sequence quickly and accurately. Both matter. The mistake is using one when the task needs the other.

This article shows you how to tell which one a topic needs, how to study for each, and how to combine them when a topic needs both.

What Understanding Really Looks Like

You understand an idea when you can do three things with it. First, you can explain it without looking at the book, in words a younger student could follow. Second, you can use it on a fresh example you have never seen before. Third, you can spot when it does not apply, and say why.

Understanding grows through explaining, comparing, and practising with new examples. Reading alone rarely builds it, because reading feels like understanding. The page makes sense while you look at it, so your brain concludes that you know it. Then the test asks you to use the idea somewhere new, and the illusion breaks.

The fix is to test yourself with new material. Close the book. Explain the idea aloud. Try a problem that changes one detail. Ask yourself why each step works, not just what the step is. If you can answer why, you understand.

What Memorising Really Looks Like

Memorising means fast, accurate recall. You need it for vocabulary, definitions, formulas, dates, names, symbols, and sequences. No amount of deep understanding replaces it: you cannot reason your way to the spelling of a word or the date of an event.

Good memorisation is active and spaced. Instead of reading a list ten times, you test yourself on it: cover the answers, recall, check, and repeat. You spread those tests across days rather than cramming them into one evening. Short, repeated retrieval beats long, passive review every time.

Memory also loves hooks. A term is easier to recall when it is tied to an image, a rhyme, a story, or a comparison with something you already know. The hook does not need to be clever. It only needs to be yours, because a connection you built is easier to retrieve than one you only read.

How to Tell Which One a Topic Needs

Before you open a chapter, ask one question: what will I be asked to DO with this? If the answer is explain, apply, compare, solve, or analyse, you need understanding. If the answer is list, name, define, label, or recall, you need memorisation.

Many topics need both, in a particular order. You usually need to understand first, then memorise the details that understanding depends on. A student learning a process needs to understand how it works before memorising its steps. A student learning vocabulary needs to memorise the words, but understanding the root or the pattern behind them makes the memory stick.

A quick test helps. After studying, try two checks. Can you explain the idea to someone else without the book? Can you recall the key terms without peeking? The check that fails tells you where to put your next study session.

Study Methods That Build Understanding

Four methods build real understanding. The first is self-explanation: after each paragraph or example, pause and explain it in your own words, aloud or in writing. The second is worked-example study: read a solved example slowly, asking why each step is taken, then cover it and redo it from memory.

The third is comparison. When two ideas are similar, list what they share and what differs. Comparison forces your brain to build a sharp picture of each idea instead of a blurry blend. The fourth is teaching. Explaining a topic to a study partner, or even to an empty chair, exposes every gap in your understanding within minutes.

A useful habit is the why chain. After each claim in your notes, ask why three times. Why does this step come first? Why does this rule hold? Why would the answer change if this detail changed? Students who ask why remember far more than students who only ask what.

Study Methods That Build Memory

For memorisation, the star method is retrieval practice: test yourself, check, and test again. Flashcards work well here, as long as you genuinely try to recall before flipping the card. So does the blank-page method: write everything you remember about a topic on a blank sheet, then compare it with your notes and fill the gaps.

Spacing matters as much as testing. Review a new set of facts the next day, then two days later, then a week later. Each successful recall strengthens the memory more than the last. Cramming the night before produces a memory that is strong for a few hours and gone within days.

Interleaving also helps. Instead of memorising one whole list and then the next, mix the items. Mixing feels harder, and that is the point: it trains your brain to choose the right fact instead of just reciting a sequence.

When a Topic Needs Both

Most school topics need both understanding and memorisation, and the best study plan combines them in the right order. Start with understanding: read for meaning, explain in your own words, work through examples. Then lock in the facts: drill the terms, formulas, and sequences with retrieval practice.

Take a science topic with a process and a set of names. First understand the process: what happens, in what order, and why each stage matters. Then memorise the names attached to each stage. The understanding gives the names somewhere to live, so they stop being a random list.

The reverse order fails. Memorising names before understanding the process leaves you with a list you cannot use. Understanding without memorising the key facts leaves you unable to answer precise questions. Check both skills before you call a topic done.

FAQ

Can I just memorise everything and skip understanding?

Memorisation alone fails the moment a question changes the wording or asks you to apply an idea. You might pass a pure recall quiz, but any question that asks why, how, or what if will expose the gap. Memorise the facts, but understand the ideas they belong to.

Is understanding slower than memorising?

Understanding takes longer up front, but it saves time later. A student who understands a method can solve ten new problems. A student who only memorised one worked example has to start over on each new problem. The slow start pays for itself many times over.

How do I know if I understand something or only think I do?

Close the book and explain it aloud, then try a new example you have not seen. If you can do both without peeking, you understand it. If you can only recognise the idea when you see it on the page, you have familiarity, not understanding. Familiarity feels like knowledge, which is why the closed-book test matters.

What is the fastest way to memorise a long list?

Break the list into small chunks, learn one chunk by testing yourself, and review it on a spaced schedule: the next day, then after a few days, then a week later. Attach a hook to each item, an image, a rhyme, or a link to something you already know. Mix the order once you know the items, so you recall each one on its own merit.

Conclusion

Understanding and memorising are both essential, but they are not interchangeable. Ask what each topic will require you to do, study understanding with explanation and new examples, and build memory with testing and spacing. Match the method to the task and your study time will work twice as hard.
