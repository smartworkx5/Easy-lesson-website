Critical Reading Questions to Ask About Any Text You Read

Critical reading questions turn you from a passive reader into an active judge of what you read. Most reading asks: what does this text say? Critical reading adds: should I believe it, and why? It does not mean being negative or suspicious about everything. It means asking fair questions about evidence, purpose, and point of view before you accept a text's claims.

These questions matter because texts are written by people with purposes, and purposes shape what gets included, emphasised, or left out. A reader who asks good questions spots weak evidence, hidden assumptions, and one-sided arguments. That skill protects your thinking in study, in work, and in everyday life.

What Critical Reading Really Means

Critical reading means engaging with a text instead of simply absorbing it. You still read carefully and fairly, but you also evaluate. You check whether the evidence supports the conclusion. You notice what the writer assumes without proving. You consider what other views exist. The goal is not to dismiss the text, but to understand it deeply enough to judge it.

This is different from criticising. A critical reader can finish a text admiring it, as long as the admiration is earned by strong evidence and honest reasoning. Critical reading is a discipline of fairness: fair to the writer's argument, and fair to your own judgement.

Critical Reading Questions to Ask Before You Trust a Text

Keep this checklist beside you when you read anything important. Who wrote this, and what do they know about the topic? Why was it written, and who is it written for? What is the main claim, and what evidence supports it? What is left out, and whose view is missing? Is the reasoning sound, or does it jump from evidence to conclusion? You do not need to ask every question of every text. For light reading, two or three will do. For material you will rely on, work through the full set. The questions become faster with practice, until they feel like a natural part of reading.

Who Wrote This, and Why?

Start with the writer. What do they know, and how do they know it? A writer with direct experience or careful study deserves a closer hearing than one who repeats rumours. Look for signs of expertise, and for signs of its absence, such as vague claims or no sources.

Then ask about purpose. Was this written to inform, to persuade, to entertain, or to sell something? Purpose is not a flaw, but it shapes emphasis. A text written to persuade will stress the strongest evidence for its side and play down the rest. Knowing the purpose helps you read for what is missing, not just for what is present.

What Evidence Supports the Claim?

Separate claims from evidence. A claim is what the writer wants you to believe. Evidence is the reason you should believe it: facts, examples, data, or careful reasoning. A text can sound confident while offering almost no evidence, so ask: what backs this up?

Check the quality of the evidence. Is it specific or vague? Does it actually support the claim, or only something near it? Does the writer show the other side fairly? Strong texts give evidence you can check, admit limits, and handle objections honestly. Weak texts lean on slogans, personal stories presented as proof, or attacks on people instead of arguments.

Bias, Assumptions, and What Is Left Out

Every text has a point of view. Bias is not always deliberate, and a point of view is not automatically wrong. The critical reader's job is to notice it: which words carry a judgement, which examples were chosen, and which facts were left out? A text that only shows one side of a contested issue is asking you to trust its selection.

Watch for assumptions: claims the writer treats as obvious without proving them. Common assumptions include "everyone agrees", "this has always been true", and "the cause is obvious". When you spot an assumption, ask whether it holds. Often the weakest part of an argument is not its evidence but the unstated belief it rests on.

Putting It Together: A Critical Reading Routine

Use this routine on important texts. First, read for understanding: what is the main claim, and what evidence is offered? Next, ask the checklist questions and note your answers in the margin. Then, consider the strongest objection: what would a fair-minded critic say? Finally, decide what you accept, what you doubt, and what you need to check further.

Write your verdict in one or two sentences: "I accept the main claim because the evidence is specific and the objections are handled, but I am unsure about the second claim, which rests on one example." That short verdict is the product of critical reading, and it is far more useful than a highlighted page. FAQ

Is critical reading the same as being negative?

No. Critical reading means judging fairly, not finding fault. A critical reader can agree with a text, as long as the agreement comes from checking the evidence rather than from habit or first impressions.

How is critical reading different from normal reading?

Normal reading focuses on understanding what a text says. Critical reading adds evaluation: is the evidence strong, is the reasoning sound, and what is missing? Both matter, but evaluation is what turns information into judgement.

Can I read critically and still enjoy a text?

Yes. Asking questions often deepens enjoyment, because you notice craft, structure, and choices you would otherwise miss. For stories and entertainment, keep the questions light; for material you will rely on, ask them in full.

What should I do if I find a text unreliable?

Do not rely on its claims without independent support. Check the key facts elsewhere, look for a stronger source on the same topic, and note what was weak: missing evidence, one-sided selection, or faulty reasoning. Your notes protect you from re-trusting it later.

Conclusion

Critical reading is a habit of fair questions: who wrote this, why, what evidence supports it, and what is missing? Ask those questions regularly, and you will read with sharper judgement and trust what you read for better reasons.
