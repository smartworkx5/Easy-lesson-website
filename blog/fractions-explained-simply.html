Fractions explained simply start with one picture: a part of a whole. in recipes, in measurements, in test scores, and in everyday sharing. Yet many students find them confusing because a fraction looks like two numbers stacked on top of each other. Once you see a fraction as a simple picture — a part of a whole — the mystery disappears. This guide builds that picture step by step, with worked examples you can follow and check.

Fractions Explained: What a Fraction Really Means

A fraction has two parts. The bottom number, called the denominator, tells you how many equal parts the whole is split into. The top number, called the numerator, tells you how many of those parts you have. In the fraction 3/4, the whole is split into 4 equal parts and you have 3 of them.

Imagine a pizza cut into 4 equal slices. If you eat 3 slices, you have eaten 3/4 of the pizza. The denominator (4) counts the slices in the whole pizza. The numerator (3) counts the slices you ate. This part-whole model works for any fraction: 2/5 means 2 parts out of 5 equal parts, and 7/10 means 7 parts out of 10.

One useful habit is to always ask two questions when you meet a fraction: "How many equal parts?" (the denominator) and "How many parts do I have?" (the numerator). Those two questions turn every fraction into a picture you can draw.

Visualising Fractions with Everyday Examples

Drawing helps more than memorising. Draw a rectangle, split it into equal parts, and shade the parts your fraction names. For 3/8, draw a rectangle split into 8 equal strips and shade 3 of them. The drawing shows at a glance that 3/8 is less than half, because half of 8 strips would be 4.

Everyday examples make the same idea concrete. Half a glass of water is 1/2. A quarter of an hour is 15 minutes, because an hour has 60 minutes and 60 divided by 4 is 15. If a class has 20 students and 3/4 of them walk to school, that is 15 students, because 20 divided by 4 is 5 and 5 times 3 is 15.

Fractions also appear on number lines. Between 0 and 1, the point 1/2 sits exactly in the middle. The point 1/4 sits halfway between 0 and 1/2. Placing fractions on a number line helps you compare them, which is the next skill.

Worked Example: Finding a Fraction of a Number

To find a fraction of a number, divide by the denominator and then multiply by the numerator. Find 3/4 of 20. First divide 20 by 4, which gives 5. Then multiply 5 by 3, which gives 15. So 3/4 of 20 is 15.

Try another: find 2/5 of 30. Divide 30 by 5 to get 6, then multiply 6 by 2 to get 12. So 2/5 of 30 is 12. Notice the order matters only in the sense that both steps are needed: divide first, then multiply. Doing it the other way (multiply first, then divide) also works: 30 times 2 is 60, and 60 divided by 5 is 12. Choose whichever order keeps the numbers smaller and easier.

This method also checks whether an answer makes sense. Since 3/4 is less than 1, 3/4 of 20 must be less than 20. Since 3/4 is more than 1/2, the answer must be more than 10. The answer 15 fits both checks, which is a good sign.

Worked Example: Adding Fractions with the Same Denominator

When fractions share a denominator, adding them is easy: add the numerators and keep the denominator the same. For example, 3/8 + 1/8 = 4/8. The picture is clear: 3 shaded strips plus 1 shaded strip gives 4 shaded strips out of 8.

The result 4/8 can be simplified. Both 4 and 8 divide by 4, so 4/8 = 1/2. Simplifying means dividing the top and bottom by the same number until no common factor remains. For instance, 12/16 simplifies to 3/4 because both numbers divide by 4.

When denominators differ, first rewrite the fractions with a common denominator. For 1/2 + 1/4, rewrite 1/2 as 2/4, then add: 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4. A common denominator is simply a number both denominators divide into. For 2 and 4, the number 4 works. For 3 and 4, the number 12 works. This same idea powers comparing fractions, which comes next.

Worked Example: Comparing Fractions

Which is bigger, 2/3 or 3/4? Drawings help, but a common denominator settles it exactly. The number 12 works for both denominators. Rewrite 2/3 as 8/12 (multiply top and bottom by 4) and 3/4 as 9/12 (multiply top and bottom by 3). Since 9/12 is bigger than 8/12, 3/4 is bigger than 2/3.

A quick sense-check agrees: 2/3 is 1/3 away from a whole, while 3/4 is only 1/4 away from a whole. Since 1/4 is smaller than 1/3, 3/4 is closer to the whole and therefore bigger. Sense-checks like this catch careless errors.

Another comparison trick: compare each fraction with 1/2. The fraction 5/8 is bigger than 1/2 because 5 is more than half of 8. The fraction 3/7 is smaller than 1/2 because 3 is less than half of 7. When two fractions sit on opposite sides of 1/2, the comparison is instant.

Common Mistakes to Avoid with Fractions

The most common mistake is adding the denominators: 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5. Denominators count the size of the parts, and adding them would change what a part means. The correct answer uses a common denominator: 1/2 = 3/6 and 1/3 = 2/6, so the sum is 5/6.

Another mistake is treating the numerator and denominator as separate whole numbers. The fraction 3/4 is a single number between 0 and 1, not "3 and 4". Comparing 3/4 with 2/3 by comparing 3 with 2 and 4 with 3 gives nonsense. Always compare fractions as whole values, using a common denominator or a drawing.

A third mistake is forgetting to simplify. An answer of 4/8 is correct but unfinished; most teachers expect 1/2. After every calculation, ask whether the top and bottom share a factor. If they do, divide both by it and keep going until they share only 1.

Try It Yourself: Practice with Answers

Try these three problems, then check your answers below.

  1. What is 2/5 of 30?
  2. Work out 3/8 + 1/8 and simplify your answer.
  3. Which is bigger: 5/6 or 7/8? Show your working with a common denominator.

Answers: (1) Divide 30 by 5 to get 6, then multiply by 2 to get 12. (2) 3/8 + 1/8 = 4/8 = 1/2. (3) Using 24 as a common denominator, 5/6 = 20/24 and 7/8 = 21/24, so 7/8 is bigger. If you got all three, you have the core skills. If one felt tricky, redraw it as a shaded rectangle and try again — the picture usually clears up the arithmetic.

Conclusion

A fraction is simply a part of a whole: the denominator counts the equal parts, and the numerator counts how many you have. Divide by the denominator and multiply by the numerator to find a fraction of a number, use a common denominator to add or compare fractions, and always simplify at the end. Draw the picture whenever you feel unsure — a shaded rectangle or a number line turns an abstract symbol into something you can see. With these habits, fractions stop being a source of confusion and become a reliable tool for everyday maths.

FAQ

What is a fraction in simple words?

A fraction is a part of a whole. The bottom number (denominator) says how many equal parts the whole is split into, and the top number (numerator) says how many of those parts you have. For example, 3/4 means 3 parts out of 4 equal parts.

How do you find a fraction of a number?

Divide the number by the denominator, then multiply the result by the numerator. For 3/4 of 20, divide 20 by 4 to get 5, then multiply 5 by 3 to get 15. This works for any fraction of any number.

Why can't you just add denominators when adding fractions?

The denominator tells you the size of the parts. Adding denominators would change the part size mid-calculation, which breaks the picture. Instead, rewrite the fractions with a common denominator, add the numerators, and keep the denominator the same.

How do you simplify a fraction?

Divide the numerator and denominator by the same number until they share no common factor except 1. For example, 12/16 simplifies to 3/4 by dividing both by 4. A simplified fraction has the same value in its neatest form.

What is the difference between a proper and an improper fraction?

A proper fraction, like 3/4, is smaller than 1 because the numerator is smaller than the denominator. An improper fraction, like 7/4, is 1 or larger because the numerator is equal to or bigger than the denominator. Improper fractions can be rewritten as mixed numbers, so 7/4 = 1 and 3/4.
