AI Courses Are Coming to Schools: What Students Will Learn

By Easy Lesson Team · Published October 17, 2026 · 7 min read

For most students, AI has arrived informally: a chatbot here, a study tool there, a teacher's warning about plagiarism somewhere in between. That patchwork is about to get more structured. In October 2026, a reported announcement described a compulsory school course on artificial intelligence and information security, planned to begin with the next academic year's cycle, with pilot testing planned earlier in the same academic year. Teacher training for the new course is already under way, and the announcement reported that a reported 250,000 teachers will be trained.

This article previews what students are likely to learn in courses like this one, why information security sits alongside AI, and how you can get ready before the course reaches your classroom.

What an AI Course for Schools Covers

The announced course has two halves: exploring what AI can do, plus the risks of using it. That pairing is the right one. A course that only celebrates AI would leave students unprepared for its pitfalls. A course that only warns about AI would leave them unable to use tools that are becoming part of everyday study and work. The balance between capability and risk is the whole point.

On the capability side, expect to learn what AI systems can and cannot do. You will likely meet the main kinds of AI tools: chatbots that generate text, tools that create images or audio, and study tools that summarise, quiz, and explain. You will learn how these tools produce their outputs, in plain terms, and why they sometimes produce confident-sounding errors. You will also practise using them for genuine tasks: brainstorming, drafting, checking, and revising, with the emphasis on staying in control of your own work.

On the risk side, expect to learn about misinformation, bias, privacy, and over-reliance. AI tools can produce plausible falsehoods. They can reflect biases in their training data. They can collect and store what you type. And they can make you dependent, so that your own skills fade while your output looks polished. A good AI course teaches you to spot each of these risks and to respond sensibly.

Why Information Security Is Part of the Course

Information security may sound like a separate subject, but it belongs in an AI course because AI tools run on information. Every prompt you type, every file you upload, and every photo you share with an AI tool becomes data that someone else's system processes. Understanding that flow is essential to using AI safely.

Expect the security half of the course to cover practical habits. Use strong, unique passwords and keep them private. Be careful about what personal information you share with any AI tool. Check whether a tool is approved by your school before using it for schoolwork. Be sceptical of messages, links, or files that arrive unexpectedly, even if they look official. And learn how to check whether a claim you read online is supported by a trustworthy source.

These habits are not just for school. They are life skills for a world where AI tools handle more of our communication, homework, and work. A student who learns them early will make fewer costly mistakes later, and will be the person others trust with sensitive tasks.

What the Teacher Training Number Means

The announcement reported that a reported 250,000 teachers will be trained for the new course. Treat that figure as reported, not as a confirmed outcome, because it comes from the announcement itself rather than an independent count. Still, the scale of the claim carries a real message: teaching AI well requires preparing teachers first, not just handing students a new subject.

That matters for students too. A course is only as good as its teaching. If your teacher has been trained in AI concepts and classroom methods, you can expect activities, discussions, and projects rather than a dry list of definitions. If your teacher is learning alongside you, be patient and curious together. Ask questions, share what you discover, and treat the course as a shared exploration. The first year of any new course is an experiment, and students who engage actively make it better for everyone.

The pilot phase matters for the same reason. Pilot testing planned earlier in the same academic year gives schools a chance to try the material, find what works, and fix what does not before the full rollout. If your school is part of a pilot, your feedback may shape the final course. Take that opportunity seriously.

How to Get Ready Before the Course Starts

You can prepare without waiting for the course to begin. Start by using one AI study tool deliberately and critically. Ask it to explain a topic, then verify its explanation against your textbook. Ask it to quiz you, then check whether its questions match what your course actually covers. Notice where it helps and where it misleads. That experience will make the course's lessons concrete instead of abstract.

Second, practise the security habits now. Review your passwords, check your privacy settings, and think about what you share with AI tools. If you use a photo-based study solver, remember that it needs the user's own free Google AI Studio API key, and keep your key private and never share it. Always verify AI solutions against your textbook, because AI can make mistakes.

Third, build the habit of asking what AI can and cannot do. When you see an impressive AI output, ask: what data did it use, what might it have missed, and how would I check it? That single habit is the foundation of AI literacy, and it will serve you in every subject, not just in an AI course.

FAQ

Will an AI course teach me to build AI systems?

Probably not at first. School AI courses typically focus on understanding, using, and judging AI tools, plus staying safe. Building AI systems comes later, for students who choose to specialise. The first goal is literacy, not engineering.

Why do schools need a separate AI course?

Because informal exposure is uneven. Some students learn good AI habits on their own, while others pick up risky ones. A dedicated course gives every student the same foundation in capabilities, risks, and safety.

What is the hardest part of learning about AI?

Judging AI output. AI tools sound confident even when they are wrong, so the key skill is verification: checking claims against your textbook, a trusted source, or your own reasoning before you accept them.

Do I need technical skills to do well in an AI course?

No. Curiosity, clear thinking, and careful reading matter far more than technical background. The course is about using AI wisely and safely, and those are thinking skills, not coding skills.

How can I practise information security at home?

Start with the basics: strong unique passwords, careful sharing of personal information, scepticism toward unexpected messages and links, and checking claims against trustworthy sources. Small habits, repeated daily, build real security.

Dedicated AI courses are a sign that schools are treating AI literacy as a core skill, alongside reading, writing, and arithmetic. The courses will cover what AI can do, the risks of using it, and the security habits that keep you safe. You can get a head start by using AI tools critically, verifying everything against your textbook, and building sound security habits now. When the course reaches your classroom, you will already be thinking like the student it is designed to create.
