Active vs Passive Voice: When to Use Each

Active voice and passive voice are two ways to describe the same action. In active voice, the subject performs the action. In passive voice, the subject receives the action. Both forms are correct. The skill is knowing when each one serves your writing better.

This guide explains the difference with transformation examples, shows when to choose each form, and helps you avoid the most common mistakes.

What Is Active Voice?

In active voice, the subject does the action. "The student wrote the summary" is active. The student is the subject, wrote is the verb, and the summary is the object. The sentence is direct and easy to follow. The reader knows immediately who did what.

Active voice is usually clearer and stronger. It uses fewer words and gives writing energy. "The teacher explained the lesson" is shorter and more direct than the passive version. For most school writing, active voice should be your default choice. It keeps sentences lively and makes your ideas easy to understand.

Active voice also helps you take responsibility for your claims. "I found three patterns in the data" is clearer than "Three patterns were found in the data." The active version tells the reader who did the work. When you describe your own actions, observations, or decisions, active voice is usually the honest and clear choice.

What Is Passive Voice?

In passive voice, the subject receives the action. "The summary was written by the student" is passive. The summary is the subject, but it is not doing the action. The doer, the student, appears at the end in a by phrase or is left out entirely. The verb form uses a form of be plus the past participle: was written, is explained, has been completed.

Passive voice is useful when the action matters more than the doer. "The library is cleaned every evening" focuses on the library, not on who cleans it. In scientific and formal writing, passive voice is common when the process matters more than the person. "The solution was heated and then cooled" describes a process without naming the person who performed it.

Passive voice is also useful when the doer is unknown or unimportant. "The window was broken during the storm" does not need to name a doer. In such cases, passive voice is not weak. It is the most natural way to express the idea.

How to Transform Active to Passive

To change an active sentence to passive, follow three steps. First, move the object of the active sentence to the subject position. Second, change the verb to a form of be plus the past participle, keeping the same tense. Third, move the active subject to a by phrase at the end, or leave it out if it is not needed.

Here are transformation examples. Active: "The class completed the project." Passive: "The project was completed by the class." Active: "She is reading the chapter." Passive: "The chapter is being read by her." Active: "They have finished the notes." Passive: "The notes have been finished by them." Notice how the tense stays the same. Present becomes present passive, past becomes past passive, and present perfect becomes present perfect passive.

To change passive back to active, reverse the steps. Find the doer in the by phrase, make it the subject, and change the verb to the matching active form. "The notes were reviewed by the group" becomes "The group reviewed the notes." Practising both directions builds a flexible sense of sentence structure.

When to Use Each Form

Use active voice when you want clarity, energy, and responsibility. Use it for stories, explanations, instructions, and most everyday writing. "Follow these steps" is clearer than "These steps should be followed." Active voice also works well when the reader needs to know who is responsible for an action.

Use passive voice when the receiver of the action is more important than the doer, when the doer is unknown, or when you want a formal tone for a process. "The results were checked twice" focuses on the results. "Mistakes were made" hides the doer, which is why passive voice is sometimes used to avoid responsibility. Avoid that habit in your own writing. If you made a mistake, active voice is more honest: "I made a mistake."

A good rule is to write active by default and switch to passive for a reason.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

One common mistake is using passive voice in every sentence, which makes writing slow and dull. Another is mixing forms in a way that confuses the reader. "The essay was written and I edited it" mixes passive and active without a reason. A smoother version is "I wrote the essay and edited it." Keep the voice consistent unless you have a reason to change.

Another mistake is leaving out the doer when the reader needs to know it. "The homework was marked" leaves the reader wondering who marked it. If the doer matters, include it: "The homework was marked by the teacher." Or use active voice: "The teacher marked the homework."

Watch for wordy passive sentences with unnecessary by phrases. "The book was read by me" is longer and weaker than "I read the book." Remove passive constructions that add words without adding meaning. When you revise, circle every form of be plus a past participle and ask whether an active version would be clearer. When you work from a textbook, verify your transformations against the textbook. AI tools can suggest rewrites, but they can make mistakes, so check the tense and meaning yourself.

FAQ

How do I recognise passive voice?

Look for a form of be plus a past participle, often with a by phrase. "The report was written by the team" is passive. If the subject receives the action instead of performing it, the sentence is passive.

Is passive voice wrong?

No. Passive voice is grammatically correct and useful in many situations. It becomes a problem only when it is overused, hides responsibility, or makes writing unclear.

Can every active sentence become passive?

Most active sentences with an object can become passive. Sentences without an object, such as "She slept," cannot become passive because there is nothing to move to the subject position.

Should I use active voice in essays?

In most essays, active voice is clearer and stronger. Use passive voice selectively when the process or result matters more than the person who performed the action.

Conclusion

Active voice shows who does the action. Passive voice shows who receives it. Both have a place in good writing. Choose active voice for clarity and energy, and choose passive voice when the action, process, or result deserves the focus. Practise transforming sentences in both directions until the choice feels natural.
