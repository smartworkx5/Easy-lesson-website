Working Backwards: A Problem-Solving Trick That Works

Most problems are presented front to back: here is the start, find the end. But some problems are much easier when you flip them around — start from the answer, or from the end state, and reverse the steps back to the beginning. This technique, called working backwards, is one of the most reliable tools in a problem solver's kit. It shines in puzzles, multi-step arithmetic, algebra, and any situation where the forward path is tangled but the reverse path is clear. This guide shows you when to use it, how to reverse steps correctly, and the traps to avoid.

When Working Backwards Helps

Reach for this technique when the starting point is messy but the ending is clean. Classic signals: the problem describes a sequence of operations and asks what you started with; the final state is simple and fully known; or the forward path branches into many possibilities while the reverse path is forced. A familiar example: a number is doubled, then 7 is added, then the result is tripled to give 93 — what was the original number? Going forward means guessing; going backward means undoing: divide 93 by 3 to get 31, subtract 7 to get 24, halve to get 12. Each reverse step is obvious.

The technique also helps when the forward direction has too many unknowns. In route and scheduling puzzles, starting from the destination and asking "what must have come just before this?" often collapses possibilities that multiply out of control going forward. A good instinct to develop: if you have been pushing forward for a while and getting nowhere, stop and ask what the end looks like. Problems that resist forward attack frequently surrender to reverse attack.

How to Reverse Steps Correctly

Reversing is mechanical if you are careful: list the forward steps in order, then undo them in reverse order, with each operation replaced by its inverse. Addition reverses to subtraction, multiplication to division, squaring to square-rooting. The two places students go wrong are reversing the order incorrectly and forgetting to invert an operation. Write the forward chain explicitly — step 1, step 2, step 3 — then build the reverse chain underneath it: reverse step 3, reverse step 2, reverse step 1. Seeing both chains side by side makes order errors almost impossible.

Take the earlier example written out. Forward: start with x; double it (2x); add 7 (2x + 7); triple it (3(2x + 7) = 93). Reverse chain: undo the tripling first — divide by 3; undo the adding — subtract 7; undo the doubling — divide by 2. The discipline is to always undo the last forward step first, like unwrapping layers. Students who try to undo steps in forward order get tangled; students who respect the reverse order find the technique almost automatic.

Worked Example: The Undo Chain

Let us work a slightly richer example. A shopkeeper sells half her stock of notebooks, then sells 15 more, then receives a delivery of 40, and ends the day with 75 notebooks. How many did she start with? The forward story has unknowns at every stage, so reverse it. End state: 75. Undo the delivery: before it arrived she had 75 minus 40, which is 35. Undo the sale of 15: before that sale she had 35 plus 15, which is 50. Undo selling half her stock: before that sale she had twice 50, which is 100. She started with 100 notebooks.

Now verify forward to build trust in the method: start with 100, sell half (50 left), sell 15 more (35 left), receive 40 (75). It matches. Notice how each reverse step was a simple, confident operation while the forward problem felt tangled. That contrast is the signature of a problem suited to working backwards. When you spot it, the technique does the heavy lifting and all that remains is careful arithmetic.

Working Backwards in Algebra and Proofs

The technique scales up. In algebra, solving an equation is working backwards through the operations applied to the unknown — you just do it formally. Recognising this connection helps: every equation you solve is an undo chain, and the "reverse order, inverse operation" discipline is exactly what keeps equation-solving correct. When students see solving as reversing, the rules stop feeling arbitrary.

In geometry and proof-style problems, working backwards means assuming the desired conclusion and asking what would imply it. To prove two triangles congruent, ask: which congruence condition could I use, and what would I need to show for it? That question turns a vague "prove this" into a concrete shopping list of facts to establish. This backward planning does not replace the forward proof — the final write-up still runs forward — but it discovers the path. Many elegant solutions were found backwards and written forwards.

Traps and When Not to Use It

Working backwards is a tool, not a religion. It fails or misleads in a few situations. First, when operations are not reversible: if a forward step loses information — like squaring (which hides the sign) or rounding — the reverse step is ambiguous, and you must track both possibilities. Second, when the end state is not fully known: the technique needs a solid starting point for the reverse journey. Third, when the forward path is already clear and simple — reversing adds nothing and wastes time.

The subtlest trap is reversing correctly but forgetting what the reversed quantities mean. In the shopkeeper example, after undoing the delivery you have "notebooks before delivery", a meaningful intermediate state. Always label what each reversed value represents; naked numbers invite misinterpretation. And as always, verify by running forward from your answer to the given end state. The forward check is quick and it is the technique's built-in proof.

FAQ

How do I know a problem is suited to working backwards?

Look for the signature: a sequence of operations leading to a known end state, with the starting value unknown. Word problems with phrases like "ends up with", "finally", or "in the end" are strong candidates. If the forward direction feels like guessing but each reverse step looks obvious, you have found one. With practice, the pattern becomes recognisable within seconds of reading.

What are inverse operations, exactly?

An inverse operation undoes another: subtraction undoes addition, division undoes multiplication, taking a square root undoes squaring. When working backwards, every forward operation must be replaced by its inverse — this is the most common source of errors, especially forgetting that the last operation done is the first one undone. Writing both chains explicitly keeps this straight.

Can working backwards help with essay or language tasks?

In planning, yes. Start from the conclusion or answer you want to reach and ask what evidence and reasoning would lead a reader there — then organise your writing around that chain. It is also useful for checking: assume your draft's conclusion and trace backwards through your paragraphs to see if each step actually supports it. Gaps in the backward trace reveal weak arguments.

What if a step cannot be reversed uniquely?

Then carry all possibilities forward. Squaring hides whether the original was positive or negative, so a reversed square root gives two candidates — test both against the problem's conditions. Losing information in a forward step does not kill the technique; it just means the reverse path branches. Track the branches carefully and let the problem's constraints prune them.

Is working backwards the same as guessing and checking?

No, though they can combine. Guessing and checking tries forward candidates and tests them; working backwards derives the start from the end by reversing operations. Working backwards is more systematic and usually faster when it applies. But if reversing stalls halfway, the partial reverse chain still narrows the possibilities — and a narrowed guess-and-check is far better than a blind one.

Adding the Trick to Your Toolkit

This week, tag every homework problem that describes a sequence ending in a known result, and solve each one backwards before trying any other method. Write both chains — forward and reverse — until the order discipline is automatic. You will quickly develop an eye for the technique's signature, and problems that used to invite guessing will start inviting reversing. The best problem solvers are not the ones who push hardest in one direction; they are the ones who know when to turn around.
