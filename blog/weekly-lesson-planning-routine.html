A Weekly Lesson Planning Routine That Saves Sundays

Many teachers lose their Sundays to planning. The weekend starts with good intentions, then the pile of lessons for the week ahead grows, and suddenly Sunday evening is gone. The problem is rarely laziness. It is the lack of a routine. When planning happens in one giant session, it feels overwhelming, and it always takes longer than expected. A weekly lesson planning routine fixes this by spreading the work across the week in small, predictable steps. You plan a little each day, batch similar tasks together, and keep Sundays free. This guide shows you how to build that routine.

Why One Big Planning Session Fails

A single weekly planning session fails for three reasons. First, it is too long. After an hour of planning, focus fades, and the later lessons get weaker plans than the earlier ones. Second, it is easy to postpone. A task that takes three hours feels heavy, so it gets pushed to Sunday evening, when energy is lowest. Third, it ignores the information you gather during the week. If Tuesday's lesson reveals that students missed a key idea, a plan written on Sunday cannot adapt to it. A routine that plans in small steps across the week solves all three problems. Each step is short enough to finish, regular enough to become a habit, and timed so that what you learn in class feeds into the next day's plan. The goal is not to plan more. It is to plan in smaller pieces, at better times.

The Three Touchpoints: Friday, Daily, and Sunday

A weekly routine needs only three touchpoints. The first is a short Friday review, ten to fifteen minutes at the end of the teaching week. Look at the week ahead, list the topics for each lesson, and note anything that must be prepared in advance, such as materials or copies. The second is a short daily planning slot, twenty to thirty minutes, where you plan the next day's lessons using a simple method like the one in how to plan a lesson in 30 minutes. The third is a calm Sunday check, ten minutes at most, where you glance over the week's plans, confirm nothing is missing, and then stop. Notice what Sunday has become: a ten-minute check, not a three-hour marathon. The heavy work lives in the daily slots, where each lesson is planned close to when it will be taught, and the Friday review makes sure nothing surprises you.

Batch Similar Tasks Together

Batching means doing similar tasks in one sitting instead of switching between different kinds of work. Planning is faster when you batch it. For example, write all the week's openers in one sitting, then all the practice tasks, then all the exit tickets. Switching between openers, tasks, and checks for each lesson separately forces your brain to change gear every few minutes, which is slow and tiring. Batching also reveals patterns: you may notice that three lessons all need the same type of practice, or that two lessons can share an activity format. A practical way to batch is to keep a simple weekly grid: one row per lesson, one column each for goal, opener, main activity, and check. Fill it column by column, not row by row. The grid gives you the whole week at a glance, and it keeps your plans short enough to be usable. If you use a template, keep one template for every lesson so batching stays smooth.

Reuse and Recycle Without Guilt

Good teachers reuse. A strong opener, a well-designed task, or a clear explanation does not expire after one use. Build a personal bank of reusable materials: your best openers, your favourite activity formats, your most reliable checks, and your template for each lesson type. When you plan a new lesson, start from the bank instead of from a blank page. Reuse also applies across years: a lesson that worked well last year is a starting point this year, not something to rebuild from scratch. The key is to adapt, not just repeat. Update the examples, adjust for this year's students, and fix whatever did not work last time. One honest line of reflection after each lesson, such as "the pair task ran long; shorten to eight minutes," makes the reused version better than the original. Reuse is not laziness. It is how experienced teachers protect their time for the work that genuinely needs fresh thinking.

Plan for the Week You Actually Have

A routine only works if it respects the real week: meetings, duties, marking, and the occasional disruption. When you plan, look at the real calendar, not an ideal one. If Tuesday is heavy with meetings, plan Tuesday's lessons on Monday. If Friday is light, use Friday's review slot to get ahead on the following Monday. Build a small buffer into the week: one lesson with a simpler plan, or a revision slot that can absorb a slip. A routine with no slack breaks at the first disruption. A routine with slack bends and recovers. Also be honest about energy. Plan the hardest lessons when you are freshest, and keep the lightest planning tasks for the end of the day. A routine that fights your energy will be abandoned. A routine that works with it will last. The best weekly routine is the one you can follow in a bad week, not just in a perfect one.

Review the Week in Ten Minutes

End the week with a short review, and the routine improves itself. On Friday, or whenever your week ends, spend ten minutes answering three questions. What worked this week? What did students struggle with? What will I change next week? Write the answers in a few lines. The first question tells you what to reuse. The second tells you what to reteach or revisit. The third turns the review into action instead of just reflection. This review also closes the loop between planning and teaching: your plans get better because they are informed by what actually happened. Over a term, these ten-minute reviews build a detailed picture of your classes that no single planning session could match. Keep the review short and regular. A ten-minute review done every week beats an hour-long review done once a term.

Conclusion

Advertisement

A weekly lesson planning routine that saves Sundays is built on three touchpoints, batching, reuse, and honest planning for the real week. Review on Friday, plan daily in short slots, check briefly on Sunday, batch similar tasks, reuse what works, and end the week with a ten-minute review. Your Sundays are yours again, and your lessons will be better planned than ever.

FAQ

How much time should weekly planning take?

Aim for twenty to thirty minutes a day plus a short Friday review and a ten-minute Sunday check. The total is similar to one long session, but spread out it feels lighter and produces better plans.

What if I have no planning time during the school day?

Protect one short slot at home, the same time each day, and keep it to thirty minutes. The routine matters more than the location. A short daily slot at home still beats a lost Sunday.

Should I plan the whole week on Friday?

No. Use Friday to list topics and flag what needs preparing. Plan each lesson close to when you will teach it, so you can adapt to what students learned in the previous lesson.

What if a disruption breaks my routine?

Use the buffer you built in, and restart the routine the next day. One broken week is not a failed routine. The routine works because it is regular, not because it is perfect.
